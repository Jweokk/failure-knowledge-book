# Chapter 39: The Ninety-Minute Death Rehearsal

> What this chapter covers: the script for a meeting that rehearses failure before the event — who chairs it, how to open, what to ask, how to record, how to close, who follows up afterward; with a one-page agenda attached.
> When to return: when a project is approaching an irreversibility point, or the team is holding a kickoff or a go-live review — run this meeting inside yours.
> Reading time: 35 minutes.

## 1 Positioning: Before the Event, Not After

The premortem and the postmortem are a pair: the postmortem comes after the outcome, the premortem before the irreversibility point. Its psychological mechanism comes from the premortem method proposed by Gary Klein: everyone assumes the project has already failed, then writes down the causes of the failure. Once the death is assumed, criticism stops being pessimism and becomes an autopsy. (Klein, "Performing a Project Premortem," Harvard Business Review, September 2007; reprinted in IEEE Engineering Management Review, 2008; Crossref record verified.)

The ninety-minute length comes from one constraint: it must fit inside a single ordinary meeting, with no additional resources. A premortem that runs past ninety minutes will never be held a second time, which makes it the same as having none. The meeting's entire output is one page: a list of three to five modes of death, one defensive action per mode, and a verifier and a date for every action. It is isomorphic with the conclusion page of Chapter 36; the premortem is that page executed as a meeting.

Which decisions deserve a rehearsal: three tests, all three required, or the meeting devalues itself.

Test one: a real point of irreversibility exists (Chapter 7's table says so), and the cost of retreat exceeds the cost of the meeting. Reversible decisions run Chapter 36's solo routine; you do not stage a full rehearsal to hang a picture.

Test two: at least three participants hold non-overlapping information. A room of identical knowledge writes identical deaths — ten duplicate slips teach nothing one slip would not. Overlap is judged in advance by the organizer: three different seats, three different histories, or one outside view at minimum.

Test three: the political temperature allows bad news to exist. Where the project already carries senior sponsorship and a public commitment (Chapter 13's sealed narrative), every death written will be auto-translated into "yes, we know, but it is decided" — fix the temperature first, or run the anonymous written variant (Section 7), or do not meet. Forcing the meeting on a cold room produces a warm-up act for Form One, the credit rehearsal.

The common meaning of the three tests: the rehearsal is a scarce resource whose output quality is set before anyone sits down. Most organizations' failure is too many rehearsals, not too few — rehearse everything and you have rehearsed nothing. Two to four a year, each meeting all three tests, beats one a month at half conditions.

Timing has one more rule: open the window two to six weeks before the irreversible point. Defense actions need an execution runway; a rehearsal the day before the switch claims actions nobody can perform and degrades into a liability disclaimer ("we did warn").

The material pack, item by item. Item one (the assessment table's conclusion page) may be an organizational standing result rather than a fresh fill, dated as such — anything over a year old gets refilled. Item two (the ledger's last three same-class entries) is selected by mechanism, not by project: a first-of-its-kind launch still has siblings in mechanism. Item three (the dependency diagram) is where packs usually fail: where no diagram exists, substitute in order — an auto-generated dependency list, the architecture review's annex, finally a hand sketch — and where none of the three exist, the invisibility of dependencies is itself the rehearsal's first documented death. One distribution rule for the pack: conclusions only, no raw dumps (nobody reads thirty pages of logs; send links for the willing), conclusion pages capped at three.

The room. A wall to stick things on — whiteboard, glass, or paper — is mandatory: death slips must hang where all can see; slips flat on a table have no public existence, and the clustering phase cannot run. Remote, a shared whiteboard obeys the same rules with one addition: deletions leave strikethroughs, never clean removals — the record's integrity is worth the mess. Materials: three colors of slips (technical, organizational, incentive — declared before writing; forced color choice is the first attribution exercise), dot stickers for voting (three per person), a timer at the chair's hand. The clock check happens the day before: a chair who leaves the room mid-session hunting for slips returns to a different meeting. Seating: no head seat; the chair sits aside; the project owner sits farthest from the wall — every read-aloud and every stuck slip crosses their sightline, a physical reminder that the meeting is about the project and in front of its owner, and owes neither flattery nor mercy. Remote adds three: cameras on (the dark verdict reads faces), write-access to the board for all (the silent phase is self-service), and stricter timing (online rooms leak time; the chair calls every phase change aloud).

## 2 Preparation (Organizer, About 60 Minutes, Not Counted Toward the Ninety)

The materials pack has three items: the conclusion page of the positioning table from Chapter 35 (with two or three candidate modes of death already screened; if it has not been done, it must be done before the meeting — a premortem cannot start from zero), the three most recent same-class entries in the anomaly ledger, and the current version of the dependency graph or dependency list. The pack goes out 24 hours in advance, merged with the cooling-off period (Chapter 7).

The chair: someone independent of the project under review. They need not hold the highest rank, but they must have the authority to keep the meeting on script. The project lead's role is that of the responding party, not the chair; chairing your own premortem amounts to putting the defendant on the bench.

Attendees: the full project team plus one outside reviewer (a sibling team, a downstream user, or risk control). Five to ten people; above ten, split into two sessions.

## 3 The Meeting Script

| Slot | Stage | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 0–10 | Opening | The chair reads out the script and the rules and declares the assumption "the project has already failed" |
| 10–25 | Silent writing | Everyone writes in silence — "how did we die?" — at least three items per person |
| 25–55 | Reading aloud | Each person reads out their modes of death in turn; names are recorded, arguments are not; exhaust the list before classifying |
| 55–70 | Classification and collision | Individual modes of death are hung on candidate mechanisms; new ones are attached on the spot (with a pointer back to the Part 3 chapter number) |
| 70–85 | Claiming actions | One defensive action per selected mode of death; the verifier and date are claimed on the spot |
| 85–90 | Close | The chair reads out the one-pager; objections are turned into written items; adjourn |

The opening words belong to the chair, three fixed sentences. First: "This meeting assumes that three months from now the project has failed. We do exactly one thing: name the causes of death." Second: "Everything said is recorded, nothing is rebutted; rebuttal begins after minute seventy." Third: "At the end of every mode of death, I will ask for the earliest signal at which it could have been seen." The second sentence is the foundation of the whole meeting. The number-one cause of a failed premortem is rebuttal arriving too early — the first speaker gets explained away, and everyone after them shuts up.

Silent writing (minutes 10–25) is the key component of Klein's method: write first, speak later, so that the framing of the highest-ranking person cannot anchor the room (the meeting-room version of Chapter 13, narrative before evidence). The silence rule is enforced to the end: the chair says nothing during this slot, and the highest-ranking person hands in their pen last.

Classification and collision (minutes 55–70) use the mapping table of Chapter 35, Section 3, and the question sets of Chapter 37. The chair begins probing with the ten general questions (the ledger-check move of Chapter 36, Section 2, becomes an oral examination here), and answers that hit a question are entered on the spot into the "evidence" column of the one-pager.

The discipline of action claiming (minutes 70–85) follows Chapter 36: one action per mode of death; the action must carry a date that can be marked on a calendar; the claimant must have the authority to execute or the authority to escalate. A mode of death that cannot claim an action is entered as an "escalation item," naming the recipient and the deadline. Only two things may leave a premortem: an action with a claimant, and a report with a recipient.

## 3bis Phase-by-phase notes for the chair

Six phases, each with its failure modes and fixes.

Opening (0–10). After the three fixed sentences, one more move: project the material pack and say "deaths must hang on these three items; the unmoored kind is not collected." That sentence rejects half the emotional entries at the door — the difference between a rehearsal and a therapy session is precisely the anchor.

Silent writing (10–25). The chair's only jobs are timing and silence. Two details: colored slips, and the most senior person's slips collected last and read last (anchoring, defended physically). Anyone who breaks silence early gets the chair's finger pointed at the clock, nothing said — maintained silence corrects better than spoken correction.

Read-around (25–55). The discipline is record-don't-refute, and its hard part is sequencing. Chair's hands: left hand writes, right hand never judges. After each slip, one question only — "which layer, which chapter does this resemble?" — and answers that fail go to the board's edge (the pending zone), never forced into a bucket. Ten to twenty slips in thirty minutes is normal density; beyond thirty, people are writing sub-items, and the room merges them live.

Clustering and collision (55–70). Chapter numbers outrank intuitions: every death must attach to a specific chapter, and the unattachable are tagged "outside the taxonomy" and kept — off-taxonomy deaths are raw material for revising the 35.3 mapping table, never discards. The universal ten get spot-asked here, against the high-vote deaths only; votes come from a thirty-second dot round after the read-around.

Claiming (70–85). Three columns per death: action, verification date, claimant. The trap is collective claiming ("we'll keep an eye on it") — the chair extracts one name or the item converts, on the spot, to escalation. Escalations also need a recipient and a deadline; an item with two blank columns does not enter the minutes.

Close (85–90). The chair reads the one-pager aloud and asks the last question: "how will we verify this page in three months?" The answer goes in the footer — date and method. A rehearsal product with no verification plan is, by Chapter 38's standard, a dead document on arrival.

## 4bis A filled one-pager

A teaching sample (synthetic; format as issued). "Death rehearsal, one page. Subject: warehouse-automation phase II cutover. Irreversible point: 11-20, 08:00, the traffic switch. Chair: L. W., quality (not on the project). Death one: practical drift (Ch. 15). The emergency-fallback procedure is written in five steps; the drilled version has three. Defense: rerun the fallback drill under 5% real traffic. Verify: 11-13. Owner: K. Z., platform. Death two: hidden coupling (Ch. 21). The legacy warehouse system's reconciliation job still writes directly to the new database; post-cutover, the overlapping windows will lock tables. Defense: complete the legacy-integration dependency scan; no cutover until it clears. Verify: 11-15. Owner: D. M., architecture. Death three: handover gap (Ch. 30). Cutover day is the day after a roster change; nobody on duty has lived through a prior switchover. Defense: double staffing on cutover day plus a signed handover page in the switchover manual. Verify: 11-18. Owner: S. H., operations. Escalation: incentive anomaly (partial misalignment, Ch. 17 territory): the launch-milestone bonus makes compressed testing pay immediately. Recipient: the division head, written reply by 11-12. Footer: look-back 12-20, method — cutover logs against the three deaths, line by line (owner L. W.). Objections: two, see minutes."

What repays study is the interlocking: every death carries a chapter number, every action a date, every escalation a recipient. The page takes ten minutes to write and three hundred words; its value is banked on December 20, at the look-back, which is the rehearsal's second sitting.

Voting and triage. Default: three dots each, stack-voting allowed. After the sort, the chair checks the layer spread — a top three drawn entirely from the technical layer (the comfortable layer, where deaths are easiest and safest to write) triggers mandatory promotion of one candidate from each of the other two, marked "chair's nominee," with equal standing. Three to five deaths is the cap; five priorities merge through their actions (one action covering several deaths is written once). The arithmetic is Chapter 36's: three actions are one action; five are two and a half.

The health metric, and how to keep it. The hit rate — of the deaths listed, the share that genuinely occurred by look-back — scored in three grades (occurred / variant occurred / did not) at the thirty-day pass, revised at the formal look-back, annotated on the original page in a second ink color, then archived. Healthy band: twenty to forty percent. Zero for three consecutive sessions means the room is writing safe nonsense. Over half means the situation is severe enough that the meeting is redundant — escalate to governance. And when the rate will not land in band, the fault is usually the cast (information homogeneity, Section 1, test two), not the procedure: change the people, then re-run.

## 4 Minutes and the Close

The minutes follow the minimum standard of Chapter 38, Section 4, plus four columns specific to the premortem: the list of modes of death (with mechanism chapter numbers attached), defensive actions, verifiers and dates, and objection items. The objection items are the dividing line between a premortem and an ordinary review meeting: opinions overruled in the meeting are recorded along with the reason for the rejection, and three months later those items are the first batch of evidence for the postmortem.

The one-pager goes to all attendees within 24 hours of the meeting, copied to both sides' superiors. A premortem whose superiors are not copied is a tea party: defensive actions must enter the resource and schedule plans, and there is no way in that bypasses the superiors.

## 5 Follow-Up After the Meeting

Follow-up has exactly two items. First, the defensive actions enter the anomaly ledger with a 30-day check-back point: the reason an action was not executed is itself a diagnosis (no resources, no authority, or an action written too vaguely to execute). Second, the modes of death identified by the premortem enter the comparison column of the postmortem template: at project close or project failure, check item by item what was rehearsed, what happened, and why the rehearsal failed to block it. This comparison is the only feedback loop by which the premortem system improves itself. Skip it, and the premortem degenerates into a ritual.

## 5bis The thirty-day review: fifteen minutes, three people

The look-back is not an email; it is three people at the ledger for a quarter of an hour. Did the actions execute (artifacts on the table)? If not, which of the three — no resources, no authority, or the action was written as a wish (pick one, into the ledger)? Wishes get rewritten into executable form against Chapter 36's cross-check item two. The commonest finding is the third kind: actions claimed in good faith that dissolve on contact with reality — feed it forward by having the next session's chair spend two minutes on the history of wish-written actions, after which claim quality visibly rises. The pass also takes the first cut of the hit-rate grades, revised at three months. And it feeds the failure-modes section below: a session whose actions were mostly wishes is Form Three in embryo, caught early.

## 6bis Detecting your failure mode: three questions

One: in the first five minutes, did the most senior person speak? Anything before the silent phase puts Form One (the credit rehearsal) past even odds — the frame is already theirs. Next time, a pre-meeting ask: first slip collected, first word last.

Two: what share of slips name individuals? Above twenty percent, and the meeting has hybridized into a tribunal. The chair re-states the record rule aloud and moves named items to oral clarification, off the minutes.

Three: how many minutes did claiming take? Under ten means Form Three (going through the motions): thirty minutes of deaths, ten of corner-cut claiming — the page's teeth pulled. Next session, lock the timetable in reverse: claiming starts at minute seventy regardless, and deaths get cut to fit the claiming, never the reverse.

Beyond the three, one cross-session vital: the hit rate (Section 4bis). Out of band twice in a row points at casting, not procedure.

## 7 Three variants

The standard model has three variants, for different sizes of situation.

The micro version (thirty minutes, three to five people): for medium irreversibility — an architecture change, a significant release. Silent writing and read-around merge (write, stick, read in one motion); clustering is dropped (deaths attach to chapters directly, no collision); claiming keeps its full fifteen minutes. Output: a half-page — three deaths, three actions, three dates. The micro version differs from Chapter 36's solo thirty in exactly one way: there are other people in the room. Where three to five colleagues can sit together, a meeting beats three solo runs stitched together; the stitch loses the collisions.

The double session (two ninety-minute meetings, a week apart): for mergers, outsourcing switches, platform migrations — wherever the dependency surface is large and poorly mapped. Session one runs standard, producing a death list; the interval is for evidence (dependency scans, supplier financials, incident-record searches against the list); session two is not a repetition but a hearing — each death against its new evidence, confirmed, upgraded, or withdrawn, withdrawals reasoned. The logic is investigation: hypothesis, evidence, findings. A single sitting over large terrain ships a page that is half true; split it and the quality doubles.

The anonymous written round (a week, asynchronous): for high political temperature, or teams that cannot meet across time zones. The organizer issues a blind form (three deaths each, layered); the chair clusters and attaches chapter numbers alone, and the output ships as a "rehearsal report," deaths unattributed, clustering reasons stated. What is lost is the collision phase — the associative sparks of live exchange — and a second anonymous round after the report recovers two or three: reading others' deaths breeds new ones. Quality sits below the in-room version and far above an in-room version nobody dared speak in: where the temperature is wrong, anonymity is not the second-best form; it is the only form that collects true deaths.

All three share the one-page format, the follow-up rules (actions to the ledger, thirty-day review), and the hit-rate statistic. They also upgrade into each other: a micro that hangs deaths on three or more chapters grows into a standard session; a standard session that cannot resolve its dependencies grows into the double. The trigger is one number: when a third of the deaths cannot be claimed, the situation was bigger than the meeting, and the meeting grows.

## 8 What the rehearsal cannot do

Say precisely what the instrument measures, so it is never blamed for what it cannot. Its target is specific: social pressure against speaking ill (Klein's core finding), and both the silent phase and the death-assumed script aim exactly there. Three failure classes it does not cover, each with a patch from elsewhere in the book.

Knowledge blind spots. What nobody in the room knows cannot be written. Only outside views and materials reach it — the dependency scan, the ledger search — which is why the material pack is not optional: a rehearsal's ceiling equals its participants' knowledge plus its materials', and technique raises output toward that ceiling, never past it.

Incentive-layer deaths. Criticizing one's own appraisal scheme is naturally costly (writing "our bonus structure rewards compressed testing" is writing one's superior's exam), so incentive entries run systematically short. The chair's standing question — "one layer up, is this an incentive problem?" — asked aloud, beats waiting for volunteers, and the escalation lane (Section 3bis, claiming) gives the answer somewhere to go.

Cast homogeneity. Five people with the same role, tenure, and background produce five similar lists; independence without diversity is echo. Diversity here means informational: another industry once, a transfer from downstream, under six months in post — each carries paths the others cannot imagine. The organizer casts for information trajectories, and the outside seat (Section 2) exists for exactly this.

The three patches share one moral: the rehearsal is an amplifier. It amplifies whatever knowledge and viewpoint the room brings; the ceiling is set before the meeting, by the pack and the cast, and no facilitation art raises it.

## 9 Where moderators come from

Moderator supply is the bottleneck of institutionalization: everyone has read the script; few will tell a project owner to be quiet for twenty-five minutes. Three steps grow them.

Observe: two sessions as a counter, with one task — count the deviations. Rebuttals allowed early: how many? Claiming minutes? Named slips? The deviation list a counter brings out of their first observation is their first textbook.

Assist: one real session as deputy. Division: the chair runs the room, the deputy keeps the log and the clock, and when the chair slips (rebuttal allowed, close question forgotten) the deputy's only move is a note in-session and a ten-minute debrief within forty-eight hours — late debriefs become precedents.

Pool and rotate: moderators rotate across the organization (the same logic as Chapter 36's verifier rotation), and each session gets scored by its participants on three axes — procedure held, rebuttals intercepted, claims closed. Three stable sessions admits a moderator to the pool; keep the pool at five or more, because an organization with one good moderator loses the practice when that one transfers.

One counter-intuitive note on casting: the best moderators are usually not the safety or quality professionals but technical leads from unrelated teams. The professional turns the session toward an inspection; the peer keeps its texture — colleagues arguing about a project. Both belong in the pool, in roughly a two-to-three ratio.

## 10 Where the data goes

The rehearsal's outputs have four destinations, named so the data does not die in the minutes.

Defense actions enter the anomaly ledger with a thirty-day review point (Section 5), watched by the housekeeper. Death lists' chapter numbers feed the Chapter 35 mapping table's calibration — a situation class whose rehearsals keep hitting the same two or three chapters has announced its fingerprint. Off-taxonomy deaths and recurring death variants feed the Chapter 37 library's first rule (additions from practice; the rehearsal is the postmortem's second source), and a real death that fits no existing question deserves a new question by the season's end. The look-back's hit sheet feeds Chapter 38's comparison column: the pre-mortem list is the postmortem's first cited document. All four destinations land with one housekeeper — the same keeper as the ledger and the question library — who reconciles quarterly. The test of the whole data flow: pull any one-year-old page at random and reach, in ten minutes, its actions' status, its look-back result, and the review that cites it. Reachable: the flow is alive. Not: you have been holding meetings, not running a system.

## 11 The organizational calendar

A model year, to show where the rehearsal sits in governance rhythm.

Quarterly, by calendar: one standard session, topic drawn from the extreme quadrant of the 35.6 distribution chart, fixed in the first month's second week — clear of quarter-end delivery pressure, with preparation time to spare. Micro sessions run as teams see fit; the organization counts pages only.

Event-triggered: after any peer's major public accident, one additional session for mechanism-similar projects, with the external report as the pack's third item. The window has a shelf life — external accidents legitimize bad talk for about two weeks, and the temperature falls back; schedule inside the window or not at all.

Milestone-fixed: one month before go-live, before merger completion, before an outsourcing switch — three standing hooks written into the project template's checklist ("rehearsal scheduled?"). Hooks convert "remember to hold one" into "cannot be skipped"; institutional memory should never rent space in human memory.

Annually: hit-rate statistics across all pages, moderator-pool ratings, variant usage — one page to governance. The most important line is the absence list: which major irreversible points ran with no rehearsal on record. Absences outrank hits as a reading, because an unscheduled rehearsal's risk stands entirely outside every page.

The year's arithmetic: four standard sessions at six hours each including preparation, plus micros and event-triggered sessions — for a hundred-person organization, roughly eighty person-hours a year. Beside the organizational cost of one real accident (the Chapter 1 tables), the comparison needs no second pass.

## 12 The objections, answered

Five objections every sponsor meets, with answers.

"One: the project team knows the risks best; an outside chair is ceremony." The team knows the risks that have been discussed. The rehearsal exists for the undiscussed, and both the silent phase and the outside chair are doors built for the undiscussed.

"Two: our project is small; this is overhead." Small projects run the micro version at thirty minutes. The criterion was never size; it is the cost of retreat (Section 1, test one), which does not scale with headcount.

"Three: nobody acts on the lists anyway." Unclaimed deaths convert to escalations with recipients and deadlines; if escalations also die, that fact is itself the finding — the rehearsal has measured the organization's deafness, which needed measuring (Chapter 19's criterion).

"Four: meeting time is expensive." The comparison set is wrong. Compare against the historical cost of this class of irreversible point — the number sits in the organization's own failure portrait (Chapter 38, Section 7) — not against an empty calendar.

"Five: this frightens people." Watch which meetings actually frighten: the ones where hazards live in corridors, or the one where they sit on a page with names and dates. Fear comes from unease with nowhere to go; the page is where unease goes.

And a sequence note for the sponsor: start with one willing team and one real decision — a micro session, run to completion — rather than a policy. A policy without a working sample collects every objection at full strength; one completed page (three deaths, three actions, one thirty-day review behind it) changes the question from "should we" to "how do we run it well," and half the resistance dissolves with the question. The same ladder runs through all five usage chapters: the table and the thirty minutes need no permission, the question pass borrows an existing meeting, the first rewritten review costs nothing, and the rehearsal — which needs people and authority — comes last. The promotion path is itself a staircase of decreasing friction; climb it in order.

## 6 The Premortem's Three Failure Modes

Mode one: the self-congratulation meeting. Symptoms: the list of modes of death is all "the client is too demanding" and "the market shifted"; the defensive actions are all "communicate more." Pathology: the chair is not independent and rebuttal came early. Fix: change the chair and reread the second opening sentence.

Mode two: the anxiety meeting. Symptoms: the list of modes of death runs endlessly long and not one has a claimed action. Pathology: the rehearsal is being used as venting. Fix: force convergence with the rule "no claimable action means it converts to an escalation item," and adjourn on the ninetieth minute.

Mode three: the rubber stamp. Symptoms: the one-pager is identical to the previous one. Pathology: the premortem has no interface with the ledger or the postmortem (the two follow-up items of Section 5 were never executed). Fix: open this meeting with the hit rate of the last premortem's modes of death as page one.

A premortem is not a one-time ritual but a machine that needs maintenance, and its maintenance log is the running comparison of one-pagers. Without the comparison, a premortem slides into mode three from its third session onward.

## Self-Check: Five Questions

1. For the nearest irreversible decision on your desk, how many of the premortem materials pack's three items exist right now?
2. If the meeting were held tomorrow, who would chair it? Would they dare make the project lead stay silent for twenty-five minutes?
3. At the last review meeting you attended, were objections written down or explained away?
4. Was the last premortem's one-pager copied to both sides' superiors?
5. If your organization has held premortems, where is the comparison table of the one-pagers? If there is no comparison, which session will you start backfilling from?
