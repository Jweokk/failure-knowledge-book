# Chapter 36: The Thirty Minutes Before a Decision

> What this chapter covers: the complete route from the situation assessment table to a conclusion page reading "which three deaths I most resemble," in timeboxes of 5 + 15 + 10 minutes; with a full walkthrough of a synthetic example.
> When to turn back to it: before any decision about to touch a point of irreversibility (Chapter 7); and when you want others to adopt this routine and need to hand them a minimal version.
> Estimated reading time: 30 minutes.

## 36.1 The route at a glance

The thirty-minute routine is the execution version of the assessment table (Chapter 35), and the master gate to the whole book's mechanism content. It does exactly one thing: before the point of irreversibility, it turns "which deaths am I most likely to die of" from a vague anxiety into three concrete mechanism names and three matching check actions.

Three timeboxes:

| Segment | Length | Action | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fill the table | 5 minutes | six-parameter assessment (table in 35.5) | 6 levels + basis |
| Consult the library | 15 minutes | check the hit mechanism chapters' criteria, weak signals, and comparison cases | candidates narrowed to 2–3 |
| Write the conclusion | 10 minutes | the three-part conclusion page | one page: three deaths + check actions + verifiers |

The threshold of applicability: the decision touches a point of irreversibility (signing, going live, announcing, committing externally), or the investment has grown large enough that undoing it would require a second tier of resources. Everyday decisions below the threshold need not run the full routine: borrow the conclusion page's format (three deaths + check actions) and use it in a five-minute spoken version.

Timing matters as well: run the routine before the decision point, and leave room for at least one overnight gap (Chapter 7's cooling period). Run after the meeting, the output is review material; run before the meeting, the output is armor.

## 36.1bis The ten minutes before the thirty (preparation)

Ten minutes of preparation, not counted inside the thirty, keep the routine from spinning empty. Four items.

Materials: one page of the decision's key numbers — budget, schedule, dependencies, the record of comparable past decisions. The routine does not digest a fifty-page feasibility report; it consumes conclusions and numbers you have already digested. Missing materials are handled by Chapter 35's evidence rule: whichever numbers are missing, the corresponding parameter fills at the most dangerous level.

Venue: a table you can write on and a stretch of time nobody can interrupt. Three timeboxes do not survive parallel messaging — phone silent, the thirty minutes on the calendar as immovable. Rescheduling is the routine's commonest death: it always looks postponable, and after three postponements the decision point has arrived.

Role: confirm the verifier candidate (the selection standard sits in 36.4). Ask before the meeting, not after — a person asked in advance can decline or nominate someone; a person asked afterwards can only be doing you a favor.

Entry point: one sentence answering "which point of irreversibility does this decision touch" (Chapter 7's test: signing, going live, announcing, external commitment, or undoing requires second-tier resources). The sentence goes at the top of the form. If you cannot write it, the decision probably fails the applicability threshold — use the spoken version.

A note on the compressed version: only the timeboxes may compress; the verifier may never be dropped. Ten minutes, one death, one action — still needs a name and a moment, or the output is self-consolation. And a compressed run that ends uneventfully earns a full thirty afterward: rerun the decision at leisure and record which mechanisms the compressed pass missed. Those misses go into the team's criteria cards. Compression is an emergency posture; habitual compression is habitual myopia.

## 36.2 First five minutes: fill the table

Fill in the six levels using the printable version in Section 35.5. One discipline: every level carries its basis, and a level without a basis is filled at the most dangerous level. The parameters for which you cannot write a basis within five minutes are exactly where your information is darkest — and that fact is itself worth recording (Section 36.6 returns to this point).

The common error is turning the form into a declaration. The basis for "incentive alignment: aligned" cannot be "our team has a good atmosphere"; it must point to a concrete appraisal clause: does the role that creates risk bear the consequences? If you cannot point to the clause, you start at partially misaligned.

## 36.3 Middle fifteen minutes: consult the library

Take the three to six mechanisms (chapter numbers) hit on the 35.3 table and run three fixed actions on each, budgeting two to three minutes per mechanism. The actions can be this fast because every Part Three chapter has a fixed skeleton (see Writing Standards, Section 10, in 规范/写作规范.md); the locations never move.

Action one: check the trigger criteria (Section 1). Every mechanism chapter gives three criteria of the form "once visible, already running." Ask each one: is there identifiable corresponding evidence in my situation? None of the three — the mechanism leaves the main list.

Action two: scan the weak-signal list (Section 4). Each chapter carries six to ten weak signals, each annotated with "the reason it was talked down at the time." One hit: mark it. Two or more: the mechanism rises to the top of the main list.

Action three: read one comparison case (Section 3 + Appendix B). Read only the case card's decision-point and minimal-counterfactual passages, not the full course of events. The purpose: give the candidate mechanism a concrete face, so that you do not discard it merely because you cannot picture it happening.

By the end of the fifteen minutes you should be holding: a main list of two or three mechanisms (each with criterion evidence), one to three observation items (suspicion without evidence), and the names of the mechanisms removed. Removals leave a trace too: at the three-month review, the judgment "it did not look like this then" is itself subject to test.

## 36.4 Last ten minutes: write the conclusion page

The conclusion page has three fixed parts, on one page.

Part one: the three deaths I most resemble. One line each: mechanism name (chapter number) + one sentence on the situation + the criterion hit that qualifies it. Model: "Procedural drift (Chapter 15): the sampling process went live three months ago; practice runs at 2% while the standard says 5%, and nobody has the authority to change the difference (criterion 1 hit)."

Part two: one check action per death. Each action must be completable before the decision point and must produce an artifact. Model: "before go-live, pull the actual execution rate of the sampling records against the standard; if the gap exceeds half, the switch does not ship." One action per death, no more (the same discipline as the minimal counterfactual): three actions equal zero actions.

Part three: verifier and moment. State who checks the artifacts of part two, and at what moment. A conclusion page without a verifier is a diary, not a tool.

## 36.4bis Making the fifteen minutes run: three speed techniques

The library segment only runs at speed if the stock is prepared.

Technique one: criteria cards. For each mechanism you hit often, make one card — three trigger criteria on the front, the weak-signal list on the back, both copied from the chapter's Sections 1 and 4. Making the cards is itself the close reading. A dozen cards covers most teams, and the library's first two moves become card-flipping instead of book-hunting.

Technique two: pre-annotation. In the two or three chapters you use most, highlight the decision-point and minimal-counterfactual paragraphs of the comparison cases. Move three of the library becomes reading the highlights.

Technique three: a fixed log format. Every library session records one line per mechanism: name, criteria hits, signal hits, reason for exclusion. A fixed format is what makes the three-month-later comparison possible — "what we thought then" against "what happened since" is a row you can actually diff.

## 36.5 Walkthrough of a synthetic example

The scenario below is synthesized for teaching purposes (not a real case); it demonstrates how the three timeboxes connect.

Scenario: a payments team decides to replace item-by-item manual review of refunds with automatic approval plus 5% sampling. Decision point: the go-live of the configuration switch (point of irreversibility: automatic approval takes effect and users begin receiving refunds nobody has reviewed).

Filling the table (5 minutes): coupling = medium (an erroneous refund hits single payments and the support queue; a manual stop-payment works, but drowns at volume); redundancy authenticity = low (the fallback beneath the automatic rules is a manual console built on the same code, with no independent verification line); incentive alignment = partially misaligned (support hours are the team's own appraisal item, while financial losses are carried by the risk-control department); time pressure = short (it must go live before the big promotion); scale = multiple teams (payments, risk control, support); regulatory density = dense (payments are subject to regulatory inspection; rule changes require an audit trail). Every basis is identifiable.

Consulting the library (15 minutes): the 35.3 table hits four rows — Chapter 15 (procedural drift: T = short + rules versus workload), Chapter 11 (normalization of deviation: the sampling ratio is nominal and the consequences delayed), Chapter 12 (the metric replaces the goal: the hours metric trumps losses), Chapter 26 (automation and skill decay: manual review skill idled). Action one, criteria: Chapter 12's criterion 3, "someone has paid a price for pointing this out," has no evidence yet — demoted to observation; Chapter 15's criterion 1, "the same deviation has occurred more than twice," hits (two instances last month of "temporarily lowering the sampling rate during peak," neither run through the change process); Chapter 11's criterion 2 hits (single losses are small and have always been explained away as "contained"). Action two, weak signals: Chapter 15 hits two (what training teaches differs from the manual; new hires are taught by the old hands). Action three, one case: the procedural-drift comparison case confirms the shape of "a rule written to be ignored."

Writing the conclusion page (10 minutes): the three deaths are set as procedural drift (15), normalization of deviation (11), and automation and skill decay (26, observation promoted: manual review skill will decay while idle, and six months from now nobody may be able to execute the rollback switch by hand). Check actions: 1. before go-live, pull the actual sampling execution rate for the past three months; if the gap against the standard exceeds half, do not ship; 2. beside the automatic rules, add one independent verification line (refunds above an amount threshold keep manual review), heterogeneous in origin; 3. keep a monthly manual-review drill and write proficiency into the shift handover. Verifiers: the risk-control interface checks 1 at the go-live review; the duty manager checks 3 monthly.

The most valuable element of this conclusion page is action 2: it comes from no mechanism's text, but surfaces naturally once the three mechanisms have been located — the structural patch of adding the missing independent redundancy. The routine's output is not "having learned some terms"; it is three concrete actions, each with a verifier.

## 36.5bis Three more conclusion pages, in template form

Infrastructure (a data-center migration). Death one, hidden coupling (21): the migration list omits the legacy backup system's dependency — criterion "nobody can state the full blast radius" hits. Action: run a full dependency scan before migration and reconcile it against interviews. Death two, the redundancy illusion (22): the backup site shares the primary's carrier — same-origin backup. Action: one switchover rehearsal with the backup site genuinely carrying production traffic. Death three, handover gap (30): the migration window crosses a quarter boundary and the on-call roster turns over. Action: the migration handbook's handover page signed and compared before the roster changes. Verifier: the platform lead, at the migration review.

Hiring (a critical seat). Death one, the expert-intuition failure zone (14): interviewers judge on conversations in a highly regular setting. Action: add one work-sample test isomorphic to the job's real task. Death two, narrative before evidence (13): the role was announced publicly and the candidate profile became a commitment at announcement. Action: a third-party colleague reviews the evidence list behind the hiring standard before the final round. Verifier: the HR partner, before the offer goes out.

Contract (a supplier agreement). Death one, maturity mismatch (28): a five-year term against a counterparty whose financial health moves quarterly. Action: annual financial-health review plus exit provisions in the contract. Death two, scale crossing thresholds (24): the supplier would carry over forty percent of the category. Action: second-source qualification begins in the same period as signing. Verifier: legal, before the seal.

All three share the same skeleton: every death cites a criterion, every action has an artifact and a verification moment, and the whole fits on one page.

The walkthrough, revisited. Three notes on what the synthetic example was quietly doing. The fill: the heaviest level was redundancy = low, and its basis ("the fallback is the same codebase's manual back office; no independent verification line") does two jobs at once — evidencing the level and pre-drawing the repair (an independent, heterogeneous verification line). Good bases carry repair hints; bad bases ("the fallback feels shaky") are mood entries. The library: demoting Chapter 12 for want of criterion 3 deserves enlarging — a mechanism can be real while the current evidence is thin, and "demoted to observation" means exactly "evidence does not currently support it," with the record kept. Exclusion is half of screening: knowing which mechanisms you cannot yet evidence is worth as much as knowing which you can. The page: action 3 (the monthly manual-review drill) serves death three and runs on a monthly clock, decoupled from the decision itself — conclusion pages carry two action classes, one-shot actions before the decision point and periodic actions after it, both with verifiers, at different moments. Automation decisions almost always spawn the second class (Chapter 26's territory).

The method has a lineage. Prospective-hindsight research shows that framing the task as "imagine the event has occurred; trace back the causes" reliably produces more, and better, causes than asking "what could go wrong" (Mitchell, Russo & Pennington, Journal of Behavioral Decision Making 2(1): 25–38, 1989; Crossref record verified). The conclusion-page segment borrows the effect directly: "which three deaths I most resemble" is hindsight phrasing, prospectively deployed. Gary Klein developed the effect into the project premortem — Chapter 39 is his ninety-minute version; this chapter's thirty-minute version compresses it to individual decisions, and the compression's price is a single perspective: one person cannot pre-mortem their own blind spots, which is why 36.4 insists on an external verifier.

## 36.6 Three failed uses

Failed use one: filling the table without basis. A level without a basis is decoration, and the whole routine fails with it. Detection is simple: show your table to an uninvolved colleague and ask which level they do not believe.

Failed use two: the library visit becomes story browsing. Reading stories during the fifteen minutes will overrun; read only the decision-point and minimal-counterfactual passages. The routine's purpose is screening mechanisms; cases are calibrators, and one is enough.

Failed use three: a conclusion page that says "heighten attention, keep monitoring." Eight characters equal zero actions. Check: does part two contain a check action that can be given a calendar date? If not, rewrite it.

One warning in the opposite direction: the routine can also do harm. Exploratory decisions (Part Six, Chapter 40) do not fit it: rehearsing death in a situation that needs trial and error kills the trying. To judge whether your decision is exploratory or operational, use Chapter 40's three criteria — then decide whether to spend the thirty minutes.

## 36.6bis The five-point cross-check

After writing the page, five minutes of mutual inspection — hand it to an uninvolved colleague, the same move as cross-filling. Five checks, in order.

One: does every death cite a criterion (chapter and number)? An uncited death is a story; back to the library. Two: does every action have an artifact? Artifacts are data, records, drill results — never "awareness," "culture," or "attention." Three: can every action be given a calendar date? An undatable action has no owner. Four: does the verifier meet the three standards (no stake, has time, knows what abnormal looks like)? Missing any one, replace them. Five: do the three deaths cover different layers (Chapter 9's three)? Three deaths in one layer — all technical, say — means the library missed the organizational and incentive candidates; go back.

Two checks failed: rewrite the page. Four failed: the routine itself deformed — back to 36.1bis and restart. The inspector initials the signature line and leaves; the whole check stays inside ten minutes, because a costlier check will not survive contact with a calendar.

The page also has a life cycle: once its actions are verified, the note archives to a public place (a fixed slot in the project tool), tagged with the decision subject and the mechanisms hit. Each quarter, pull the archive's three numbers: pages filed, action on-time verification rate, and the cross-check item most often failed. Three numbers, three responses — low usage sends you back to rollout step one, low verification rate to the verifier's time commitment, the frequent failure to the checklist itself. Stop there; a dashboard would be over-engineering.

## 36.7 Four common questions

One: "the decision-maker has no thirty minutes." Delegate it: an assistant runs the fill and the library; the decision-maker attends only the final ten. Delegation softens the bases by one grade (a ghostwritten basis is a softer basis), so the verifier adds one question: which level do you disagree with? Whatever they name, refill on the spot.

Two: "my actions got overruled." Overruling is a normal output — the routine put the risk on the table, and the table's decision rights belong to the line. The page then gains a line: the action overruled, the reason, and a signature acknowledging "risk known." That line converts an override from default to trace, and the trace is itself a defense (a sticky note against Chapter 18's diffusion).

Three: "several decisions are running in parallel." Sequence them by irreversibility — nearest decision point first. The library stock is shared, so the second decision's thirty minutes runs shorter than the first. Never stack multiple decisions into one thirty: the timeboxes are budgeted per decision.

Four: "we are remote." Relay the timeboxes through a shared document: fill and library asynchronously; the conclusion page written live in a fifteen-minute call. Remote blunts the case reading (screens flatten texture), so one person reads the case aloud while everyone listens — slow reading is the price of admission, and reading aloud enforces it.

A fifth arrives with use: "does this make an organization timid?" Look at the output, not the mood: the page's yield is three actions and a signature, after which the decision proceeds. The routine blocks exactly two things — actions refused while the decision sails through (its job), and exploration decisions wrongly routed into the routine (Chapter 40's reverse warning). Timid versus careful divides on the artifact: the careful produce actions; the timid produce delay. This routine only recognizes actions. And the honest scorecard: teams that run the page do not decide less — they decide the same and fight fewer fires afterwards, with fewer "we never thought of that" moments in the post-mortems. Those two lines are what the thirty minutes actually buys.

## 36.8 Division of labor with the pre-mortem

The thirty-minute routine and the ninety-minute death rehearsal (Chapter 39) are one family at two sizes, separated on three axes. Scale: thirty minutes serves one decision; the rehearsal serves a project or a carrier class (the extreme quadrant of the 35.6 distribution chart). People: the routine runs solo; the rehearsal needs at least five — viewpoint diversity is its fuel, and a solo rehearsal is only a longer conversation with yourself. Timing: the routine runs close to the decision point (days before); the rehearsal close to milestones (a month before go-live, before architecture freeze, at sanction). The outputs differ too: a three-line note versus a hazard list with owners. The usual sequence runs rehearsal first, routine second — the rehearsal's hazards become the project's checkpoints, and each checkpoint's decision then runs its own thirty minutes. A quarter on the two-tier calendar: a rehearsal early in the month; the checkpoint decisions each taking their thirty minutes mid-month; ten minutes at month-end laying the notes beside the hazard list to see whether both tiers hit the same mechanisms — a match means the mechanism is the project's theme, and its criteria card moves to the front of the team's card box. The two sizes do not compete for time; they compete for coverage. The rehearsal sweeps the area; the routine scans the point. When point and area agree, coverage is real.

## 36.9 Organizational rollout, three steps

The routine survives easily in one person and dies easily in an organization; three steps carry it across.

Step one: a pilot cell. One decision-dense team (product, operations, portfolio — any), two or three people, ten full runs. Track two numbers only: runs completed, and which of the five cross-checks fails most. After ten runs, one retrospective: build the team's own criteria cards for its high-frequency mechanisms — a team card library outperforms the generic one on home ground.

Step two: publicize the templates. The pilot's cards, sample pages, and checklist move into the organization's shared template shelf — placed inside tools everyone already passes through (the project-initiation flow, the release flow), never into a free-standing "knowledge base." Knowledge bases have no foot traffic; templates placed in doorways do.

Step three: quarterly sampling. Governance draws five archived pages a quarter and reads only two things: verification rate and criterion-citation completeness. Results never enter individual appraisal — the moment they do, filling turns into performing, and 36.2's warning returns. The results feed exactly two uses: more cards, better procedure. Rollouts fail in the same three ways everywhere: nobody accompanied the first runs (a solitary tool rarely survives its third use), templates went to an unvisited knowledge base, and sampling became appraisal (appraisal summons performance). Run the three steps, dodge the three pits, and within a year the conclusion page is part of the organization's working vocabulary — at which point the tool has become tissue.

## 36.10 Three interfaces with existing governance

Whether the page stacks one more sheet of paper or becomes part of the decision's skeleton depends on three interfaces.

Reviews. Wherever a sanction review, architecture review, or change review exists, the page's three parts become the fixed front page of the review pack. Nothing else in the pack changes. The review itself shifts register: reviewers read the three deaths first and ask whether a harder death was missed — additions go onto the front page before the discussion of approval. No charter amendment needed; it is one sheet of paper.

Approval chains. The verifier's signature line and the approval chain's countersign line are different instruments — do not merge them. Countersigning is a power act (consent or refusal); verification is a fact act (did the action happen; does the artifact exist). Merged, the verifier becomes countersigner number N, and the fact act inherits the dilution of the power act (Chapter 18, replayed in a form field). The correct integration is one mandatory field on the chain — "conclusion page archive No." — and the chain will not advance without it.

Post-mortems. Chapter 38's review opens by pulling the archived page: at the decision point, this is what we expected. Line by line against the outcome — how many of the three deaths occurred, how many actions held, did the verifier verify — the page gives the post-mortem a snapshot of the judgment as it stood, not a reconstruction. The page raises the post-mortem's ceiling by giving it something honest to compare against.

All three interfaces share one principle: the new tool does not build a second building; it becomes one sheet and one field inside the existing flow. Grafted tools outlive transplanted ones.

## Self-check: five questions

1. Your most recent irreversible decision: which of the three timeboxes could you actually not run? Was the obstacle time or authority?
2. The last external commitment you made without an overnight gap: was it worth it, looking back?
3. Can the actions in part two of your conclusion page be given calendar dates? Which one cannot?
4. Who on your team should serve as the verifier? Does that person have the authority to halt?
5. What corresponds in your own system to action 2 of the synthetic example (the independent verification line)? Does it exist now?
