# Startups and Products | st

> This page has five sections: record quality, feedback delay, coupling, the three deadliest mechanisms, and what outsiders most easily mislearn.

## 1 Record Quality

The worst record of any domain, and structurally so (the extreme form of Chapter 2's survival skew). Firsthand material consists mainly of founders' own postmortems (this book's 4 st cases were collected and verified through Failory Cemetery and all contain the founders' own words), but the sample is self-selected, the attribution self-reported, and the moment of writing sits under emotional and legal constraint (NDAs, refund clauses); bankruptcy and liquidation filings offer a factual skeleton without narrative; media retellings cannot serve as evidence (the Chapter 19 rule applies here too). The gap: the fastest and largest deaths (the cash-burnout type) are precisely the least postmortemed. Overall assessment: read this domain's failure material with Chapter 2's three quality checks switched on the whole way through.

## 2 Feedback Delay

The cash-flow clock: measured in months, and nonlinear (when financing tightens, every company's runway shortens at once; st-004's Xinja attributed its shutdown directly to "an increasingly difficult funding environment"). Product-market fit: measured in quarters, but actively deferred by the growth narrative (st-001's Atrium kept expanding without reaching fit). The regulatory clock (crypto, fintech): measured in years, and exogenous (st-002's Koinex, where the freezing of bank channels transmitted regulatory uncertainty). That the distribution of feedback delay is governed by exogenous variables is what essentially distinguishes this domain from the others.

## 3 Coupling

Coupling at the level of the individual company is low (one company's death does not directly infect others); coupling at the level of the ecosystem is high: the financing environment, channel policies, and platform rules are an upstream shared by the entire industry (Chapter 33's form of dependency concentrated on the venture cycle). For platform-type startups, coupling rises with success: a small company dependent on a single acquisition channel, a single cloud vendor, a single ecosystem buys coupling that becomes its jugular as it scales (Chapter 33).

## 4 The Three Deadliest Mechanisms

Escalation of commitment (Chapter 27): investment continued past the point where product-market fit had failed to arrive (st-001; the founder's own sentence is the most honest line of postmortem in this book). Narrative before evidence (Chapter 13): the growth narrative running ahead of product validation (st-003). Horizon mismatch (Chapter 28): the cost cycle of the customer-acquisition model mismatched to the cycle of the funding environment (st-004). Next most frequent: regulatory lag and capture (st-002's bank channels); nonlinear scaling across thresholds (organization and product failing on two fronts during expansion).

## 5 What Outsiders Most Easily Mislearn

Mislearning one: taking the attribution in a failure postmortem for the truth. A founder's attribution passes three filters — self-report bias, confidentiality clauses, and the needs of narrative. Read the postmortem to learn "what the information environment was at the time," not "why it died." Mislearning two: treating the survivors' list of lessons as a complete set (Section 2.2: what is absent is precisely the deadliest death — cash exhaustion, co-founder rupture, and the regulatory veto have the lowest rates of appearance in public postmortems). Mislearning three: taking an anecdote for a method. "Raise densely, validate quickly" appears in the sample with both successes and st-001-style failures; an anecdote cannot tell you the prior probability. This is the book's recurring reminder, and it is loudest in the startup domain.

## 8 Mechanism Variations in This Domain

Four startup deaths, four mechanisms. Escalation wears the term sheet: Atrium's $75 million and ScaleFactor's $103 million converted into irreversible organizational commitments the day they landed — hiring, pricing, footprint — so the first post-funding pivot carries the heaviest sunk-cost tax in business. The check is textual: in the pivot meeting's minutes, how often does "already invested" appear as an argument? The external adjudicator of Chapter 27 has a startup name here: the board.

Narrative before evidence runs on the raise calendar: the deck finalizes and the narrative seals, which is why the automation-accounting case reads as narrative outrunning product by exactly one funding cycle. The device is definitional disclosure — write the core metric's definition ("what counts as automated," "what counts as active") into the offering documents' annex, so the definition itself becomes a disclosure.

Horizon mismatch arrives as environment: the Indian exchange died when the banking-channel premise was withdrawn by policy — a product cycle of weeks hanging on a regulatory cycle of years. The local artifact is a dependency list of premises (banking rails, app stores, cloud terms), each priced for single-sourcing and substitutability. Xinja is the clean counter-case: recognizing a negative-expected-value continuation and surrendering the license — the book's tidiest startup stop-loss.

## 9 Cross-Reading

Pair with Finance (the two ends of the financing chain; together, a diligence manual). Pair with Software (startups die of product-market mismatch, mature platforms die of coupling and scale — nearly disjoint mechanism sets, and the mapping from organizational maturity to failure type is the point of reading both). Pair with Public Policy (a single firm's survival hanging on a policy timetable; policy uncertainty is part of the venture's risk exposure, whether or not it appears in the deck).
