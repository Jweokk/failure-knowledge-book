# Maritime and Transport | ma

> This page has five sections: record quality, feedback delay, coupling, the three deadliest mechanisms, and what outsiders most easily mislearn.

## 1 Record Quality

The United Kingdom's MAIB (Marine Accident Investigation Branch) is the global reference: full texts published on the gov.uk platform (a dual track of formal reports and general investigation reports; all 13 ma cases in this book come from its 2026 reports), and investigations independent of the maritime judicial process (Chapter 3). The IMO framework establishes the principles of investigation, but jurisdiction fragments with the flag state: the same accident under another flag may have no public full text (Section 3.4). The China Maritime Safety Administration's bulletin channels have limited availability [Appendix C, to be verified]. Overall assessment: small and mid-size vessels on flags of convenience are the dark zone of the record, while small fishing vessels inside MAIB's jurisdiction are, contrary to expectation, the densest sample library of small accidents anywhere (9 of this book's 13 cases are fishing vessels and small workboats — which is precisely MAIB's value: it turned the failures of ordinary people into searchable knowledge).

## 2 Feedback Delay

At the operational layer, feedback is measured in seconds and minutes (dozing onto rocks, collisions), isomorphic with aviation; at the maintenance and fatigue layers, in weeks and months (ma-013's sleep debt accumulating over a week; ma-004's seawater pipe fitted in 1998); at the institutional layer (flag-state regulation, the survey regime), in years. Fishing has a special structure: voyage economics make "keep going" the default (Chapter 27), so feedback delay is shouldered head-on by the system as a matter of course.

## 3 Coupling

Within a single hull, coupling is moderate (the physical links of propulsion, electrical power, windlass, and firefighting: ma-001's loss of power leaving the anchor unable to hold is coupling in textbook form). Ship-to-ship coupling rises steeply in close-quarters operations (ma-005's tanker and fishing vessel; ma-008's collision in a traffic separation scheme). Coupling outward varies with the water: coastal waters and traffic lanes connect a single-ship accident directly to the interface of public waterway order (VTS), and the VTS's own boundary of authority is often the very scene where responsibility diffuses (ma-008).

## 4 The Three Deadliest Mechanisms

Practical drift (Chapter 15): the watch alarm masked by music (ma-002); the lone watchkeeper asleep (ma-003); the new procedure executed off-pattern (ma-007). The invisibility of maintenance (Chapter 29): the seawater pipe fitted in 1998 (ma-004); the non-original bearing that left the anchor useless after the blackout (ma-001); the inspection gap in the lifting chain (ma-013). Margin erosion (Chapter 25): drain openings made smaller one change at a time (ma-009); working postures ossified into habit (ma-012). Next most frequent: normalization of deviance (ma-005's give-way assumption); handover rupture (ma-008's VTS authority boundary).

## 5 What Outsiders Most Easily Mislearn

Mislearning one: imagining the whole industry from the sample of big ships and cruise liners. Most individual vessels in the world's merchant and fishing fleets have neither a modern wheelhouse nor redundancy; half the lessons in MAIB reports come from vessels under 25 meters. Mislearning two: taking flag-state registration as a regulatory guarantee (the fragmentation of Chapter 3). Mislearning three: dismissing the reporting value of small accidents. Fishing-vessel capsizings and man-overboard cases are the best teaching material for the pattern of "one person, one point, low-cost defense": the defenses are cheap (watch alarms, immersion suits, anchoring redundancy), failure is cheap and lethal, and the entire cross-industry sense of proportion is contained there.

## 8 Mechanism Variations in This Domain

Normalization compressed to its minimum: one person, deep night, no second pair of eyes. Both fishing-vessel groundings carry the same physics of fatigue. The domain-specific device is the watch alarm, and the local lesson is that "tested monthly" answers the wrong question — the test must reproduce the worst condition, and the worst condition was the radio playing. Compliance carried the single-handed watch; physics carried the rock. And the post-event disposition is itself a signal: if the outcome is "the watchkeeper was educated," normalization has already filed the case.

Invisible maintenance forms a spectrum in this fleet: a non-OEM bearing wearing early, a seawater line aging through, an unbraced suction hose — spares quality, component life, installation craft. The structural constraint is small-owner economics: maintenance is exquisitely sensitive to freight rates. The honest metric is blind-spot rate — the share of actual failure points lying outside classification-survey coverage. The broken drag chain completes the spectrum: survey covers the principal structures; deck gear rides on crew self-checks, and the failure lands exactly in the fatigue band of self-checks.

Redundancy's illusion wears a rescue uniform here: the emergency-response vessel whose crew entered an oxygen-depleted chain locker three times. The procedure existed; the rescue scenario exempted it. The local check is entry-permit compliance specifically in rescue scenarios — the moment the permit system is most needed is precisely when it goes absent.
