# Public Policy and Administration | po

> This page has five sections: record quality, feedback delay, coupling, the three deadliest mechanisms, and what outsiders most easily mislearn.

## 1 Record Quality

The public reports of audit institutions are this domain's distinctive wealth. The United States' GAO and the United Kingdom's NAO run year-by-year, cycle-by-cycle tracking audits of government project failure (multiple reports across years for the same project; HS2 and the VA electronic health record each have multiple installments in this book's library), and China's National Audit Office reports its annual audit work to the National People's Congress in the name of the State Council and publishes the PDF (po-008). The value form of an audit report differs from that of other investigations: what it records is not a single-point accident but the year-by-year deviation of plan from execution — the public-sector version of feedback delay. Royal commissions and committees of inquiry (the deep postmortems of major administrative failures) are scattered across countries. Overall assessment: strong on tracking, weak on narrative; the right way to read this domain is to read the complete set for one project and align it by year.

## 2 Feedback Delay

The longest delays of any domain: policy feedback is measured in decades (po-007's rail program, more than a decade without landing); project-governance feedback in years (the GAO's yearly comparisons); fiscal-execution feedback in years (the audit office's annual report). The institutional consequence of the delay: a decision-maker's tenure (Chapter 28) is naturally shorter than the policy consequence cycle, and cross-period debt — maintenance arrears, aging populations and assets, promised obligations — has no line item in the budget table (the Westminster refurbishment arrears of po-005 are one form of it).

## 3 Coupling

Within a project, coupling is moderate to low (work can be phased; segments can be stopped); between policies, it is high: the same object is acted on by successive governments, multiple departments, and multiple layers of governance, and the cost of a scope change is absorbed by the administrations that follow (po-007). Cross-domain coupling becomes visible in a crisis: the costs of public project failure — interrupted services, fiscal loss — fall on an unselected public, which makes failure in the public domain a political event by nature, and the pressure of narrative before evidence strongest by nature.

## 4 The Three Deadliest Mechanisms

Escalation of commitment (Chapter 27): HS2's two resets and the cancellation of Phase 2 (po-004; the £46.8 billion already spent serving as the reason to continue). Goal displacement (Chapter 12): an IT dashboard's risk ratings diverging from actual risk (po-003); conceptual schedules supplied to the oversight body (po-002). Diffusion of responsibility (Chapter 18): the dispersion of authority in a refurbishment program that went thirteen years without a decided scheme (po-005); scope swinging across administrations with nobody answerable for delivery (po-007). Next most frequent: the invisibility of maintenance (po-005); information attenuation through hierarchy (po-008's "over-tax" incentives and information structure).

## 5 What Outsiders Most Easily Mislearn

Mislearning one: reading a single audit report as event news. The value of GAO and NAO reports lies in the series: only by reading five years of reports on one project in sequence can you see metric drift and the accumulation of arrears; reading only the latest is reading a checkup sheet without the medical history. Mislearning two: attributing project failure to "government inefficiency." Audit reports attribute structurally — governance structure, measurement conventions, incentives — and the mechanisms that are isomorphic across public and private sectors (Chapters 12, 17, 27) merely change ledgers in the public domain. Mislearning three: underestimating the deterrent value of public audit. Cycle-by-cycle tracking, plus publication of the rate at which recommendations are implemented (Chapter 19), is one of the few operable cross-period accountability devices the public domain possesses.

## 8 Mechanism Variations in This Domain

Horizon mismatch at the book's largest scale: electoral cycles in years, consequence cycles in generations. The local artifacts are all documentary: does a whole-life cost estimate exist at sanction (the 138-untied-systems case had none), and who triggers a re-baseline (the rail project reset twice without an owner for the decision). The devices are constitutional in ambition — cross-term project contracts in legislation — and the design premise is honest pessimism: assume the contract gets torn; write exit terms anyway.

Escalation wears a briefcase here: "the new delivery model reduces risk" is escalation wearing an innovation label, while the footnote carries "no comparable project has avoided overspend." The fast screen for policy escalation is typographic: where do the decisive facts sit — in the argument or in the footnotes? The external adjudicator exists in this domain (the audit office; the recommendation ledger with public implementation rates — 4 of 18 is the genre) and its implementation rate is the polity's correction speed.

Invisible maintenance becomes constitutional: the repair plan unschemed for thirteen years because future users have no vote and current treasuries have a veto — the invisibility is structural, not managerial. The only working device is status-report compulsion: periodic, public, budget-linked condition assessments. The VA's recommendation tracker shows the genre's mature form: the tracking is the device.
