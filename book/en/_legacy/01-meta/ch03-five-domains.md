# Chapter 3 The Five Fields That Record Failure in Earnest

> What this chapter covers: what failure-recording institutions look like in five fields — aviation, chemicals, maritime, medicine, and nuclear power; the four devices that constitute "in earnest"; and which channels are actually available to readers in China.
> When to turn back: when you want first-hand failure material for your own industry, or need to judge how much to trust an external accident report.
> Estimated reading time: 30 minutes.

The systematization of failure knowledge is work for institutions; voluntary goodwill has never produced it at scale. Only about five fields worldwide have built failure-record systems of any size. They never coordinated with one another, yet the institutions they grew are strikingly similar. This chapter first extracts the four components of that system, then surveys how far each of the five fields has assembled them, and finally lands on the channels available to readers in China.

## 3.1 What "In Earnest" Means: Four Devices

Whether a field's failure-recording system counts as "in earnest" comes down to whether four devices are present at the same time.

Device one: mandatory reporting. A clear legal basis must define which events carry a reporting obligation and what consequences follow when reports are withheld. Where no mandatory duty exists, reporting rates fluctuate with human willingness, and the sample loses meaning.

Device two: independent investigation. The investigating body must be independent of the party under investigation and independent of the regulatory-enforcement apparatus. An investigation's output is "what happened and why"; assigning blame lies outside its mandate. Merge the two functions and the investigated party will trade silence for safety.

Device three: public full texts. Investigation reports must be available in full, under a numbering system, citable. A field that produces press releases with no full texts has headlines and no body copy.

Device four: liability separation. Report content must, as a principle, be kept out of individual disciplinary proceedings, and statements by those involved must face no threat of criminal prosecution (gross negligence excepted). Without this device, devices one through three all fail: nobody writes the details that would put them in prison into a report.

The four devices interlock. Remove the second and the fourth loses its institutional carrier; remove the third and the output of the first two cannot circulate; remove the first and the sample facing the other three is defective at the source. Now consider how the five fields have assembled the set.

## 3.2 Aviation

Aviation is the prototype of this system. At the international level, ICAO Annex 13 (Aircraft Accident and Incident Investigation) established three principles: the state of registry and the state of occurrence carry the obligation to investigate; investigation is separated from judicial liability, and investigation reports serve no purpose of apportioning blame; reports should be published promptly. Major countries established independent investigation agencies under Annex 13, producing a steady output: the NTSB in the United States, the AAIB in the United Kingdom, the BEA in France, the JTSB in Japan, the BFU in Germany, the ANSV in Italy, the AESA in Spain (with the CIAIAC under it), the ATSB in Australia, and the TSB in Canada. Full reports are public and numbered (for example NTSB AAR-10/01, AAIB AAR 1/2023); major investigations open a public docket, with physical evidence, interview records, and transcripts of communications itemized by number.

China's civil aviation operates within the international framework. The Civil Aviation Administration of China organizes accident investigations and publishes investigation reports under the Annex 13 framework; major accidents (such as the March 21, 2022 crash of China Eastern flight MU5735, in which all 123 passengers and 9 crew members died; the CAAC issued an investigation progress bulletin on March 20, 2024) are made public as progress bulletins, and the pace and completeness of the final report is something readers can verify themselves in the case library (see the av-038 entry in Appendix B).

Another output of the aviation system is often overlooked: follow-up tracking of reports. Every NTSB safety recommendation carries a numbered entry on the books, recipients must respond in writing, and whether it was adopted is a matter of public record. Failure knowledge includes not only "the analysis" but also a ledger of "what changed after the analysis".

The cost of the aviation system is on the record as well: investigation cycles run long. For the January 2, 2024 collision between a Japan Airlines A350 and a Japan Coast Guard aircraft at Tokyo Haneda Airport (five dead aboard the Coast Guard aircraft and its captain seriously injured; all 379 aboard the airliner evacuated, one passenger seriously injured and four lightly injured; JTSB Interim Report No. 2, released December 25, 2025), the JTSB still had no final report when that interim report appeared — nearly two years had elapsed (av-037, Appendix B). Deep investigations are counted in years; that is the price of "in earnest".

## 3.3 Chemicals and Process Industries

In the United States, the chemical sector's investigative device is the U.S. Chemical Safety and Hazard Investigation Board (CSB), established under the Clean Air Act Amendments of 1990, independent of the enforcement agencies OSHA and EPA, and doing accident investigation only, with no power to penalize. (the CSB began actual operations in 1998; verified against the About page of its official website) The CSB's output has two parts: full investigation reports (including animated reconstructions of events, a public-facing practice rare among investigation agencies) and execution tracking of safety recommendations.

The institutional difference between chemicals and aviation lies in coverage: aviation's investigative body has jurisdiction over every major accident in its industry, while chemical investigation resources concentrate on the most serious accidents; small and mid-sized leaks and near misses rely mainly on internal company management and local regulatory records. The UK's HSE system and continental Europe's devices are relatively complete; in most developing countries, chemical accident records depend on media coverage and full texts are scarce. In China, chemical accident investigation reports are issued by the Ministry of Emergency Management and provincial and municipal emergency management departments; major accidents have public investigation reports, while small and mid-sized ones mostly appear as bulletins, with uneven completeness (Chapter 31 and Appendix C give the channel details).

## 3.4 Maritime and Transport

Maritime failure recording combines the rule framework of the International Maritime Organization (IMO) with national accident investigation bodies. The UK's MAIB is the model agency: full public reports, independent of maritime justice, its investigations aimed at safety improvement rather than fault finding. The system's peculiarity is fragmented jurisdiction: whichever flag a ship flies, that state leads the investigation; flag state, coastal state, and the crew's nationality state may belong to different agencies, and report quality fluctuates with the flag state's institutional level. The same accident, under a different flag, might have no public full text at all. This feature warns the reader: in the maritime domain, "no report" frequently tells you nothing about "no accident".

## 3.5 Medicine and Medical Devices

Medicine's device takes a different form: the units it records are adverse events and adverse reactions, with the accident playing almost no role. The U.S. FDA's MAUDE database collects medical device adverse event reports, with mandatory reporting duties on manufacturers and user facilities; on the drug side the parallel systems are the FDA's Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) and the EU's pharmacovigilance framework; China's counterpart is the National Medical Products Administration's (NMPA) bulletins, recall announcements, and the drug adverse reaction monitoring system. 〔Verification pending: the NMPA website loads dynamically via JavaScript; annual-report figures are to be checked once access is in place; this sentence is a qualitative statement〕

How the four devices stand in medicine: reporting is mandatory (device one present); the investigation side has no aviation-style independent accident investigation body (device two absent — post-market risk "investigation" is replaced by signal detection and safety updates); publication is half-present (device three partial — databases are queryable, but the quality of individual records is uneven); liability separation is present (device four present — the device adverse event reporting law states explicitly that reports are not to be used in liability determinations; see the language of 21 CFR Part 803, with manufacturer reporting duties at 21 CFR 803.50, verified against the current eCFR; user-facility reporting has its own separate provision in the same part).

This structure gives medical data a distinctive skew: reporting rates fluctuate with how recent an event is and how hard the causal link is to judge, and the same adverse event may be reported several times through several channels or missed entirely. Beyond Chapter 2's threshold checks, using such data adds one more rule: treat medical data as "signals"; to use them as "evidence" requires additional epidemiological verification.

## 3.6 Nuclear Power and Energy

Nuclear power is the field with the deepest international sharing. The International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) and the OECD Nuclear Energy Agency jointly operate the Incident Reporting System (IRS); nuclear operators in member states report operating events, and the database is shared among member states; the U.S. NRC requires licensed operators to submit operating event reports (regulatory location: 10 CFR 50.73, "Licensee event report system"; verified against the current eCFR); the World Association of Nuclear Operators (WANO) runs another reporting and assessment system inside the industry, closed to outsiders. Nuclear events additionally carry the International Nuclear Event Scale (INES), with public ratings from 0 to 7.

The weak point of nuclear's device set sits on the publication side: IRS detailed reports are for member states only, WANO is fully closed-source, and in the open world what you can obtain is the documents each country's regulator publishes on its own. This is the first sample of "institutionally serious but externally closed"; the depth a reader can reach depends on how open the regulator of the reader's own country is.

## 3.7 Final Assembly of the Four Devices

| Field | Mandatory reporting | Independent investigation | Public full texts | Liability separation | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Aviation | Full | Full | Full | Full (Annex 13 principles) | System prototype; the most complete tracking ledger |
| Chemicals | Medium (major accidents) | Independent in the US (CSB); uneven elsewhere | Medium | Medium | Small and mid-sized accidents depend on internal company records |
| Maritime | Full | Split (follows flag state) | Medium | Full | Fragmented jurisdiction |
| Medicine and devices | Full (adverse events) | Absent (replaced by signal detection) | Medium (uneven quality of individual database records) | Full | Read the data as signals |
| Nuclear power | Full | Full | Split (international systems closed; openness of national regulatory documents varies) | Full | External publication is the weak point |

How to read this table: find the row for your own industry (or the nearest neighbor); the gaps in that row are exactly the discount to apply when you read external cases. If you work in chemicals, major accidents have CSB-grade full texts to read, while small and mid-sized accidents have no public sample — when you study those, you reason by analogy from aviation or maritime, and how heavily to discount that analogy is yours to mark.

## 3.8 Where China Stands

Readers in China have five channels for failure records, with clearly differing availability and quality.

Channel one: accident investigation reports. Under the Regulations on the Reporting, Investigation and Handling of Production Safety Accidents (State Council Decree No. 493), serious and particularly serious accidents are investigated by provincial governments or the State Council. Investigation reports for major chemical and mining accidents are published by the Ministry of Emergency Management and provincial departments, with uneven completeness. Against the four devices: mandatory reporting present; independent investigation weak (investigation teams are led by government and staffed across departments, with investigators and those responsible for accountability heavily overlapping); publication incomplete (many bulletins, few full texts); liability separation weak (report and accountability run as one process, and an accountability-first workflow steers statements toward self-protection). 〔Verification pending: the original State Council gazette, Article 19 onward, needs checking〕

Channel two: civil aviation. The CAAC operates under the ICAO Annex 13 framework — of the five channels, the one that meshes best with the four devices; the MU5735 progress bulletin came through it.

Channel three: drugs and devices. NMPA recall announcements, bulletins, and annual reports on drug adverse reactions are standardized public outlets; the depth of any single record is limited, but retrieval works.

Channel four: court judgments. China Judgements Online once offered the world's largest public corpus of court judgments; tort, product liability, and labor dispute documents contain rich detail on organizational failure. Availability has shifted in recent years 〔Verification pending: the Supreme People's Court's annual work reports and judicial statistics need checking; this sentence is a qualitative statement〕; retrieval strategies are in Appendix C.

Channel five: exchanges and regulatory documents. CSRC administrative penalty decisions, exchange inquiry letters and regulatory letters, and audit reports cover financial and governance failures at listed companies — full texts, public, uniformly formatted.

Stacking the five channels: readers in China have usable full-text channels in three areas — civil aviation, drugs, and listed-company compliance; in chemicals, public safety, and other areas, full texts are scarce, with bulletins and second-hand press accounts carrying the load, pushing the overall grade of learning material below B (grade definitions in Chapter 10). This structure determines the distribution of evidence grades among this book's China cases, and explains why most of its China cases are tagged "reconstructed".

## 3.9 Putting the Map to Work

Three moves. First, locate the nearest primary database for your own industry: skip the search engine and go to specific databases with report-numbering schemes (Appendix C lists entry points by field). Second, read three recent full-text reports from your industry and note which of the four devices are present and which are missing — the missing one is the extra caution to carry while reading them. Third, if none of the five fields resembles your industry, then when you reason by analogy from a neighboring one, first run Chapter 2's three sample checks, and only then decide how strong the analogy may be.

## Self-Check: Five Questions

1. Of the four devices, which is missing from your field's accident-reporting system? Last year, how much detail did that missing device cost you in a specific case?
2. Your industry's most recent major failure: at what URL does the full investigation report live? How many minutes do you need to find it?
3. Beyond regulatory inspections, does your organization have its own failure-recording system? How would you estimate its reporting rate?
4. If only one could be kept — regulatory reporting or internal postmortems — which would your organization keep? What follows from that choice?
5. Of China's five channels, how many have you used? For the unused ones: did you never find the entrance, or did you search and come away empty?
