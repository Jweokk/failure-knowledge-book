# Chapter 2 Three Biases: Recording, Survivorship, Attribution

> What this chapter covers: how the failure samples you are handed have been filtered three times — by institutions, by human nature, and by narrative — and the three specific things you will learn wrongly if you study from filtered samples.
> When to turn back: when you cite a case to support a decision, when you design a review process, when you see figures like "industry accident rate".
> Estimated reading time: 35 minutes.

Before reading any failure case, confirm one thing: the sample in your hands was never drawn from the full population of failures. It passed through three filters, and each filter systematically favors certain kinds of failure. The three filters operate at the recording stage, the storytelling stage, and the attribution stage. This chapter takes them apart one at a time, and after each asks the same question: if you learn from this filtered sample, what exactly will you learn wrong?

## 2.1 The Recording Bias: Filing Thresholds Pre-select Your Sample

For a failure to leave a written trace, it must first clear a threshold. The threshold is set by institutions: at what level of severity an event gets investigated, written up, archived. Failures below the threshold, however numerous, do not exist in the written world.

Aviation has the lowest recording threshold and the most complete coverage of any industry, which makes it a convenient yardstick. The U.S. National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) investigates every accident involving scheduled passenger air carriers (operators subject to 14 CFR Part 121); for general aviation accidents (private light aircraft, flying clubs, agricultural and forestry operations), it dispatches on-scene investigation teams to only a portion, completing the rest in writing. 〔Verification pending: on-scene investigation coverage rates need to be checked against the annual NTSB Annual Review volumes; this sentence is a qualitative statement〕 In other words, even in the field with the most complete recording system, the reports you read concentrate on the events with the gravest consequences, the most heavily used aircraft types, or those that happen to carry typical significance. Every one of the forty aviation cases in this book's case library qualified for formal investigation: that is the shape of the threshold.

China's recording system offers another yardstick. The Regulations on the Reporting, Investigation and Handling of Production Safety Accidents (State Council Decree No. 493, 2007) classify accidents into four levels — general, relatively serious, serious, and particularly serious — by deaths, injuries, and direct economic losses, with each level investigated by a different tier of government. The grading itself is a reasonable allocation of resources, but it doubles as a filtering net: investigative capacity follows the grading, and the industry's vast numbers of events below the serious-injury line, equipment-damage events, and near misses have almost no public written existence. Civil aviation is the exception: the Civil Aviation Administration of China (CAAC) publishes investigation reports and progress bulletins under the framework of International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) Annex 13, making it one of the few domains with a full-text outlet. 〔Verification pending: the current text of the Civil Aircraft Incident Investigation Regulations (CCAR-395) needs to be checked〕

The shape of the threshold can also be read from inside the records themselves. The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission's recall announcement for Cub Cadet commercial lawn mowers (Appendix B mn-010) records that the recall came only after 106 reports of fuel leakage had accumulated, one of them involving a fire — the records existed, but institutional pacing kept them lagging behind the risk. Set this against the timeline of a foodborne outbreak investigation (Appendix B ag-001: 33 days from first illness onset to production halt and recall) and the differing threshold heights across industries in the same period become obvious. Before reading any "accident rate", ask where its threshold sits.

The absence of records below the threshold does not mean the absence of information. The information exists in another form: experience carried in practitioners' bodies. A veteran aircraft mechanic knows which line "has a temper"; a nurse knows which combination of orders "tends to go wrong". This knowledge walks out the door with staff turnover and resets to zero at retirement, because it has never touched paper. The handover gap in Chapter 30 is about how this sub-threshold knowledge disappears.

What you will learn wrong: First, you will treat recording frequency as occurrence frequency. "We never hear of such accidents in our industry" often really means "our industry does not record such accidents". Second, you will treat recording quality as safety level. Aviation looks like the industry with the most thorough accident analysis because it has the best recording system; the two facts are correlated yet point in different directions — a field that starts producing large volumes of failure analysis may be becoming safer, or may simply have just cleared its filing threshold. Third, you will systematically ignore near misses. Retrospective statistics show that behind every major accident stand large numbers of near-miss precursors; precursors that never cross the threshold never enter the sample, and so never appear in learning materials.

## 2.2 The Survivorship Bias: The Storytellers Have Been Filtered

The threshold filters events; the survivorship bias filters storytellers. Failures structurally fail to leave narrators: the failed lose the position, the platform, and the credibility a story requires. Degree of failure runs inversely against willingness to tell — the more complete the demise, the fewer the tellers. The failure narratives in circulation are therefore supplied mostly by people doing reasonably well.

Commercial publishing amplifies this filter. The failure chapter of a CEO memoir is written by someone now on stage recalling past peril; the people who actually executed the erroneous decision and paid for it with their jobs usually hold no book contract. Interview samples work the same way: founders willing to sit for a failure postmortem are those with the energy and the social inclination left after failing. The U.S. data platform CB Insights compiles startup failure analyses from postmortems voluntarily submitted by founders — a self-reporting sample in which the more painful the case, the higher the probability of its absence. The software industry offers a more precise observation window, because it has a tradition of the public postmortem; Chapter 4 analyzes this exception in its own right. The point here: companies that write public postmortems are companies that survived the outage and still have an engineering team to write documentation. A company that collapsed over its architecture publishes no postmortem of its collapse; its architecture documents go offline with its servers. So even in this most transparent industry, the collection of public postmortems remains a survivor collection: it covers "fell ill but recovered" cases and never "died of the illness" cases. The mechanisms behind the two may be entirely different — the first an immune-system problem, the second organ failure — and you can only learn from the former.

What you will learn wrong: First, the catalogue of deaths systematically omits the most lethal deaths. Fast, catastrophic organizational deaths (cash running out, the core team leaving en masse, a regulatory veto) are precisely the ones with no time to write a postmortem and the highest shame quotient; they therefore appear least often in circulating failure knowledge and carry the least weight in your mind. Second, you will misjudge an industry's transparency. Industries full of postmortem writing may simply be industries full of healthy companies; postmortem culture and the death rate are two separate variables. Third, you will learn risk avoidance from survivors. A survivor's account of "what we did right back then" is methodologically equivalent to a lottery winner's tips on picking numbers; the correct use is to extract from survivor narratives the information about "what was visible at the time" and to discard the attribution about "why we won".

## 2.3 The Attribution Bias: Every Postmortem Is Written After the Ending

The first two biases determine which cases reach you; the attribution bias determines what shape the telling takes. Every postmortem is written after the outcome is known. When someone who knows the ending retells the process, narrative reshapes the causal chain: chance is braided into necessity, forks are smoothed over, the ambiguity of the moment is compressed into clear error or clear correctness.

The Space Shuttle Challenger accident provides the most thoroughly studied sample. At 11:38 a.m. Eastern Time on January 28, 1986, Challenger lifted off from Cape Canaveral, Florida, broke apart roughly 73 seconds into flight, and all 7 astronauts aboard died (Rogers Commission report, Report to the President, Washington, June 6, 1986; Volume 1, Chapter 3 opens: "Flight of the Space Shuttle Challenger on Mission 51-L began at 11:38 a.m. Eastern Standard Time on January 28, 1986," and records that the flight "ended 73 seconds later in an explosive burn of hydrogen and oxygen propellants"; official full text at nasa.gov/history/rogersrep/v1ch3.htm, read and verified for this book, November 1, 2026). The night before launch, on the evening of January 27, 1986, engineers at the contractor Morton Thiokol recommended against launching in cold temperatures on a teleconference call, citing O-ring erosion found in solid rocket booster joints recovered from previous flights and evidence linking low temperature to seal failure; company management then reversed this position and approved the launch. In the meeting where the position changed, one sentence entered the investigative record: take off your engineer's hat and put on your management hat (Rogers Commission report, Volume 1, Chapter 5, "The Contributing Cause of the Accident," recording Arnie Thompson's and Roger Boisjoly's testimony of the off-net caucus: Boisjoly testified that Jerry Mason "turned to Bob Lund and asked him to take off his engineering hat and put on his management hat"; official full text at nasa.gov/history/rogersrep/v1ch5.htm, read and verified for this book, November 1, 2026).

Once the ending is known, attribution can stack many layers. The first layer is physical: cold reduced O-ring resilience, and combustion gases burned through the seal. This layer is entirely sound; it is the physical mechanism the Rogers Commission itself identified. The second layer is organizational: Diane Vaughan's The Challenger Launch Decision (University of Chicago Press, 1996) explained that the company and its regulator had launched repeatedly with signs of O-ring erosion and succeeded, with each success written down as "this deviation is within acceptable limits"; the decision standard drifted with each success, and the decision on the evening of January 27 was one routine slide along that drifting trajectory. This layer is also sound.

The two layers do not contradict each other, yet they train completely different readers. A reader who absorbs only the physical attribution learns "cold is dangerous" and returns to his organization to keep installing gates on single parameters like "temperature". A reader who absorbs the organizational attribution goes back to audit his own logs: do we have some deviation indicator we have been running successfully for years, each occurrence explained away as "the margin still holds"? The same case yields different learning depending on the layer of attribution — and which layer you receive on the market depends on who does the telling: accident bulletins lean toward the physical layer, management scholars toward the organizational layer, memoirists toward the luck layer.

Attribution bias also has an institutionalized form that deserves honest acknowledgment: the investigation report itself is retrospective writing. Investigators know the ending, and procedure requires them to exhaust hypotheses before converging, yet narrative gravity is always present. The counterweights are procedural: numbered evidence, public dockets, multi-hypothesis screening, conclusions annotated item by item with evidence grades. NTSB reports attach an evidence chain to every conclusion, so readers can dispute the conclusion while checking the reasoning — the fundamental reason they are more reliable than journalistic reconstructions. Residual bias can never be zeroed out; it can only be compressed.

What you will learn wrong: First, you will overestimate "they should have seen it coming". Playing the film backward from the ending, every fork is clearly visible; standing at the decision point, information arrives mixed with noise. Second, you will fail to develop in-the-moment judgment. Reading finished attributions is like studying games others have already played — your own play never improves; this is why every case in this book stops at the decision point (the third contract in the preface). Third, you will flatten complex failure into a single cause. Retrospective attribution tends to converge on one "root cause", while real failure chains usually span technology, organization, and incentives (the failure chain model of Chapter 9). The flattened "root cause" suits reporting; it is useless for prevention.

## 2.4 With All Three Biases Stacked, What Do You Actually Hold

All three biases act on every case simultaneously. Take aviation for a full physical examination — it is the industry where all three biases weigh least: recording has a mandatory system, telling has a statutory body, attribution has procedural discipline. Even so, residues remain: near misses largely absent (recording bias), public postmortems written by surviving institutions (survivorship bias), the causal convergence structure of reports (attribution bias). Other industries carry more skew, never less: chemicals have a filing system but coverage concentrates on major accidents; software has a postmortem culture but its sample consists entirely of survivors; healthcare event reporting systems run at low voluntary participation in every country; finance's failure records lie scattered across enforcement documents and litigation, with essentially no industry-level compilation.

Here is an operational sample-inspection routine; run any case through three checks before reading:

1. Check the threshold: how did this case originally enter the record — voluntary report, mandatory investigation, or media exposure? The threshold tells you which consequence tier it belongs to.
2. Count the autopsies: what stake does the narrator hold in the outcome? Does the institution still exist? Was the narrator at the time a decision-maker, an executor, or a bystander?
3. Take apart the attribution: does the narrative mark the boundary between "visible at the time" and "reconstructed afterward"? With an unmarked postmortem, default to reading the whole text as hindsight.

Run these three checks and your trust in a case acquires a scale. Fields 6 (visible at the time) and 12 (evidence grade) of the case cards throughout this book perform these three checks for you in advance, on the writing side.

## Self-Check: Five Questions

1. What is the accident-recording threshold in your industry? In the past year, how many events crossed it, and how many did not cross it although you knew of them?
2. Of the failure cases you have cited, how many can be traced to a primary full text? Count only the ones for which you can name the document and its number.
3. Of the postmortems your organization produced last year, how many distinguished "information visible at the time" from "information known only afterward"?
4. In your industry, who usually discusses failure publicly — winners or losers? Can you estimate the order of magnitude of the industry's "autopsy rate"?
5. Take your team's most recent postmortem and rewrite one of its causal conclusions in a "visible at the time" version. Does the conclusion still stand?
