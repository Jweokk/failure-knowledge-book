# Chapter 1: Why Failure Is the Most Expensive Knowledge — and the Least Organised

> What this chapter covers: the price and supply of failure knowledge, and the gap between "having heard of a way to die" and "knowing a way to die."
> When to return: when you need to persuade someone (or yourself) to invest time and budget in organising failure data; when you receive a postmortem and need to judge whether it is worth reading.
> Reading time: 30 minutes.

## 1.1 Eliminative Knowledge

An organisation makes structurally similar decisions every day. Invest or not, ship or hold, sign or refuse, staff or leave short-staffed. Every decision selects one option and discards the rest. The only function knowledge serves in a decision is narrowing the search space.

Narrowing comes in two forms. A success case says "this path works" — it validates one point in the set. A failure case says "this region contains a lethal path" — it eliminates an entire area. The same unit of information eliminates more when it comes from failure.

This is sharper in repeated-decision contexts, because organisations do not make a decision once; they make structurally similar decisions daily. The expected value of a negative sample equals the number of wrong paths it eliminates multiplied by the expected loss per path. Loss distributions are heavy-tailed: most errors cause absorbable damage; a few erase the organisation. Those few concentrate on a handful of paths — precisely the ones that accident investigation agencies write about year after year.

Failure knowledge therefore dominates on two properties simultaneously: it eliminates more area, and it concentrates on the largest losses. Together these justify the chapter's central claim: per record, failure records carry higher informational value than success records.

That claim has a premise, which is what this chapter must establish: "failure knowledge" here means organised failure records. Unorganised failure does not even qualify as knowledge. That threshold comes next.

## 1.2 Is "I Know a Company That Died This Way" Knowledge?

The failure knowledge circulating at dinner tables and in meeting rooms usually looks like this: "I know a company that died exactly this way." The holder may genuinely have watched a company collapse; the information itself is not false. But as knowledge it has three gaps.

Gap one: the fact layer. The statement cannot be verified. Which company, what year, based on what document, did an investigation report exist — all absent. The listener can only choose to believe or not. Knowledge's first requirement is verifiability: a claim that a third party can check against source material. Without this layer it degrades to anecdote, and anecdote cannot participate in decisions — only in atmosphere.

Gap two: the condition layer. The statement has no boundaries. It does not specify that company's scale, regulatory density, cash structure, market stage; you therefore cannot know whether this death mode applies to your project. All failure conclusions have scopes of validity, and applying them beyond scope actively manufactures accidents: taking "internet companies must burn cash for scale" into a cash-flow business, or "manufacturing must be asset-heavy" into a contract-manufacturing model. Condition-layer-free failure knowledge sounds more profound the more it hurts, because it encourages direct conclusion transfer.

Gap three: the structure layer. The statement cannot be retrieved, accumulated, or cited. Three months later you will not remember the full version; a colleague who needs it cannot get the source from you; it cannot merge with other information into a pattern. For knowledge to participate in organisational decisions it must enter a system: searchable, comparable, citable, refutable. All four verbs require structure.

Compare what a well-organised failure record looks like. A US National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) formal aviation accident report has four fixed sections: factual information, analysis, findings, and recommendations. The factual section carries a complete timeline where every statement traces to docket material — physical evidence, recorded communications, interview transcripts. The findings section lists each finding, tagged as contributory or observational. Recommendations are numbered, and recipients must respond in writing within 90 days. A reader can disagree with the analysis but every step of verification has a path. That is what it means to be fielded: fact, condition, and structure layers all present.

Chapter 5 compresses this fielding into a twelve-field case card. Here the only requirement is a standard: before discussing failure, check whether it passes the three gates in this section.

## 1.3 The Word "Replicate" Has Misled Everyone

A widespread claim holds that success can be replicated but failure cannot. This directs attention to the wrong place. The accurate version is: success cannot be replicated; failure can be avoided; neither is "replication."

Replicating success requires condition replay. The condition list includes timing, team composition, competitive landscape, regulatory windows, funding environment, and a large quantity of luck that even the participants cannot articulate. Business school cases compress success into a three-part narrative — vision, execution, results — and what gets compressed is exactly what replication requires. Following success cases therefore raises, not lowers, the failure rate; and no one writes your postmortem afterwards (the survivorship bias of Chapter 2).

The reusable portion of failure knowledge is far more stable. A lethal path's requirements on initial conditions are typically broader than a success path's: many different starting states funnel into the same failure chain. Goal displacement, one mechanism, appears with nearly the same structure in hospital wait-time metrics, school exam metrics, and platform content metrics. Success is the opposite: every success path depends on a narrow set of conditions.

Failure knowledge therefore exists naturally as patterns, not cases. Patterns transfer across industries; cases are reference only. This is why Part 3 of this book is organised as twenty-four mechanism chapters, each with four cases from four different industries — a mechanism is defined by its requirement to hold across industries. What appears in only one industry is not a mechanism; it is an industry characteristic, filed in Part 4's domain profiles.

## 1.4 Who Produces Failure Knowledge

If failure records were this valuable, the market should already have produced them. Look at the actual producer list and the problem becomes clear.

Producer one: statutory investigation agencies. Aviation has the NTSB, the UK AAIB, France's BEA, Japan's JTSB; chemicals has the US CSB; nuclear has the IAEA's incident reporting system; maritime has the UK MAIB. Their output is stable, free, and citable — the largest legitimate supply source of failure knowledge. But it exists as legal documents: hundreds of pages, dense technical terminology, serving hearings and litigation, with very low service-to-reading ratio.

Producer two: insurance and reinsurance companies. They hold the largest failure datasets across industries and years, with loss amounts attached. These are underwriting assets — closed. You pay for them indirectly in premiums; you cannot buy them on any open market.

Producer three: judicial and regulatory documents. Judgments, penalty decisions, recall notices — public and growing, with China's judicial document database having once provided the world's largest disclosure surface. Their weakness is fragmentation: each document covers only a slice of one dispute, and no one maintains a cross-document data structure.

Producer four: academia. Vaughan's normalisation of deviance, Perrow's normal accidents, Snook's practical drift, Dekker's drift into failure — Part 3 borrows its mechanism names from this tradition. But academic theory arrives wrapped in cases, cases wrapped in second-hand retellings, and the citation chain back to the original report frequently breaks at a dead link. The theory is reusable; the cases are not verifiable.

Producer five: the organisations themselves. This category produces the least, for the hardest reasons: the legal risk of writing failure details, the competitive intelligence value for rivals, the reputational cost to employees and founders — all three point toward silence. Chapter 4 dismantles each gate.

Placing the five together reveals a mismatch: the largest producer of failure knowledge is a set of documents its largest potential consumer group happens to be unable to read. Investigation agencies have been writing for decades; business schools barely cite them; corporate training ignores them; decision-makers never recall them at the moment of choice. Production is not the bottleneck. Circulation is.

## 1.5 Circulation Breaks at the Last Mile

Take a real NTSB formal report and trace where circulation breaks. It might be a 300-page English PDF, buried on the fourth level of a database, written in airworthiness terminology, with three parallel numbering systems (accident number, recommendation number, docket number). Its information density is extraordinary: every page carries timestamped communications, instrument readings, signed documents. For an engineer making a release decision tomorrow, everything needed is in this file — except three things: they do not know the file exists, they do not know which file matches their situation, and they cannot finish 300 pages.

Three "do not knows" and one "cannot finish" are the total composition of circulation cost: discovery cost, matching cost, reading cost. When these costs exceed a threshold, the effective supply of a public good drops to zero — no matter how free it is in nominal terms.

The circulation cost of success narratives has been industrially compressed: media, biographies, podcasts, business school texts, trending topics — someone pays at every stage. The circulation of failure records has no sponsor: citing an investigation report generates no academic citation, retelling it generates no traffic, and using it requires first understanding it. This is textbook market failure — the commodity is just obscure.

## 1.6 Where This Book Stands

First, look at one piece of failure knowledge that has completed circulation. ValuJet 592 (Appendix B, av-035): the NTSB formal report (AAR-97/06) used one page to pin down the physical origin — a maintenance contractor packed unspent chemical oxygen generators into the cargo hold as ordinary spares. It used several hundred pages to establish the condition layer: the gap in dangerous-goods rules for the category "maintenance residue," the handover habits of the contractor system, the regulator's handling of prior similar warnings. The same pattern (invisibility of maintenance, Chapter 29) can be found in warehousing, chemical processing, and software configuration management. This card's value lies in pinning "how maintenance residue becomes a dangerous good" to a citable number. Circulation engineering aims to replicate this process across hundreds of reports.

This book does not investigate; it engineers circulation, through three actions. Compression: reducing hundreds of pages to a twelve-field case card, preserving the fact layer, condition layer, and structure layer. Matching: hanging cases onto twenty-four mechanisms so a reader starting from their situation (Chapter 35's assessment table) can pull the relevant list of death modes. Delayed conclusion: cases stop at the decision point; the reader judges first, then reads the outcome and the minimal counterfactual.

The third action is the dividing line between this book and retelling-style failure literature. Retelling gives conclusions: so-and-so failed from arrogance, so-and-so succeeded through humility. Conclusions cannot be verified and cannot participate in decisions. This book gives process slices: on a certain date, in a certain room, on a certain document — here was the information, here were the options, here is the action taken. Judgment belongs to the reader; structural analysis is already written. This is the antidote to the attribution bias of Chapter 2.

Circulation engineering has boundaries. Compression necessarily loses information, so every case retains its full citation and link; the reader can follow the number back to the original report. Compression may introduce errors, so every number requires four-part provenance, and anything that cannot be sourced is rewritten as a mechanism description. What this book submits to the reader is a verifiable intermediate layer; its value depends on how easy it is to check, not on how well it reads.

## Self-Check: Five Questions

1. Has your organisation's most recent major loss got a retrievable primary document? Ask three colleagues and compare their answers.
2. The last time you retold "a case of failure," what source document did you read it from? If you cannot name the document, which category in Section 1.2 does that retelling fall into?
3. For the success cases your team references before deciding, can you list three narrow conditions they require? If not, what does that mean?
4. Of the three circulation costs — discovery, matching, reading — which is highest in your organisation? Is anyone lowering it?
5. If you were given a budget specifically to organise your industry's failure knowledge, what would you build first? How does that answer relate to the assessment table in Chapter 35?
