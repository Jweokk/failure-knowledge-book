# Chapter 18: Diffusion of Responsibility

> What this chapter covers: the mechanism by which responsibility thins as signatures multiply; three criteria, four field sites, and the design of "unilateral stop authority" plus a cap on signature counts.
> When to return: when your organisation runs countersigning workflows, forms committees, or splits one matter across three departments.
> Reading time: 50 minutes.

Diffusion of responsibility happens quietly: no one shirks; responsibility is simply averaged away at the moment work is divided. A fraudulent annual report carries four signature lines; a collision on the water involves four categories of stakeholder; a level crossing's warning equipment spans two owners; a helicopter's landing rests on two teams' assumptions. Looked at afterwards, every party signed, performed its segment, and can state precisely what it did. No link in the chain is in charge, and every link sincerely believes "someone else has it." This chapter is about that "someone else."

## 1 What It Is

Definition: diffusion of responsibility is the psychological and structural phenomenon by which responsibility dilutes as the number of participants grows. The structural face is called dispersal of responsibility: staged approvals, multi-party signatures, committee decisions — each additional gate is meant to add a line of defence, but its actual effect is to add one more contributor to the assumption that "someone else will check." The psychological face is the workplace version of the bystander effect: the more parties involved, the higher the psychological cost of unilateral intervention and the greater the social risk of "making a fuss over nothing." Stack the two layers and no link in the chain is in charge.

Three trigger criteria (if any one appears, the mechanism has already started):

① Many participants, and the responsibility interfaces between them are not delimited in writing;
② Every party can state precisely what it did, and no party can state who is responsible for the whole;
③ The organisation has no unilateral stop authority anywhere.

The self-test for criterion ② is cruel but effective: after an incident, the organisation's first reflex is "find out who signed," and the signature chain holds seven names. Seven signatures mean this step was confirmed seven times, which means no single confirmation was necessary.

## 2 How It Grows

Three generating conditions. Condition one: an institutional tradition of multi-party signatures and staged approvals. Signing is meant to add defence, but the cost structure of signing determines its behavioural consequence: the benefit of checking each prior signature accrues to the chain, while the cost accrues to the signer — so a rational signer checks only his own cell. Defences stack nominally and run in parallel behaviourally. Condition two: layered outsourcing and subcontracting. Each subcontracting tier lowers the visibility of risk by one grade and weakens the responsibility link by one grade; where subcontract terms cover service clauses but not risk clauses, only a commercial relationship remains between tiers. Condition three: committee decisions. The resolution belongs to the collective, execution requires assignment, and supervision has no post; whatever a collective passes has no individual claimant by nature, and the credential of "having participated in the decision" makes each person feel they have already done their duty.

The tragedy of dilution is that it is asymptomatic in ordinary times: every link signs, approves, and meets, and the system looks deeply defended (the responsibility-layer projection of Chapter 22's illusion of redundancy). The symptom appears only at the moment someone must call a stop: at that moment, seven signers equal zero signers.

## 3 The Mechanism in Four Domains

### Case 1 (Maritime): A VTS Where Nobody Knew the Limits of Their Own Authority

ma-008. The bulk carrier Polesie and the general cargo ship Verity collided on 24 October 2023 inside the traffic separation scheme of the German Bight; Verity sank and five people died. The MAIB report (Report 5/2026, reviewed for this book): the two vessels held divergent assumptions about the give-way obligation and neither acted — the fishing vessel assumed the larger ship would give way, as convention held, while the larger ship acted under its own navigational constraints. Meanwhile, neither the VTS (vessel traffic service) operator nor the supervisor was clear about the boundaries of their own authority: how far a warning should go, whether it could be made mandatory, and who bore responsibility if enforcement failed — nobody could say. Monitored and monitor, operator and supervisor: four parties formed a scene where responsibility had no edges. Each did "what should be done" under its own understanding, and none held — or considered itself to hold — the power to force a change.

Stop at the decision point: the two ships converging, the final minutes at the VTS watch station. On the table: two radar tracks, vague clauses of authority in the rules, and a question the institution had never answered — if I can see what they cannot, what may I do, and what must I do. 〔Stop point: reader judges first, then turns the page〕〔Outcome〕Verity sank too fast for the crew to don lifejackets and abandon ship. Primary source: MAIB, Report 5/2026, 2026; link in Appendix B, ma-008.

### Case 2 (Healthcare): The Incompatibility Only the Registry Knew

me-008. Joint replacement surgery in the English NHS. HSIB report (Implantation of wrong prostheses during joint replacement surgery, 2018; archived snapshot reviewed for this book): during surgery, a 62-year-old patient was implanted with prostheses from different manufacturers that were incompatible with each other; the defect surfaced days later through data matching at the national joint registry. Surgical team, instrument supply, pre-operative checking, registry entry — the person at each link could explain how their step followed that link's process: the operating table took instruments per the instrument list, the supplier shipped per the order, the check ran per the checklist. No link owned full responsibility for "whether the two components match each other," because matchability straddled the gap between four links — and gaps do not sign.

Stop at the decision point: the instant between the implantation of the first component and the second on the operating table. On the table: instruments each bearing their own label, process records verified correct at each link, and a registry nobody thought to query on the spot. 〔Stop point: reader judges first, then turns the page〕〔Outcome〕The patient underwent revision surgery; the report highlighted the registry's detect-after-the-fact pattern: detection after the fact means no one was responsible before it. Primary source: HSIB, 2018; link in Appendix B, me-008 (archived link, grade A−).

### Case 3 (Construction): A Governance Interface at a Level Crossing

ce-008. The Staniforth Road level crossing on the Supertram network, Sheffield, 22 June 2025. RAIB report (Report 10/2026, reviewed for this book): a tram struck two pedestrians — one seriously injured, one slightly injured; both were taken to hospital. The pedestrians had not noticed the tram approaching; the audible and visual warnings did not work; risk management between the infrastructure owner and the operator involved at the crossing was weak. The crossing's warning equipment straddled the governance interface between two bodies: the equipment's ownership, maintenance, effectiveness assessment, and actual effect for pedestrians sat on different responsibility lists — and the unlabelled seam between the lists fell exactly on the question "can pedestrians get across safely."

Stop at the decision point: the last time the crossing's warning equipment was assessed under the framing "actual effect for pedestrians." On the table: the equipment's status record (serviceable), the owner's and operator's respective duty documents (complete), and the seam between them that neither had ever signed. 〔Stop point: reader judges first, then turns the page〕〔Outcome〕Two people taken to hospital; the report listed the unclear risk interface as an investigation finding. Primary source: RAIB, Report 10/2026, 2026; link in Appendix B, ce-008.

### Case 4 (Finance): The Ex-Post Cross-Section of a Signature Chain

fi-007. The accountability decision in the Qingyue Technology fraudulent issuance case (〔2026〕No. 59, reviewed for this book): chairman Gao Yudi — warning, RMB 9 million fine, 8-year market ban; executive deputy general manager Mu Xinju — RMB 9 million fine, 6-year ban; chief financial officer and board secretary Zhang Xiaobo — RMB 6 million fine, 5-year ban; supervisory board chairman Wu Lei — RMB 4 million fine, 3-year ban (same case as fi-006). The decision is a cross-section of ex-ante responsibility dilution: the fraudulent offering documents and annual report carried four names, and the four signatures constituted, ex ante, the organisational narrative of "layer upon layer of checks." Ex post, the regulator reconstructed each signature's true degree of knowledge one by one: signature layering was the shape of dilution ex ante and the sequence of accountability ex post.

Stop at the decision point: the process by which the fraudulent content was finalised and the four signatures applied in sequence. On the table: each signature's true degree of knowledge, each document's hand-off path of "it is signed further up," and the half-step by which each signer mentally pushed the gatekeeping duty to the signature before or after. 〔Stop point: reader judges first, then turns the page〕〔Outcome〕Fines totalled RMB 28 million; market bans ran three to eight years. Primary source: China Securities Regulatory Commission, 〔2026〕No. 59, 2026; link in Appendix B, fi-007.

## 4 What Shows Earliest

Six weak signals, each with the reason that usually overrides it.

1. Every box on the process chart has a person; the whole has no owner. Override: "roles are clearly divided."
2. After a problem surfaces, the first meeting convened is a responsibility determination meeting, not a repair meeting. Override: "lessons must be drawn."
3. A committee resolution has no named executor, or the executors are a different set of people from the deciders. Override: "collective decisions are steadier."
4. The outsourcing contract contains service clauses but no risk clauses and no stop clauses. Override: "business is business."
5. Three departments could all own one matter; before the incident nobody owns it, after the incident everyone does. Override: "joint management."
6. "That is not mine to handle" rises in frequency in internal communication. Override: "follow the process."

## 5 How to Defend

Device one: a single-accountable-person clause. For every critical process and every risk point, the institution names one person who carries the responsibility (the person may change; the seat may not sit empty). All other signers carry verification duty, not gatekeeping duty — and verification duty is defined in writing: your signature asserts only that the specific items you verified are true. Had the four signature lines in the Qingyue case specified "who verifies what" ex ante, the regulator would not have had to divide it up for them ex post.

Device two: unilateral stop authority. At least one role holds independent authority to stop critical irreversible actions, written into the job description and the contract (Chapter 7), with the cost of stopping borne by the organisation (the interface with Chapter 27's termination reward). Stop authority that does not land on a specific person is no stop authority at all; stop authority that lands on a person without budget backup is authority no one dares use.

Device three: a governance-interface register. For every object maintained jointly by multiple parties (crossing equipment, shared data, interface systems, matchability verification), build an interface register: each entry names the owner party, the operating party, the assessing party, and the acceptance party, with the four roles barred from collapsing into one party. Once a year, drill against the register: "if this thing breaks, who finds out first" — write the drill's answer into the register, replacing assumptions.

Device four: a cap on signature counts. Any process whose gatekeeping signatures for one action exceed three is force-restructured: gatekeeping with more than three signatures equals no gatekeeping. Extra signatures either convert to informational CC (not constituting gatekeeping) or are deleted; every retained signature must state the specific items it verifies. Restructuring will meet heavy resistance — every deleted signature line will claim to be critical. The handling: have each signature line write down "what I verify"; any line that cannot name its verification items is deleted automatically.

## 6 Boundaries with Adjacent Mechanisms

Against misaligned incentives (Chapter 17): misalignment is a direction problem (the metric points at the wrong target); dilution is an existence problem (no carrier of the direction at all). The criterion question: if you hand the responsibility to one named person, does the situation change? If yes, it was dilution; if handing it over is useless (his metrics force him to act against it), it was misalignment. Colgan 3407 (Case 3 of Chapter 17) has both layers: the regional carrier's economic structure was misalignment; the absent stop authority was dilution.

Against the invisibility of maintenance (Chapter 29): unclaimed maintenance debt often has dilution as its cause. The Westminster restoration (po-005) shows both faces: responsibility scattered across two Houses and multiple bodies is dilution; the debt itself is invisible maintenance. Classify by the principal contradiction: repair the responsibility structure first, and the debt will enter the agenda.

Against information decay down the hierarchy (Chapter 16): decay is vertical distortion; dilution is horizontal dissipation. In organisations with both, bad news neither travels up nor gets claimed sideways: Chapter 16's direct channel and this chapter's single accountable person are a pair of parts that must be installed together.

## 7 Self-Check: Five Questions

1. Which of your organisation's recent gatekeeping processes ran a signature chain longer than three? Can you write down the specific items each signature verified?
2. For the matter you are responsible for, is unilateral stop authority in your hands? Have you exercised it? Who paid the cost of exercising it?
3. Pick one cross-departmental shared object (equipment, data, process) and write its four-party register. If you cannot write down the assessing party and the acceptance party, who owns it right now?
4. In the most recent committee resolution you took part in, was the accountable executor named in the resolution text?
5. After an incident, does your organisation convene the repair meeting or the responsibility meeting first? Where is that order written down?

## 6bis Installing the Devices: Order, Failure Modes, and Cost

The single-responsible-person clause goes first — it is the naming device; dilution's opposite is a name. Stop-work authority follows (the name needs teeth), the signature-count cap third (it blocks dilution by crowd rather than by anonymity), and the governance-interface list last (a contract-layer device with the longest negotiation cycle, so start it early and let it run). First ninety days: week one, name every top-tier risk entry (one name each, into the ledger; the test: when this risk detonates, who is called into the first meeting — no name, no owner); weeks two and three, draft the stop-work clause (who, over what, through what procedure, with what immunity and what recording duty); month two, count the signature nodes on live projects and converge anything beyond five; month three, draft the first governance-interface list for projects with external partners.

Failure modes. Naming goes nominal — a name on the ledger with no resources behind it, a risk noticeboard rather than an owner. Bind resources to the name: the entry's resource field lists the inspection rights, budget veto, and direct escalation path the name can actually exercise. Stop-work authority dies procedurally — the approval chain outruns the hazard — so make it effective-first: stop now, ratify after; reversal requires an affirmative override, never inaction. The signature cap gets gamed by splitting one big signature into five small ones — cluster related signatures on the same matter and count them together (the ledger's linkage field does this automatically). Interface lists signed, then entombed — bind them to the joint meeting's standing first agenda: any change to list items, every session.

Cost: naming and the stop-work clause are clause-level work (drafting plus one legal pass, inside a week); the signature cap is half a day per project; interface lists cost one to two weeks of negotiation per partner. Against the North Sea collision (five dead in a multi-party responsibility vacuum), the fraud cases whose accountability chain frayed into nobody, or a rail project re-scoped for a decade, dilution's price is counted in lives and years, the clauses' in weeks.

Toolchain: the locator triggers on "multi-team plus multi-signature structures"; risk-naming is the opening move. Question set 18's item 1 ("whose contract, which clause") audits the stop-work device. Post-mortems keep the culture thermometer: the first question after an event — "who signed" or "what broke" — and improvement actions install single owners. And the pre-mortem's action-claiming rule (no claimant, becomes escalation) is this chapter's naming device in miniature: a death nobody can claim in the meeting is an ownerless risk entry — it goes to the ledger for a name before it goes anywhere else.
