# Chapter 20: Failure of Success (Sitkin)

> What this chapter covers: how a long accident-free period shrinks defensive investment; three criteria, four field sites, the accounting method that "counts what did not happen," and a system of live testing for defences.
> When to return: when a budget review proposes cutting backups, drills, or inspections that have "gone unused for years"; when you yourself feel a process has become "going through the motions."
> Reading time: 50 minutes.

Every line of defence fights a losing battle for its own existence. The backup generator has not started in ten years; three years of drills have found no problem; the inspection process has never caught anything. On the budget sheet these line items share one name: years without output. Their real output is the things that did not happen, and things that did not happen enter no ledger. This is the problem Sitkin (Lyle Sitkin) identified in the organisational learning literature: success undermines learning. What an organisation learns during its success period is "this way of doing things is fine," not "this way of doing things produces no accidents because the defences are in place." The two learnings differ by a single phrase; a few years later they differ by an entire accident.

## 1 What It Is

Definition: failure of success is the structural tendency for success to undermine learning: during success periods an organisation treats defensive investment (backups, drills, inspections, redundancy) as cost without output and progressively cuts it or lets it hollow into formality; the price of the shrinkage arrives late, and by the time it arrives it can no longer be traced back to the cut. It operates in the time dimension — the organisational-learning version of margin erosion (Chapter 25): that chapter attacks the margin itself, this one attacks the will to maintain margins.

Three trigger criteria (if any one appears, the mechanism has already started):

① A defensive investment has for a long time produced no visible value (no record of ever "catching" anything);
② It is beginning to be treated as redundancy: cut proposals list it as a reason, or its execution has de facto become a ritual;
③ The first accident after the cut or the hollowing falls more than two years out (the causal chain has already broken in time; no one will draw the line).

The two-year figure in criterion ③ deserves a pause: why two years? Because budget cycles are counted in years, personnel turnover in twos, and memory decay in quarters — when the consequence of the cut finally appears in year three, the people who decided it are gone, and the people who inherited the system face a status quo that "has always been this way." Criterion ③ describes not the size of the risk but a structural guarantee that accountability cannot occur.

## 2 How It Grows

Three generating conditions. Condition one: the success period runs long enough. Defences are seen as necessary only while accident memory is fresh; memory decays faster than accidents recur, and that time gap is the growing season of slack. Condition two: the causes of success cannot be attributed. The organisation cannot say how much of "no accident" is the defences' credit and how much is luck (Chapter 11's two hypotheses: the margin held vs. luck held). What cannot be attributed cannot be entered in the books on behalf of defence, and investment with no book entry is waiting to be slaughtered in any budget review. Condition three: budget pressure arrives cyclically. Every year something must be cut, and the safest cut is the line item that "has never produced an accident" — a reason unimpeachable in the meeting: it is true, and it cannot be rebutted, unless the organisation keeps a system of accounts for "did not happen."

The consequence of slack has one precise feature: its punishment falls outside the decision-makers' terms of office (Chapter 28), and by the time it lands the punished party is often no longer the decision-maker. That is why the mechanism replays generation after generation at the organisational level, each generation feeling it is the first.

## 3 The Mechanism in Four Domains

### Case 1 (Construction): Four Years Before Anyone Noticed the Ramp Was Gone

ce-007. London Overground, Norwood Junction station. RAIB report (Report 09/2026, reviewed for this book): the mobile ramp for wheelchair passengers to board and alight had been missing since September 2021; on 1 July 2025 a train departed carrying a ramp that should not have been on board — a dangerous event, with no injuries, thankfully. The report's key sentence must be quoted in full: "The correct ramp had not been available since September 2021 and none of the station checks in the period up to the accident had identified this." Missing for four full years, and the entire run of platform checks in the period failed to notice. During a long accident-free period, checking decays into sign-in: its output is the record of "having checked," no longer the possibility of "having found."

Stop at the decision point: one of the routine platform checks after the ramp went missing. On the table: the checklist box "ramp in place," four years of tick marks, and a question nobody ever asked — what does this tick actually check? 〔Stop point: reader judges first, then turns the page〕〔Outcome〕A dangerous event on record; no casualties. The missing ramp and the failure of checking entered the report together. Primary source: RAIB, Report 09/2026, 2026; link in Appendix B, ce-007.

### Case 2 (Healthcare): Counting Mutates in the Accident-Free Years

me-004. Maternity services, UK. HSIB investigation (Detection of retained vaginal swabs and tampons following childbirth, 2019; archived snapshot reviewed for this book): a postpartum haemostatic tampon was retained in the body and discovered only 5 days after discharge (original text: "It was left in and not discovered until five days after leaving hospital"). The count-and-track process for instruments and swabs exists to prevent exactly this event; its strictness mutates naturally during accident-free periods. No decision is required — only busyness. During the 5 days of retention the patient attended hospital twice as an outpatient, and neither visit prompted anyone to check for a thing that "should already have been removed."

Stop at the decision point: the moment in some delivery when the count is skipped or abbreviated. On the table: the busyness of the labour, the count sheet present and correct, "nothing like this has ever happened here." 〔Stop point: reader judges first, then turns the page〕〔Outcome〕Retained for 5 days; the report made the system of counting and tracking an investigation focus. Primary source: HSIB, 2019; link in Appendix B, me-004 (archived link, grade A−).

### Case 3 (Maritime): The Backup Pumps Never Verified

ma-006. The stern trawler Freedom II (CN 111), 21 February 2024, 11 nautical miles southwest of Oban, Scotland. MAIB report (Report 8/2026, reviewed for this book): the deck wash pump's suction line lacked support, was damaged, and admitted water; the vessel sank; the crew were rescued by lifeboat with no casualties. The investigation also found that not all of the pumps fitted on board were operational. On paper and on deck, backup capability had parted ways during the accident-free years: the inventory said "present," the deck said otherwise — and the only way to make the two lines agree, actually starting the pump once, had for many years been scheduled into no calendar.

Stop at the decision point: the most recent moment "backup pump available" was assumed rather than verified. On the table: the entry in the equipment inventory, the distant record of the last live test, and the day's operational priorities. 〔Stop point: reader judges first, then turns the page〕〔Outcome〕Vessel sunk; no casualties. Primary source: MAIB, Report 8/2026, 2026; link in Appendix B, ma-006.

### Case 4 (Aviation): The Assumptions Certified Without Verification

av-002. Japan Airlines Boeing 787-8 (JA829J), 7 January 2013, Boston. NTSB investigation report (AIR-14/01; the PDF of this report was downloaded and reviewed for this book): a fire broke out in the auxiliary power unit (APU) lithium battery of a parked aircraft; no one was aboard, emergency and firefighting responders attended, and one firefighter suffered a minor injury. The report went straight to the core gap in the safety assessment: the assumption of internal cell short circuit and the evaluation of thermal-runaway propagation passed type certification without adequate verification. The certification system's defensive investment — measured verification of the worst failure modes — was deemed dispensable in the face of the fleet's prior safe operating record: acceptable to rely on assumptions. After the fire came the grounding of the entire type, and the global 787 fleet sat on the ground for months. Relief is not verification, and the tuition for that lesson was paid by the entire industry.

Stop at the decision point: the moment the safety assessment accepted assumptions in place of measured tests and signed off type certification. On the table: the operating safety record of previous fleets (different chemistry, different failure modes), and the cost and duration of verification testing. 〔Stop point: reader judges first, then turns the page〕〔Outcome〕The Boston fire, together with subsequent events, triggered the worldwide grounding. Primary source: NTSB, AIR-14/01, 2014; link in Appendix B, av-002.

## 4 What Shows Earliest

Six weak signals, each with the reason that usually overrides it.

1. The defence of defensive line items in budget meetings begins to read "no accident in years." Override: "it is the truth."
2. The actual execution of drills, spot checks, and reviews has shifted from live testing to sign-in. Override: "the process is running."
3. Backup equipment stays unpowered and untested while its inventory status keeps reading "available." Override: "the equipment is new." (Freedom II's pumps.)
4. New employees understand defensive processes as "the old rules" and cannot say what they defend against. Override: "just follow it."
5. The last record of a defensive action "catching a problem" is more than two years old. Override: "problems have become rarer."
6. The years of data cited in cut proposals do not include events "caught by defences but never counted." Override: "no data." (No data is itself criterion ②.)

## 5 How to Defend

Device one: keep books for "did not happen." Give defensive investment an output ledger: every problem found in a drill, every batch intercepted by a spot check, every error corrected by a review — all entered. The ledger must separate two kinds of "empty": nothing to catch (true calm) and not catching (formality). The first is evidence the investment works; the second is evidence the investment is dead. Mixed together, they can never be told apart.

Device two: live testing for defences. Backups and standby processes are verified for real on a cycle: backup pumps powered and run, standby circuits actually switched, backup staff actually standing in (the principle of Chapter 22 — redundancy must be genuinely rehearsed — repeats here, because it is the defence most often breached in this chapter). Test results enter the ledger, bound to the "available" status claim: any entry not verified within the current cycle automatically drops to "unverified."

Device three: a two-year look-back on cuts. Every approved defensive cut registers a look-back date (two years out); at that date, the field's actual risk indicators are compared against the pre-cut baseline. The look-back cannot recover the loss, but it gives the next "never had an accident" in the next cutting meeting one more piece of history that can be questioned: each time the institution remembers once, the next defence speech has to write one more paragraph.

Device four: institutionalised memory. Write the reason each defence was created onto one page — "what it defends against" — and keep it with the process (same format as Chapter 30's "why" documents). A process whose new hires understand the defence is a process that can be kept; a line item where the organisation still remembers the accident is a line item that cannot be cut. The page's format: what it defends against, what once happened (accident number or industry case), and the minimal estimate of the cost of cutting it.

## 6 Boundaries with Adjacent Mechanisms

Against margin erosion (Chapter 25): eating margin is the cut itself (booked margins declining); slack is the growth of the will to cut (why it comes to feel permissible). In the Opportune case (ma-004) the seawater line was margin (Chapter 29), while "nobody has felt the need to look at it since 1998" was slack. The two ledgers are best kept on the same page.

Against normalisation of deviance (Chapter 11): normalisation files deviations ("the margin still suffices"); slack downgrades defences ("it is no longer needed"). The two share "no accident" as false evidence and diverge in direction: one relaxes the defence against the abnormal, the other relaxes the guardianship of the normal.

Against maturity mismatch (Chapter 28): slack's cost is redeemed late, and by redemption the decision-maker has left the stage — the structural reason it replays generation after generation, isomorphic with Chapter 28's time condition. Chapter 28's bill hand-over checklist should carry a line reading "which defences I cut."

## 7 Self-Check: Five Questions

1. Write down three defensive investments in your organisation that have "had no accident in years"; when did each last undergo a live test or catch a problem?
2. At your last budget meeting, what passed or got cut using "never had an accident" as the reason?
3. Your backup system — when did it last undergo a real switchover? Was the switchover done at business peak?
4. Pick one defensive process and ask a new colleague "what does it defend against." Does the answer reassure you?
5. If the defence ledger (Device one of this chapter) were set up today, how many "caught problems" do you estimate it would register in the first year? The question itself is the estimate.

## 6bis Installing the Devices: Order, Failure Modes, and Cost

Booking "nothing happened" goes first — it is the translation device that moves defensive spending from cost-language into output-language; without it, every later device fights from the defensive at budget season. Live testing follows (it supplies the ledger's evidence), the two-year look-back third (it attaches deferred accountability to cuts), institutionalized memory last (an annual-cycle device). First ninety days: weeks one and two, inventory the defensive portfolio — detection, maintenance, redundancy, drills, safety posts — line by line; weeks three through six, open output accounts for the first ten lines (what path it guards, current exposure, the last time it demonstrably fired — or an honest "no evidence on file"); month two, run the first live tests on two testable defenses (a real switchover, a red-team pass, a blind sample); month three, install the look-back clause — from the next budget document on, every defensive cut carries an automatic two-year review date.

Failure modes. The output account turns fictional (every quiet quarter booked as the defense's doing) — force an evidence field: each firing needs an identifiable event record, and no-evidence entries go into their own honest bucket. Live testing gets staged (drill day, full staffing, a rehearsed fault) — align conditions toward production and let results stand unedited, the same rule as Chapter 22's true switchover. The look-back turns prosecutorial (a hunt for culprits, driving the next round of cuts into subtler line items) — share Chapter 38's no-blame footing: ask about structure and arithmetic, not motive. Memory turns ceremonial (anniversary observed, manual dusted, nothing cited) — wire memory to decisions: the project-initiation template carries a "memory entry No." field; memory cited without a trail counts as absent.

Cost: the inventory and accounts are a one-time two to three days plus quarterly upkeep; live testing costs what drills already cost, plus the increment from paper drills to real ones; look-back and memory are clause costs. Against a missing boarding ramp that ran four years on "never happened," an aging seawater line, or a digital bank burning to a licensed surrender — slack's bill arrives in one lump years after the cuts; the monthly cost of the ledger doesn't cover the lump's first decimal.

Toolchain: the locator triggers on "long time pressure plus a long consequence-free history"; the defensive inventory is the opening move. Question set 20's item 4 ("does no-incident count as output in the finance口径" — in the finance definitions) audits the output account. Chapter 38's success-post-mortem variant (the verified minimum defense) supplies this ledger's evidence entries; and in pre-mortems, "defense already cut" deaths check their action against the output account's gap bucket — the pre-mortem and the ledger illuminate each other.
