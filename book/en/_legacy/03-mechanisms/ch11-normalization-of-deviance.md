# Chapter 11: Normalization of Deviance (Vaughan, 1996)

> What this chapter covers: how a deviation becomes routine on the strength of "nothing went wrong last time"; three activating criteria, the on-the-ground pattern in four domains, eight weak signals, and the countermeasures.
> When to return: when someone answers a challenge with "it has always been done this way"; when you are about to approve a request to relax a standard, extend an interval, or waive an inspection.
> Reading time: 55 minutes.

## 1 What It Is

Definition: the first occurrence of a deviation is handled as an anomaly. As it recurs and brings no consequence, it is reinterpreted as an acceptable state of affairs; the reinterpretation stops entering the ledger, and the standards for inspection and release quietly move. Diane Vaughan named this mechanism while studying the Challenger launch decision (Vaughan, The Challenger Launch Decision, University of Chicago Press, 1996), and the coldest part of her account is this: normalization does not happen in the explanation. It happens in the filing. Every deviation event receives two organizational treatments — an explanation (why nothing went wrong this time) and a file classification (which category it belongs to). The explanation is public and arguable; the filing is quiet and not arguable. The substantive act of normalization is moving the deviation from the "to be eliminated" drawer into the "known, acceptable" drawer — and the grounds on which it gets filed are the result: nothing happened.

Three activating criteria (the mechanism has started if any one of them appears):

1. The same class of deviation has occurred at least twice, with no consequence on any occasion;
2. Each occurrence was explained away as "the margin is still sufficient," "an isolated case," or "under control," and none of the explanations produced a ledger entry;
3. Inspection criteria, limits, or intervals have been lowered or relaxed and written into procedure — or habit has replaced procedure without anyone saying so.

The arithmetic core of the mechanism deserves a moment, because it explains why "it has always been done this way" is an understandable error rather than a stupid one. When a deviation appears for the nth time and nothing happens, two hypotheses are live. Hypothesis A: the margin really is sufficient, and the absence of consequence is a credit to the structure. Hypothesis B: the margin is insufficient, and the absence of consequence is a credit to luck. The organization almost never holds data that can distinguish A from B. Distinguishing them requires knowing the tail of the distribution, and the tail is precisely the part that has not happened yet. Normalization is the default to Hypothesis A when no distinguishing data exists. The cost of that default compounds with the number of exposures: every run conducted with the deviation in place is one exposure, and under Hypothesis B the probability of an accident rises with each exposure, while under Hypothesis A it stays flat. The "realized-in-installments" form of irreversibility described in Chapter 7 is exactly this arithmetic as it shows up in a release decision.

## 2 How It Grows

There are four generating conditions, corresponding to structure, incentives, time scale, and information.

Structural condition: deviations are produced at the front line, while the authority to eliminate them sits far away. Stopping a production line, switching a supplier, changing a design — each is costly and slow. Writing an explanation is cheap and finished on the spot. When the cost gap between eliminating and explaining grows large enough, the organization chooses explanation, again and again.

Incentive condition: the person who explains a deviation does not bear its tail risk. The manager who signs off on a relaxation has usually rotated to another post before the consequence lands (Horizon Mismatch, Chapter 28), or the deviation itself delivers gains in the current period — output, on-time rate, saved labor hours. And when the person who sees the deviation is also its beneficiary (Chapter 17), the explanation tilts toward Hypothesis A automatically.

Time scale: consequences are delayed. Delay tears the learning loop apart: months or even years separate a deviation from its consequence, and by the time the consequence arrives, the deviation record has already been papered over by the word "explained." Delay has a second effect: an unbroken consequence-free stretch is itself taken as evidence for Hypothesis A. Success becomes the fuel of deviation (the slack that success brings is the sibling mechanism — Failure of Success, Chapter 20).

Information condition: no cumulative ledger. Each deviation is assessed independently, and the assessment materials are the current event plus the assessor's memory; the inventory of historical deviations does not exist, or exists with every entry already marked "explained." Where no independent review exists, the Hypothesis A account never has to face cross-examination.

The mechanism has a single amplifier: years of service. Personal experience is not isomorphic with the organizational ledger in time. "I did this for twenty years and nothing ever happened" treats one person's exposure count as data, and the sampling bias of that move is the microscopic version of the very mechanism in this chapter (the border with Chapter 14: intuition is most confident precisely inside its own failure zone).

## 3 The Pattern in Four Domains

The four cases below come from four domains. By the convention of this book, each one stops at its decision point; the reader judges first, then turns the page for the outcome. Case IDs match Appendix B.

### Case 1 (Spaceflight): The "In-Family" Classification of Foam Strikes

mi-001. Space Shuttle Columbia, mission STS-107. At launch (January 16, 2003), foam shed from the external tank struck the thermal protection system on the left wing. This was not the first time: foam shedding had recurred across earlier flights, and each occurrence had been assessed and filed as an "in-family" phenomenon — the same kind of shedding as previously seen, posing no new safety concern. The report of the Columbia Accident Investigation Board (CAIB) quotes the program documents' classification clause directly: deviations that had been previously experienced, analyzed, and understood could be treated as in-family with special approval of the Space Shuttle Program or the responsible design project (CAIB Report Vol. 1, 2003, PDF p. 123; the official PDF of this volume has been downloaded and verified for this book). During the on-orbit assessment after STS-107 reached orbit, engineers requested imaging of the left wing to establish the damage; the request was refused somewhere along the decision chain. The Debris Assessment Team had only a mathematical model named CRATER with which to estimate damage, and CRATER was not designed for impacts of this kind (CAIB report, p. 39).

〔Stop point: reader judges first, then turns the page〕

1. What sits in front of the assessment team at this moment? An "in-family" habitual classification, a model built for a different purpose, a refused imaging request.
2. If you were program management, what would approving the imaging mean right now — in cost, in schedule, and as an admission of what, to whom?
3. What do you bet will happen at reentry?

〔Outcome〕On February 1, 2003, Columbia broke up during reentry and all seven astronauts aboard died. During the debris search, two more ground personnel died in a helicopter accident (CAIB report, pp. 7, 10; verified against the original). The CAIB's review of the "in-family" classification is the source of this chapter's criterion 3: the movement of the standard was written into the clause itself, the approval channel was intact, and every movement was compliant. Primary source: CAIB, Report Volume I, August 2003, official NASA PDF (six volumes bound, 3,535 pages); link in Appendix B, mi-001.

### Case 2 (Aviation Maintenance): Interval Extensions, Approved One Signature at a Time

av-034. Alaska Airlines Flight 261, an MD-83. The company extended both the lubrication interval and the end-play check interval for the horizontal stabilizer jackscrew assembly, and the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) approved the extension (NTSB AAR-02/01 executive summary; the report PDF has been downloaded and verified for this book). The report concluded that the extended intervals gave a missed lubrication deficiency the opportunity to develop into failure-grade wear; the threads of the jackscrew's acme nut ultimately failed in flight, and the aircraft plunged into the Pacific Ocean on January 31, 2000, killing all 88 people aboard.

Stop at the decision point: the moment the extension is approved. On the desk: the inspection records from all the years the original interval was honored, the manufacturer's manual recommendations, the cost-benefit calculation for the extension — and one fact that nobody present can see: Hypothesis B (nothing has happened yet because of luck) and Hypothesis A (the margin really is sufficient) cannot be distinguished from this seat. The approver's signature occupies a precise position in this chapter's mechanism: it is criterion 3 — the relaxation has now been written into an approved document. Every subsequent release of an aircraft at the new interval is one more exposure (Chapter 7). Primary source: NTSB, AAR-02/01, 2002; link in Appendix B, av-034.

### Case 3 (Chemicals): Thirty-Two Alarms

ch-001. DuPont's La Porte works, pesticide manufacturing unit. In November 2014, methyl mercaptan leaked into the manufacturing building during a maintenance operation. The CSB report (No. 2015-01-I-TX, released 2019; the PDF has been downloaded and verified for this book) records, in the original log, 32 methyl mercaptan gas alarms triggered on the control panel in the 17 hours before the incident (verified against the report). An alarm is a deviation signal. The 32 alarms were explained at the operator station as the normal accompaniment of a maintenance leak: the deviation had not only produced no consequence, it was repeating at high frequency — and the repetition itself became evidence of harmlessness. The building's roof exhaust fan had been out of service for over a month (a maintenance debt; the territory of Chapter 29), leaving the escaped material in the very space where people worked.

Stop at the decision point: any one of the alarm-response moments across those 17 hours. On the desk: the alarm readings, the maintenance work plan, and the accumulated experience that "alarms have never led to anything." The choice: escalate the alarm through the response procedure — interrupt the maintenance, trigger ventilation and an evacuation assessment — or treat it as a routine byproduct of maintenance and keep working, as convention dictates. 〔Outcome〕The leak intensified. Four workers — three operators and one shift supervisor — died inside the building. Primary source: CSB, No. 2015-01-I-TX, 2019; link in Appendix B, ch-001.

### Case 4 (Finance): Alarms Closed in Seconds

fi-013. The brokerage Moors & Cabot, anti-money-laundering (AML) monitoring. FINRA's disciplinary decision of August 2026 (an AWC; the monthly digest PDF has been verified for this book) found that the firm closed large numbers of AML alerts within seconds, without corresponding review, after a prior independent AML test had already flagged an alert backlog and no remediation followed. The alert system's design logic is "alert appears — human review — close or escalate." Second-scale closure means the review stage has been hollowed out: alerts have turned from to-do items into noise, and closing an alert has turned from a disposition into a clock-punch.

Stop at the decision point: the moment the independent test report reaches management and names the backlog. On the desk: the test findings, the scale of the backlog, the cost of remediation (staff, systems), and an operating record that reads "nothing has happened for years." The choice: add people and systems as the test recommends, or hold the status quo and the existing closing rhythm. 〔Outcome〕FINRA found the violations established and in August 2026 imposed a censure and a fine of $125,000. The amount is small; the archival value of the behavior is not. It shows what normalization looks like in the compliance domain: compliance alerts, too, can be house-trained by "nothing has ever happened." Primary source: FINRA, Disciplinary and Other FINRA Actions – October 2026; link in Appendix B, fi-013.

## 4 What Can Be Seen Earliest

A list of weak signals, each with the reason it is usually steamrolled by. Ordered from earliest to latest.

1. The ledger of deviations is missing, or every entry in it is marked "explained." The reason it gets waved off: "We already dealt with that."
2. The number of anomaly reports falls while the volume of problems reported by the front line stays flat. Reason: "The safety situation is improving." (A drop in reporting more likely means the reporters have given up.)
3. The wording of inspection criteria or acceptance clauses changes from "must" to "in principle." Reason: "For operability."
4. "It has been this way before" appears in meeting minutes for the first time, cited as a basis for a decision. Reason: "Respect for practical experience."
5. Requests to relax limits, intervals, or thresholds are raised by the users themselves, and the review is carried out by the users' routine collaborators. Reason: "Standards should fit reality."
6. A newcomer's question about existing practice receives an answer in the form of convention rather than in the form of evidence. Reason: "Mentoring is the best training."
7. The same issue carries two or more official explanation versions that never cite each other. Reason: "Just a difference of phrasing."
8. "No consequence occurred" is used as safety evidence for the first time — rather than as an acknowledgment that data is lacking. Reason: "Let the results speak."

Item 8 deserves a note of its own: it marks the mechanism in its fully developed form. From this point on, the mechanism's output is a system of argument that takes "nothing happened" as evidence, and any later challenge must first refute that system before it can reach the facts. The abuse of black-swan rhetoric described in Chapter 41 mostly grows on this foundation.

## 5 How to Prevent It

The principle common to all the countermeasures: take the filing action back from "explanation" and hand it to the ledger and the signature. Four devices.

Device one: a three-state ledger for deviations. Each deviation entry may hold only one of three states: eliminated (with evidence of elimination attached), remediation by a deadline (with the deadline and a responsible person attached), or operating with accepted risk (with a risk assessment and a signature attached). A fourth state — "explained" — is forbidden. Explanations may go in the notes field, but the state itself must land in one of the three. Device one in Chapter 8 is this same ledger as it is used in escalation scenarios.

Device two: margin stated in numbers. Any claim that "the margin is still sufficient" must be written as a number together with its data source — and the data source may not be the absence of consequences itself (evidence for Hypothesis B cannot be used to prove Hypothesis A). A margin claim that cannot cite a source counts as no margin.

Device three: an exposure audit. Every accepted-risk item registers two numbers: the current cumulative exposure count, and the exposure increment per release. The audit asks exactly one question: is this deviation's continued operation a bounded exposure that we approved, or an unbounded one? (This is the ledger form of the realized-in-installments irreversibility from Chapter 7.)

Device four: dual signatures for relaxation. Any approval that relaxes a standard, extends an interval, or waives an inspection requires two signatures: the requesting side's responsible person, plus a representative of the risk-bearing party who has no stake in the deviation and whose own evaluation is not affected by current-period output. The FAA's approval in the Alaska 261 case failed to constitute an independent gate because its information source was singular. The dual-signature rule hard-wires the information requirement: the material supporting approval must include inspection or test records from parties other than the requester.

## 6 Boundaries with Adjacent Mechanisms

Versus practical drift (Chapter 15): drift describes the fork between practice and rules — how the work is actually done. Normalization describes the fork being redefined as acceptable — how it gets judged. Drift can exist without normalization: a fork watched by the ledger remains in "remediation by deadline" status. Normalization usually takes drift as its raw material, but it requires the second step — explain, then file. When both appear, first locate the drift's fork point, then find out who filed it.

Versus margin erosion (Chapter 25): erosion is a reduction with a physical action behind it (knife work), and it leaves a ledger to inspect. Normalization is an interpretive slide (mouth work), and it usually leaves nothing. A single approval that extends an interval is both an erosion action and a normalization; distinguish them by their carrier — if it landed in a document, it is Chapter 25; if it landed in the tone of meetings and in habit, it is this chapter.

Versus narrative before evidence (Chapter 13): narrative operates after a conclusion goes public (external commitments, internal alignment); normalization operates at the level of internal archives (filing, classification). The two often appear as a pair: first internal normalization, then external narrativization. The distinguishing test is the audience — filing actions aimed inward belong to this chapter; commitments aimed outward belong to Chapter 13.

## 7 Self-Check: Five Questions

1. In the deviation ledger of your own process, how many entries sit in "explained" status? Pick one: is its deviation still running today?
2. When did you last say "it has always been done this way," and in what setting? Did the other side's question contain anything worth a ledger entry?
3. Of the relaxations you have signed (intervals, limits, waivers), how many rested on material that included records from parties other than the requester?
4. In your organization, who can state the current cumulative exposure count of any given accepted-risk item? If no one can, in whose hands is the exposure accumulating?
5. Pick one habitual practice of yours that has run for more than two years without ever producing a consequence, and run it through criteria 1, 2, and 3. If all three hit, what is it called in this chapter's terms?

## 6bis Installing the Devices: Order, Failure Modes, and Cost

The ledger comes first; the other three devices grow out of it. A workable first ninety days: weeks one and two, build the ledger — do a one-time census of standing deviations, which is itself an audit, and most organizations are frightened at this step by the number of entries marked "explained." Weeks three through eight, run the three states on everything new (leave the stock untouched rather than choke on the first bite). Month three, put margin-in-numbers to work: pick ten accepted-risk entries and force each to produce its number. The exposure audit and dual signatures come later, because both consume the ledger as input — an exposure audit without a ledger is an interview collecting anecdotes; dual signatures without a ledger are just a new bureaucratic node.

Each device has a characteristic way of being gamed. The three-state ledger gets gamed by sprawl: "operating with accepted risk" becomes a long-term residence, so give accepted-risk entries a mandatory re-review date (quarterly; renewal requires fresh evidence and a signature). Margin-in-numbers gets gamed by optimistic sourcing — the data source is chosen to be the friendliest one (the same-source problem of Chapter 22, projected onto margins) — so require two independent sources, and when they disagree, take the smaller number. The exposure audit gets gamed by definitional drift in the counting unit (per flight or per hour, per batch or per piece — pick the unit and the exposure shrinks), so register the unit when the entry is created and route any change through the change-of-caliber register (Chapter 12, Device 4). Dual signatures get gamed by diluted representation — the risk-bearing representative becomes a permanent appointee from a friendly department — so rotate the seat and register conflicts of interest annually.

The cost, in hours. Ledger upkeep: ten to twenty minutes per new deviation (entry, state, evidence link); at two hundred new deviations a year that is roughly fifty hours, plus a one-time census of thirty to fifty. Margin-in-numbers: two to four hours per entry, about thirty in total, once. Exposure audit: half a day per quarter. Dual signatures: zero marginal cost. On the order of a hundred hours a year, against the price tags of Alaska 261 (88 lives), La Porte (4 lives), or the AML fine — the arithmetic needs no further help.

Toolchain interfaces: Chapter 35 flags this mechanism (long consequence delay plus two-plus consequence-free recurrences), and the opening move for a flagged organization is the census above.
