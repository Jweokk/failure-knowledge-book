# Chapter 14: Breakdown Conditions of Intuitive Expertise (Kahneman & Klein)

> What this chapter covers: under what conditions expert intuition is reliable and under what conditions it fails; three criteria, four domains on the ground, a test for "experience" used as an argument, and how to make intuition explicit.
> When to return: when a veteran is moved into a new environment; when someone answers data with "I've done this for twenty years"; when you yourself feel "this just feels right" about a novel situation.
> Reading time: 50 minutes.

Two scholars of judgment and decision-making — Daniel Kahneman, an experimental psychologist who spent decades suspicious of intuition, and Gary Klein, a naturalistic decision researcher who spent decades defending expert intuition — once co-wrote a paper whose title is itself the answer: intuitive experts and non-intuitive experts reach "a failure to disagree." They found that under specific conditions the two schools agree entirely about when intuition can be trusted. The conditions are only two: the environment must be regular enough, and the feedback must be timely enough. This chapter is that consensus turned into engineering: it converts "can intuition be relied on here" from metaphysics into three criteria that can be checked against the facts.

## 1 What It Is

Definition: intuition is a recognition ability trained jointly by environmental regularity and practice feedback. Kahneman and Klein gave its two standing premises: the environment is regular enough that its patterns can be learned, and practice comes with timely, high-quality feedback, so that errors are seen and corrected (Kahneman & Klein, "Conditions for Intuitive Expertise: A Failure to Disagree", American Psychologist, 2009 〔to be verified: volume, issue, pages〕). Remove either premise and intuition enters its failure zone: it still delivers answers fast, its confidence does not drop, and its accuracy falls to that of a novice.

Three activating criteria (if any one holds, intuition may no longer be used as a basis):

1. The environment is unfamiliar, or its rules have just changed (new industry, new equipment, new population, new regulatory framework);
2. Feedback is delayed by months or more — or does not exist at all (the consequences of the decision are invisible or unmeasurable);
3. There is no opportunity for repeated practice (a once-in-a-career situation).

The real danger of the failure zone must be stated plainly: inside the failure zone, intuition is not weakened — it is decoupled from its own confidence. The felt certainty of the judge and the actual hit rate of the judgment part ways, and the judge can feel only the former. That is why the most dangerous person in the failure zone is precisely the veteran whose environment changed but whose confidence did not. He is not bluffing. He genuinely feels sure, and in the old environment that feeling was calibrated.

## 2 How It Grows

There are three paths into the failure zone.

Path one: cross-domain transfer. Intuition accumulated in domain A is carried into domain B — the veteran CEO parachuted into a new industry, the senior mechanical engineer handed a software system, the successful clinician moved into management. The environment's regularity has changed; the recognition templates have not. Transfer happens as often as it does because what organizations prize in promotions and postings is exactly a record of past intuition: the more successful the person, the more transferable they are presumed to be.

Path two: one success mistaken for a pattern. A single survival gets distilled into reusable "experience." A pattern with a sample size of one is exceptionally confident inside the failure zone, because it never meets a counterexample — counterexamples require repetition, and it has only the one trial.

Path three: rule changes erode the old regularity. The environment has not moved; the rules have (new regulations, new aircraft type, new software version, new process). The old intuition has gone stale at the level of detail, and the person carrying it perceives no change. Failure to perceive the rule change is the quietest entrance into the failure zone: paths one and two at least come with the visible signal of a changed environment; path three offers no signal at all.

The amplifier is hierarchy: the more senior the judgment, the fewer the people in a position to check it. An error in front-line operation is discovered the same day — a wrongly turned screw will not go back together. An executive's strategic judgment takes three to five years to resolve, and the resolution can be re-narrated in the meantime (Chapter 13). The feedback loop is weakest at the top of the organization, and the exposure period of the intuition failure zone is longest there.

## 3 The Pattern in Four Domains

### Case 1 (Aviation): The Narrow-Runway Illusion

av-011. Cessna A185F (N888DK), July 19, 2025, Lansing, Michigan. NTSB final report (Aviation Investigation Final Report CEN25LA260; the PDF has been downloaded and verified for this book): after a bounced landing the airplane veered; the right wing and aileron struck the ground and were damaged; all four people aboard escaped injury. The report records the pilot's own account: he suspected he had been affected by the runway width illusion — when a pilot transitions from a wide runway to a narrow one, the narrow runway appears farther away and higher than it is, so the pilot retards the throttle and flattens too early, and the airplane touches down short of the runway or at an excessive descent rate. This is one of the repeatedly studied perceptual illusions of visual flight, and training materials carry a specific countermeasure for it: trust the instruments; check altitude and descent rate rather than the "felt altitude."

Stop at the decision point: the moment of the flare. On the desk: the runway as it "looked" to the eye, the altimeter's true reading, and a sense of flare timing built over years of visual flying. The frightening property of an illusion is that it is a systematic error at the layer of perception itself: when instrument and perception conflict, intuition inside the failure zone overrides the instrument — and the judge calls that "flight experience." 〔Outcome〕Right wing and aileron destroyed; no injuries. Primary source: NTSB, CEN25LA260, 2025; link in Appendix B, av-011.

### Case 2 (Chemicals): A Novice in Front of Three Look-Alike Valves

ch-004. The Shell Polymers Monaca ethane cracker, June 4, 2025, Pennsylvania. CSB report (No. 2025-05-I-PA, September 2026; the PDF has been downloaded and verified for this book): the operator interface displayed three similar valves on the same screen; an operator without experience opened the wrong one; flammable gas flowed back into the furnace and exploded. Before the incident, the process hazard analysis (PHA) had already identified the backflow scenario as lethal; the safeguards relied entirely on 11 purely administrative controls — procedures, training, reminders — with no physical interlock anywhere. Shell estimated property damage at $95 million; 15 employees were evacuated; there were no injuries.

Stop at the decision point: the moment the novice, facing three valves on one screen, presses the button. On the desk: valves that look identical on the display, a distinction point from training that never grew into intuition, and a risk that was written into the PHA but implemented as a "verbal reminder." This case's failure zone has two layers: the operator's intuition had not yet been built (criterion 1 on the training side), and the system's designers assumed intuition would be built on schedule (criterion 1 on the design side). The effectiveness premise of administrative controls — that people have stable attention and recognition — is exactly what the failure zone confiscates. 〔Outcome〕Explosion and fire; no casualties; $95 million in losses. Primary source: CSB, No. 2025-05-I-PA, 2026; link in Appendix B, ch-004.

### Case 3 (Maritime): The Chain of Reaching Out to Help

ma-010. The emergency response and rescue vessel Viking Islay, September 2007, off the East Riding of Yorkshire, England. MAIB report (Report 12/2008; the page has been verified for this book): corrosion of the steel structure inside the anchor-chain locker had consumed the oxygen and created an oxygen-deficient atmosphere. The first crew member entered and collapsed; shipmates went in after him, one after another, without gas-detection equipment, to rescue him. Three seamen died. The report states in its own words: "three seamen on board the ERRV Viking Islay lost their lives as a consequence of entering an enclosed space." The rescue instinct — you see a shipmate collapse, you reach out — is a virtue in open air, and in an oxygen-deficient compartment it was the second and third cause of death.

Stop at the decision point: the moment the second crew member stands at the hatch. On the desk: the collapsed shipmate, the darkness inside, the impulse to "go down and pull him out," the testing-and-standby requirements written into the procedure, and the gas detector hanging on the wall. The failure-zone signature of intuition here is criterion 3: this is a situation most people meet once in a lifetime; there is no practice opportunity; and every correct action — test first, ventilate first, attach a rescue line, never enter alone — violates present instinct. 〔Outcome〕Three dead; the report drove reforms that put enclosed spaces into procedures. Primary source: MAIB, Report 12/2008, 2008; link in Appendix B, ma-010.

### Case 4 (Finance): The Short Calls Missing from the Net-Long Calculation

fi-012. Cowen and Company. FINRA disciplinary action, August 2026 (monthly digest PDF verified for this book): in a partial tender offer, internal procedures never defined how 100,000 shares of short call options were to be counted in the net-long calculation; the computation ran along the old convention; the tender was oversubmitted by 150,000 shares; the fine was $80,000 (of which $40,000 was payable to FINRA).

Stop at the decision point: the moment the net-long calculation convention meets a new form of instrument. On the desk: the familiar old worksheet, a new position type living off the sheet, and the deadline pressure of the tender-offer close. The rules changed — the instrument innovation came first — and the computational intuition did not. Computational intuition fails with particular quietness: it outputs a number precise to the share, and the feeling of precision is itself the down payment on the error. 〔Outcome〕Violations established; the procedures gained a conversion definition. Primary source: FINRA, Disciplinary and Other FINRA Actions – October 2026, 2026; link in Appendix B, fi-012.

What the four cases share: in the moment of judgment, every judge possessed complete confidence — and the sources of that confidence: visual experience, trade common sense, the rescue instinct, the old worksheet — all sit within the range of criterion 1 or criterion 3. Identifying the failure zone does not require doubting the judge's intelligence. It requires checking only which environment the judgment's underlying experience was formed in.

## 4 What Can Be Seen Earliest

Eight weak signals, each with the reason it is usually steamrolled by.

1. Decision rationales contain analogies rather than measurements ("this is about like the one back then"). The reason it gets waved off: "Experience is the fastest theory we have."
2. The environment's rules, equipment, or population changed recently, and the people in key positions did not. Reason: "Core capability is transferable."
3. A veteran answers data with seniority in a meeting ("I've done this for twenty years"). Reason: "Respect for practice."
4. A class of decisions has never received feedback in the organization's history: nobody ever went back to check whether they were right. Reason: "It has always run fine."
5. Training hours are cut on the grounds that the system handles it automatically. Reason: "Efficiency." (Chapter 26 develops from here.)
6. A newcomer asks a basic question, and the experts present answer vaguely. Reason: "I forget the details, but I'm sure about the direction."
7. The onboarding period for cross-domain hires is shorter than their original domain's formation period by an order of magnitude. Reason: "We hired him to plug in immediately."
8. The organization's "expert judgments" leave no record: the judgment is made, the basis is not written, and later calibration is impossible. Reason: "The business moves fast."

Items 4 and 8 together amount to the definition of calibration: no record means no feedback; no feedback means no calibration; confidence without calibration is just personality.

## 5 How to Prevent It

Device one: an intuition admission table. Add one mandatory line to the review sheet for any major judgment: "Does the experience this judgment rests on hold in the current environment (rules / equipment / population)? On what basis?" A judgment with no citable basis is downgraded to an unverified hypothesis (the tiered release of Chapter 13). The admission table costs almost nothing — one line of text — and it intercepts the entire failure-zone category.

Device two: convert intuition into checklists. The veteran's intuition is an asset: invite it out into the open, write it as explicit checklist items, so novices benefit and veterans become reviewable. The operating method is interview-based extraction: have the expert work while narrating "what I am looking at right now," and record the observed attention points as checklist candidates. Whatever cannot be written down gets marked as the not-reliable-to-depend-on portion, and the expert signs it personally. The signature is the crux: it converts "you have to be there to get it" from a disclaimer into a risk statement.

Device three: environment changes trigger retraining. Every change to rules, equipment, or interfaces produces a list titled "intuitions that depended on the old environment," and each item is assessed for whether retraining is needed. The interface change in the Shell Monaca case and the instrument innovation in the Cowen case should both have triggered this step. The trigger's carrier is the change order: add a field to it — "list of existing experience affected" — filled in by whoever proposes the change.

Device four: equip the failure zone with instruments. Four-gas detectors for enclosed spaces, approach aids for narrow runways, automatic conversion for net-long calculations: inside intuition's failure zone, substitute measurement for recognition. The budget-ordering rule: scenarios where criterion 1 holds (the environment is unfamiliar) come first, because that is where failure is most total.

## 6 Boundaries with Adjacent Mechanisms

Versus extrapolation beyond the validated domain (Chapter 23): extrapolation moves a conclusion into a new environment (the data layer); this chapter moves a judge into a new environment (the person layer). When one situation occupies both, check the person first (whether his experiential premises hold), then the conclusion (whether its validation boundary holds): repairing the person is cheap; repairing the conclusion is expensive.

Versus practical drift (Chapter 15): drift is the fork between practice and text (an organizational phenomenon); the failure zone is the overreach of an individual's recognition ability (an individual phenomenon). A whole crew can drift together; the failure zone appears only in specific people. Conversely, in a drifted organization an individual's intuition can remain perfectly accurate — accurately executing a set of practices that has already departed from the text.

Versus everything said about expert confidence: this chapter does not oppose experience; it opposes experience acting beyond its writ. The test is always the three criteria, never an attitude toward experts — neither blind deference nor dismissal of a claim because of the person making it.

## 7 Self-Check: Five Questions

1. Your core judgments right now: do the two premises — environmental regularity and feedback quality — still hold? When did each last change?
2. Whose data have you answered with "I've done this for twenty years"? Looking back, was that moment intuition, or the failure zone?
3. After your team's most recent environment change (new system, new rules, new population), which pieces of old experience were formally re-evaluated? If there is not one list, where did the re-evaluation happen?
4. Which decisions in your role are "once in a lifetime"? What do they run on today — measurement, or recognition?
5. Can you write some veteran's intuition as a checklist? Try one item. For the part you cannot write, does he dare sign "this portion cannot be relied on"?

## 6bis Installing the Devices: Order, Failure Modes, and Cost

The environment-change trigger goes first — it is the detector that tells you whether you are standing in the failure zone at all. The admission table follows (the register), then the checklist conversion and instrumentation (the treatments). First ninety days: weeks one and two, list the organization's high-stakes judgments and score each on environmental stability and feedback delay (the two criteria of Section 1, item by item); weeks three through six, open admission tables for every judgment that lands in the zone (each veteran intuition registered with its exposure history: how many cases, in what environment); month two, convert the two highest-risk judgments into checklists — sit with the expert and decompose "something feels wrong" into inspectable sub-items; month three, instrument at least one judgment with data monitoring, a test tool, or a structured second opinion.

Failure modes. The admission table turns decorative (case counts inflated, environments described in fog) — sample it: pick registered cases at random and ask the holder to narrate one in detail; unverifiable detail discounts the count. The checklist calcifies into new dogma — so bind checklist versions to environment snapshots, and let the change trigger refresh the checklist too. Instrumentation has the subtlest failure: the instrument becomes the intuition's new coat, readings cited to dress a predetermined conclusion (Chapter 13 reborn around a sensor). Separate the timestamps: record readings first, judgment second, and audit afterwards how far judgments drifted from readings. The trigger itself fails silently when environment changes are never registered — so fold "experience environment" into the caliber register (Chapter 12, Device 4).

Cost: lists and tables are one-time builds plus quarterly upkeep (a day or two each); conversion takes two to four days per judgment, and the decomposing session doubles as knowledge extraction; instrumentation ranges from a free checklist to capital equipment, but most judgments' "instrument" is cheap — a structured data sheet, a second reader, a standard interview script. Against the delayed aortic dissection, the reflux-to-furnace blind spot, or the three entries into an oxygen-depleted chain locker, the failure zone prices in lives and the devices price in hours — no exchange rate is needed.

Toolchain: the locator triggers on "network scale plus long feedback." Question set 14's item 4 ("twenty years of experience" deployed against data) is the admission table's direct audit. In pre-mortems, this chapter's deaths need outsiders in the room — the zone is defined by local experience being unreliable, so local rooms cannot pre-mortem their own blind spots. In post-mortems, any "experienced judgment erred" finding converts to checklist form via Device 2 — never to the generic verb "more training."
