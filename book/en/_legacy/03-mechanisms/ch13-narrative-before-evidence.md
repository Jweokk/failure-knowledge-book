# Chapter 13: Narrative before Evidence; Motivated Reasoning

> What this chapter covers: how conclusion-before-investigation actually operates — how an explanation that has already been spoken aloud goes on to filter evidence, raise the bar for opponents, and bind the organization to its first draft of judgment; three criteria, four domains on the ground, four lines of defense.
> When to return: before roadshow materials are finalized; in the middle of an accident-attribution meeting; when a project proposal enters the approval workflow; and whenever you catch yourself waiting for "the supporting data."
> Reading time: 50 minutes.

There is a moment in the prehistory of almost every major failure, and it never appears in the cause chain of the accident report. It is this: an explanation that is not yet mature gets spoken aloud in full for the first time. Spoken to investors, spoken to superiors, written into the summary field of a project proposal, posted into the work chat. From the moment it is spoken, the nature of the explanation changes. Before that moment it is a hypothesis awaiting test — wrong, and you simply revise it. After that moment it is a position — wrong, and you have broken faith; to test it is a provocation. This chapter is about what happens to an organization after that moment.

## 1 What It Is

Definition: narrative before evidence is an inversion of publishing order — the conclusion goes public, is committed to, and is written into documents before the evidence is complete, and from then on every piece of new evidence is filed under the established explanation, while opponents are asked to carry a heavier burden of proof. Its psychological engine is motivated reasoning: a large body of experiments in cognitive psychology shows that people do not gather evidence first and form a position second; they hold the position first and select the evidence afterward, and the process is transparent to the person it happens in — it cannot be detected by introspection (Ziva Kunda's 1990 review of motivated reasoning is the standard citation in this field 〔to be verified: Kunda, "The Case for Motivated Reasoning", Psychological Bulletin, 1990, volume and pages〕). Private motivated reasoning is harmless enough. Public commitment is what upgrades it into an institution: an individual revising a position has no audience; an organization does.

Three activating criteria (the mechanism has started if any one of them appears):

1. The conclusion has already been said outward — to customers, investors, superiors, the public, regulators; any form of public commitment counts. The key word in the criterion is "outward": what you say to your own people can be retracted quietly; what you have said outward becomes an event when retracted.
2. When new evidence arrives, the first question the discussion asks is "how do we explain it," not "does it shake the premise." The filing action replacing the testing action is the operational signature of the narrative having taken over.
3. Opposing views are required to "bring more complete evidence," while the proposing side never passed an equivalent bar when it first published its conclusion. The asymmetry of the burden of proof is the institutional evidence that the narrative has already acquired status.

Criterion 3 deserves special emphasis, because it is the easiest to misread as rigor: "We welcome disagreement, but bring data." That sentence looks identical in two different organizations — one genuinely verifies the data of both sides, the other verifies only the challenger's. The way to tell them apart is precedent: what happened to the last person who raised a challenge.

## 2 How It Grows

Three generating conditions, plus one amplifier.

Condition one: the public commitment has already happened. The form does not matter — a roadshow, a project proposal, a press release, a statement to a regulator, a launch date promised to customers. Once the commitment is out, the cost of retreat changes character: it stops being "revise a conclusion" and becomes "admit the earlier judgment was wrong," which is an order of magnitude more expensive, because it offends professional reputation and organizational face at the same time (the third gate of Chapter 4). Understanding this condition explains why narratives always rush ahead: the organization that speaks first has effectively bought insurance on its own judgment.

Condition two: information overload. When the evidence flow is too large, the existing explanation is the only filter available. What reaches a project team in a single day — data, rumor, competitor moves, internal complaints — exceeds anyone's processing bandwidth, and fitting material into a ready framework is the cognitively cheap path. Overload is what turns the cheap path into the default path. This also explains why the "deep-dive study" that follows an overload so often just deepens the first impression: the more that gets invested, the more consistency has already been sunk into the first reading.

Condition three: no independent analytical path. Nowhere in the organization is there one person, one role, authorized to set the mainstream explanation aside and analyze separately. Note what this is not: it is not a shortage of smart people. It is the absence of an institutionalized opposition lane for the smart people already there. When the lane is missing, every objection must hitch a ride on the mainstream explanation ("I agree with the general direction, but..."), and the objection is disarmed at the level of wording.

The amplifier is time pressure: the more an occasion demands an answer right now — the early hours of an accident, a financing window, a policy launch, a bid deadline — the more easily the narrative outruns the evidence. Time pressure does not manufacture motive; it grants hesitation a waiver: "we have to say something first."

## 3 The Pattern in Four Domains

### Case 1 (Finance): The Offering Text and the Underground Reality

fi-005. American Patriot Brands (APB), a cannabis company's securities offering. What gives this case's material its value is the shape of its ending: it was not muted by a settlement — it was litigated by the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) to summary judgment in federal court and a final judgment entered without settlement. On July 10, 2026, the U.S. District Court for the Central District of California ordered APB and its subsidiaries, jointly and severally, to disgorge $17,786,703, with prejudgment interest of $6,202,777; it fined APB $4,729,004; it held Chief Executive Officer Lee individually liable for a combined $6,399,792 and barred him from serving as an officer or director; it fined Chief Operating Officer Pallas $472,902 (Litigation Release No. 26587; the page has been verified for this book). The court found the violations under Section 17(a) of the Securities Act and Section 10(b) of the Exchange Act and Rule 10b-5 — offering fraud.

Stop at the decision point: the node at which the offering materials set down the company's prospects as final text, go to print, and begin reaching investors. On the desk: the real state of operations and assets (the court found the gap between it and the offering narrative to be the core of the fraud), the fact that investors would pay money on the strength of this text, and counsel's final edits to the wording. Once the offering text leaves the building, the narrative is the commitment; every investor communication after that glosses the established text rather than testing it. 〔Outcome〕Fraud established; disgorgement, interest, penalties, and officer bars all landed in full; the former Chief Financial Officer had earlier resolved his part by consent judgment. Primary source: SEC, Litigation Release No. 26587, 2026; link in Appendix B, fi-005.

The transferable judgment this case offers the reader: every adjective in an offering text is future evidence. Write it on day one to the standard of "this will be checked line by line." That is not compliance advice; it is the physical form of this chapter's criterion 1.

### Case 2 (Public Sector): The Replication Narrative and Its Precedents

po-006. The Sizewell C nuclear power station (UK). The May 2026 report of the National Audit Office (NAO, HC 33, 2026-27; the page has been verified for this book) contains a sentence that deserves to be engraved on the title page of every project proposal that argues from "replicating proven experience": "no nuclear power station of this type has so far avoided overrun in schedule or cost." Yet the project's approval argument was built on the narrative that replicating an existing design reduces risk; the audit further noted that investor returns were "on the high side," that the incentive structure for cost control was unclear, and that the generation date pointed to 2038.

Stop at the decision point: the approval cycle in which "replicating an existing design" served as the core argument while schedule and cost were promised to the treasury and the public. On the desk: the engineering similarity of the replicated design (real, and present), the complete public history of comparable projects (real, present, and sitting in the file), and the way the two were combined — similarity written up as the conclusion, history relegated to a footnote. 〔Outcome〕The project was approved to proceed; the audit recorded the gap between narrative and precedent, as the baseline for any future re-estimate. Primary source: NAO, Sizewell C, 2026; link in Appendix B, po-006.

This case's teaching point is criterion 2 in its most concealed form: the new evidence — every comparable project overran — was not denied. It is public fact; denial is impossible. It was filed under the existing explanation: "which is why we adopted a new model." Every knock of evidence at the door was used to demonstrate how solid the door was.

### Case 3 (Drugs and Devices): Into Distribution before Clearance

ph-005. Adept Medical's ear ventilation T-tube (9mm). The FDA device recall record (Z-0966-2025; the API record has been verified for this book) gives the reason for recall in one line: Lack of 510(k) clearance. The product had already been distributed — 2,500 units in the United States and 3,780 abroad, into markets including Canada, Australia, and New Zealand. The recall was marked as ongoing.

Stop at the decision point: the internal node at which distribution was set to begin while clearance was still pending. On the desk: the status and expected date of the clearance application, the readiness of the distribution channels, the lower threshold of 510(k) awareness in overseas markets — and one question scheduled for later: if clearance is ultimately refused, what happens to the units already sold. The sell-first, paper-later narrative has a pleasant name, "seizing the window"; its structural name is criterion 1 — treating the accomplished fact as part of the clearance argument. 〔Outcome〕A Class I recall and a worldwide pullback. Primary source: FDA, Enforcement Report Z-0966-2025, 2025; link in Appendix B, ph-005.

### Case 4 (Agriculture): A Process Assumption Standing in for a Safety Conclusion

ag-003. Twin Sisters Creamery's Whatcom Blue raw-milk cheese. FDA enforcement report (event 97880 / H-0193-2026; the API record has been verified for this book): a Class I recall of 3,438.2 pounds for potential E. coli O103:H2 contamination. Raw-milk cheese is never pasteurized; its safety rests on process assumptions — and finished-product testing by the state agriculture department went straight through the assumption. Products from the same supplier entered the recall simultaneously through retail-channel bulk slicing (ag-004, Appendix B): the assumption's failure amplified along the dependency chain.

Stop at the decision point: the moment the process was configured to rest safety on a process assumption rather than on finished-product testing — and then every subsequent release that skipped finished-product testing. On the desk: the known risk profile of raw milk, the cost and turnaround of testing, and an operating record reading "this has never caused a problem." 〔Outcome〕Class I recall; the whole product category stood exposed to the same assumption. Primary source: FDA, Enforcement Report event 97880, 2026; link in Appendix B, ag-003.

The four cases span finance, the public sector, drugs, and food. The vehicles of the narrative differ — an offering text, an approval argument, a distribution decision, a process assumption — but the structure is isomorphic: a proposition that had not been adequately verified acquired the status that only evidence should confer. Recognizing them requires no industry knowledge. It requires watching only the ordering between proposition and evidence.

## 4 What Can Be Seen Earliest

Eight weak signals, each with the reason it is usually steamrolled by.

1. The conclusion's release date precedes the existence of a validation plan — the press event is on the calendar before the experiment schedule. The reason it gets waved off: "The market window waits for no one."
2. Meeting minutes begin to say "we have confirmed," and the vehicle of the confirmation — report, data, record — cannot be found. Reason: "It was stated verbally."
3. When new data arrives, the first question in the room is "how do we explain it," and no one asks "does it shake the premise." Reason: "Keep the discussion efficient."
4. The challenger is asked for ten times the evidence; the supporter needs only to restate the position. Reason: "Let's not relitigate."
5. The wording of due-diligence, test, and pilot reports begins to align with the established conclusion: the same fact is characterized more and more identically across different reports. Reason: "The team has reached consensus."
6. "The devil's advocate doesn't understand the business" becomes the standard categorization inside meetings, and those doing the categorizing never test their own business judgment. Reason: "Division of expertise."
7. The appendices of reporting materials grow thicker as the main text grows thinner: evidence is moved out of sight rather than into the argument. Reason: "Leadership reads only conclusions."
8. The original wording of early-version conclusions is silently revised, with no revision record. Reason: "Polishing the phrasing." (Compare the definition-change registry of Chapter 38: revisions to the narrative must leave a trail too.)

Items 5 and 8 together are the signal that the narrative has taken over the paperwork in depth: by this stage the organization's written record has itself become an annex of the narrative, and a later investigation trying to reconstruct the real decision process from documents will meet systematic difficulty.

## 5 How to Prevent It

Device one: tiered release of conclusions. Divide all outward expression into three tiers: hypothesis (explicitly marked unverified), working conclusion (with an evidence list and the conditions under which it would be falsified), and settled conclusion (with independent review on record). Only the third tier may serve as the basis of a commitment. Roadshows and project proposals are outward commitments and may cite only the third tier — or cite the first tier honestly and absorb the corresponding discount. The discount is the price of honesty; an organization unwilling to pay it has announced that it accepts only decorated conclusions. Execution point: the three tier labels go into the header of the document template, where they do not depend on the author's conscience.

Device two: a named falsification owner. For every major conclusion, one person's assignment includes "overturn it": with budget, with data access, with immunity from blame (the role-based form of Chapter 19). The comparison question for a Sizewell-type project: who was authorized to tabulate the complete history of comparable projects? The falsification owner's annual report is not "I checked and found nothing wrong"; it is "here are the counter-evidence items I found this year, and here are the ones that were adopted." A report of zero counter-evidence is itself called into question.

Device three: a symmetric burden-of-proof rule. Write it into meeting rules: proposing and challenging are subject to the same bar of evidence, and precedent is public — whatever level of evidence the last proposing side provided is all the challenging side needs to provide. The chair enforces the rule, and violations go into the minutes. The effect of the rule is that every activation of criterion 3 leaves a mark.

Device four: an overnight clause. Early attribution after an accident, finalization of an offering, a commitment in a project proposal — all wait one night (the cooling-off period of Chapter 7, applied to narrative situations). The overnight rule does not stop liars and zealots; it stops decent, ordinary people — and most organizational failure comes from decent, ordinary people. The clause executes as procedure: the sign-and-send button on any outward-commitment document must be more than twelve hours away from the last edit, enforced by the system.

## 6 Boundaries with Adjacent Mechanisms

Versus normalization of deviance (Chapter 11): normalization operates at the archive layer (a deviation is filed as "acceptable"); this chapter operates at the expression layer (a conclusion is published early). One case can hold both: first the internal filing as "acceptable," then the external telling as "safe." Both are present in the Sizewell replication narrative: the overruns of precedent were filed as "manageable," and the replication hypothesis was published as "low risk."

Versus sunk cost and escalation of commitment (Chapter 27): escalation explains the investment (why we keep going); this chapter explains the expectation (why we are confident). The narrative running ahead is often escalation's preparatory work: once the story is out, exiting becomes breaking a promise. The division between the criteria: if what is on the review table is an alignment problem about return projections, go to Chapter 27; if it is an ordering problem about when the conclusion was published, stay here.

Versus failure of success (Chapter 20): slack is the withering of defensive investment during a no-accident period; this chapter is the swelling of belief during a no-counter-evidence period. The two share one time condition — no bad news arriving — and one antidote: keep an independent post of doubt, and keep feeding it.

## 7 Self-Check: Five Questions

1. Your most recent outward commitment about a project's prospects: was the validation plan complete at the time? If not, which of the three tiers did the commitment belong to?
2. Who in your team is authorized to overturn the mainstream conclusion? When did that person last exercise the authority — and who paid the cost of the exercise?
3. The last time you challenged a superior's conclusion, what burden of proof were you required to meet? Did the proposing side pass the same bar when the conclusion was first stated?
4. Your most confident judgment at this moment: can you write it in the form "I abandon this if the following evidence appears"? If you cannot write it, what is it called in this chapter's terms?
5. Look back at the initial public statement after the most recent accident in your industry: how long between the attribution announcement and the investigation report? How many times did the narrative change in between — and where are the records of the changes?

## 6bis Installing the Devices: Order, Failure Modes, and Cost

Order follows the clock: symmetric burden of proof and graded release must exist before the narrative starts running — they are rules, and a late rule cannot govern a narrative already at speed. The designated refuter is seated at project initiation; the time-gap clause belongs to the contract and public-commitment layer and follows the legal calendar. First ninety days: week one, map the current release paths (who says what to whom at what time) and find where the narrative first congeals; weeks two through four, draft the symmetric-burden rule (the claimant's evidence threshold may not exceed the dissenter's) and trial it in one real review; month two, appoint the first refuters — the people who have historically raised objections in review records are usually a better signal than job titles; month three, write time-gap clauses into the next two outbound documents.

Failure modes. Graded release degrades into "certainty first, details later" — the grade is announced, the qualification lost in the headline. Bind the grade to the channel: unqualified conclusions do not go to external channels, and internal ones carry the label explicitly. The refuter role hollows out (named but unfed), so give it minimum resources — raw-data access plus a fixed speaking slot — and check its written output quarterly. Symmetric burden gets gamed in the subtlest way: the rule is on paper but the "what counts as enough evidence" ruling stays with the claimant. Move the ruling to a third party (chair or independent reviewer) and log the rulings. The time-gap clause dies by exception creep — every project claims specialness — so route exceptions up a level and keep a public exception ledger.

Cost: drafting and one training session for the rules (two days total); half a month per refuter; near zero for the clause. Against ScaleFactor's $103 million collapse, the AI-fraud cases, or Sizewell C's buried precedent, narrative governance is nearly free.

Toolchain: the locator triggers on "public commitment made plus short time pressure"; the mapped release path is the opening move. Question set 13's item 2 ("whose job includes being refuted-against") directly audits the refuter seat. Chapter 39's opening script — assume the death, record without rebuttal — is this chapter's symmetric-burden rule meeting-ized; and Chapter 38's two-column discipline is the same defense after the fact: the harder the visible-at-the-time column, the less room narrative has to rewrite the record.
