# Chapter 27: Escalation of Commitment (Staw)

> What this chapter covers: how money already spent takes the next decision hostage; Staw's classic experiment, the criteria, three field sites, and the design of "exit conditions up front."
> When to return: at project renewal reviews, in motions to add budget, or whenever you hear "let's hang on a little longer."
> Reading time: 50 minutes.

The experimental evidence for the sunk-cost effect is now fifty years old. Barry Staw's 1976 experiment, published in Organizational Behavior and Human Performance ("Knee-deep in the big muddy," volume 16, pages 27–44; the Crossref citation has been verified), asked subjects to play the head of a corporate R&D project: half were told the project had previously failed and the responsibility was theirs; the other half were told responsibility lay with others or with circumstances. Everyone was then given the chance to decide whether to invest more. The result was stable and embarrassing: the group with responsibility for the failure chose to invest significantly more often. Admitting the earlier misjudgement was too expensive, so future money was spent keeping past face alive. Fifty years on, the experiment's conditions can be reproduced verbatim in any boardroom, command post, and project review.

## 1 What It Is

Definition: the sunk-cost effect means decisions driven by past investment rather than future returns — the sheer size of what has already been invested becomes the argument for investing more. Chapter 8 broke escalation into seven steps (the process view: explain it away, shrink it, find a scapegoat, promise more, change the metrics, enlarge the commitment, seal off information). This chapter returns to the decision point itself: why exit is so expensive, psychologically and institutionally, and how to make exit cheaper.

The criteria (all three present, and the escalation structure stands):

① The investment already made is publicly visible (on the books, in buildings, in promises already published);
② The main argument for the new money is "we have already put in so much," not a calculation of incremental returns;
③ Exit is equated with admitting error, and a specific person will pay for the admission.

## 2 How It Grows

Three generating conditions. Condition one: the decision-maker is bound to the project. The project is the decision-maker's political achievement, body of work, or career wager; its failure at the level of identity equals personal failure, and no accounting treatment resolves a problem of identity. Staw's "responsibility is yours" manipulation corresponds, inside an organisation, to the answer to the question "whose project is this?" Condition two: the exit mechanism is absent. The founding document contains no exit conditions, or contains them with an interested party as arbiter; exit then can only happen as a coup — replace the person, replace the line, wait out the term of office — and the organisational cost of coups makes everyone prefer to wait. Condition three: external oversight is weak. No party is both able and willing to ask the same question at every review: if we started from zero today, would we still choose this project? The question costs nothing, and its frequency of appearance is close to zero.

## 3 Field Sites

### Case 1 (Public): After Two Resets

po-004. Britain's High Speed Two (HS2). NAO report (High Speed Two reset, June 2026; reviewed for this book): by the end of March 2026 the Department for Transport and HS2 Ltd had spent 46.8 billion pounds; the project's potential cost runs as high as 102.7 billion pounds; there was a first reset in 2020, Phase Two was cancelled in 2023, and in January 2025 Phase One was reset again, with the timeline pushed beyond 2027. Every reset has been accompanied by a fresh argument for the feasibility of the remainder — and the object of the argument is always "the remainder," never the question "if we started from zero today."

Stop at the decision point: the review cycle of the 2025 reset, in which Phase One's continuation was decided. On the table: the 46.8 billion already spent, the contractual and political cost of cancelling the whole project, the return calculations for the remaining sections — and one question absent from the agenda: what weight should those 46.8 billion carry in deciding whether to continue? The correct answer is zero (sunk costs do not, by definition, affect the optimal decision), and any weight greater than zero is this chapter's mechanism running in plain sight. 〔Outcome〕Phase One continued and the cost ceiling was raised; the evidence chain of escalation is archived in full in the national audit. Primary source: NAO, High Speed Two reset, 2026; link in Appendix B, po-004.

### Case 2 (Aviation): One More Leg and Home

av-025. A DG Aviation LS8-e glider (G-CMMG), 25 May 2025. AAIB investigation (reviewed for this book): after roughly five hours aloft the glider was short of height, and the pilot chose to keep trying for the home airfield rather than land out, finally force-landing in a field; the pilot was seriously injured, including a head injury. Gliding culture has a name for this decision line: the glider's version of "get-there-itis." Five hours already flown, home straight ahead, a landing out meaning a trailer and a world of trouble — every item of investment (time, height, the homeward leg) keeps paying into the account of "one more try." This is a complete specimen of escalation at the individual scale: no committee, no budget meeting; the entire mechanism runs inside one person's single thought, and it is just as lethal.

Stop at the decision point: the moment the height falls below the margin needed to reach home while the outlanding window is still open. On the table: the altimeter, the distance to the field, the options for a landing ground, and the mental account of five hours. 〔Outcome〕Forced landing in a field; the aircraft destroyed and the pilot seriously injured. Primary source: AAIB, 2026; link in Appendix B, av-025.

### Case 3 (Maritime): Sleep Debt and the Voyage

ma-013. The prawn trawler Crystal Stream (LH 147), 6 June 2025, Loch Fyne, Scotland. MAIB report (Report 11/2026; reviewed for this book): the master fell asleep while keeping a single-handed watch and the vessel struck rocks; she was presumed a total loss and the master was slightly injured (the report's casualty table records minor injuries). Over the week before the accident, sleep debt had accumulated continuously. The economics of fishing make every voyage hard to abort: fuel money, the schedule, the market window for the catch — the voyage's sunk and expected costs together turn "continue" into the default, and "continue" presupposes the master still on watch, even on overdrafted sleep.

Stop at the decision point: the morning before the ill-fated voyage sailed. On the table: a week's account of fatigue, the voyage's fixed investment, and the effect of aborting on schedule and income. 〔Outcome〕Struck rocks; presumed total loss. Primary source: MAIB, Report 11/2026, 2026; link in Appendix B, ma-013.

### Evidence Gap Note

Primary evidence for escalation cases is naturally thin: institutions almost never publish the decision chain of "why we did not exit" (a further form of Chapter 2's survival skew — the projects that exited vanish, the projects that escalated leave reports). Escalation cases from wars and large policies are scattered through historical archives and after-the-fact inquiries; within this book's collection scope none has verifiable full text, and the gap is marked honestly. Supplementary collection directions appear in Appendix C (legislative reviews of renewals and long-tail loss research in the insurance industry).

## 4 What Shows Earliest

Six weak signals, each with the reason that usually overrides it.

1. The frequency of "hang on a little longer" and "we've come this far" rises in meetings. Override: "morale."
2. The assumptions of a re-forecast are adjusted toward optimism unilaterally, and the adjuster does not sign. Override: "model update."
3. Someone writes an exit plan; no one puts it on the agenda. Override: "the timing is not ripe."
4. Milestones get redefined: the original target's achievement criteria are replaced with more reachable ones. Override: "a pragmatic adjustment."
5. People who oppose renewal are moved to other projects. Override: "workforce optimisation."
6. The project's external narrative switches from the language of returns to the language of persistence ("strategic value," "we can't give up halfway"). Override: "one message." (The language switch precedes the budget increase; it is the joint where Chapter 13 meets this chapter.)

## 5 How to Defend

Device one: exit conditions up front. The founding document must contain an exit clause: which signals trigger an exit review (quantified), who adjudicates (named), and how much already-spent money is disqualified as an argument at the review (written down). The clause is negotiated at signing, when every party is at their calmest; it is the founding-stage version of Chapter 8's device three. The clause's drafting point: the trigger signals must be externally observable (schedule, external evaluation, third-party milestones) — never "whenever management sees fit."

Device two: an external adjudicator. The adjudicator of the exit review must have no historical binding to the project — never signed its founding papers, never stood on a stage for it — and a tenure covering the project's whole expected life. The adjudicator's sole agenda is answering the zero-based re-ask, not balancing interests.

Device three: the zero-based re-ask. The first agenda item of every review is fixed: "If not a single unit had been invested as of today, would we start this project?" Answer that first; discuss reasons to continue afterwards. What HS2-style reset reviews lack is exactly this procedure: every minute of the reset discussion went to distributing "how to wind down what has been sunk."

Device four: termination rewards. People who "called a stop to their own project" enter the same recognition and promotion channels as the successful. In an organisation without termination rewards, exit clauses automatically decay into text — because the career interest of whoever executes the clause runs against the clause (Chapter 19's alarm logic applies here too: whoever executes the exit must first be protected).

## 6 Boundaries with Adjacent Mechanisms

Against the steps of escalation (Chapter 8): Chapter 8 governs the process (which step you have reached); this chapter governs the decision point (why you are still walking). The self-test order: first grade yourself with Chapter 8, then apply this chapter's exit machinery.

Against narrative before evidence (Chapter 13): escalation's external expression is often completed as narrative ("strategic value"), and narrative-first often preheats the escalation (get the expectation out first, and exit becomes a broken promise). Distinguish by the object: managing expectations goes to Chapter 13; accounting for the investment stays here.

Against normalisation of deviance (Chapter 11): the cost overruns and delays of the escalation period get normalised step by step ("all big projects are like this" — Sizewell's NAO text says exactly this); normalisation is escalation's lubricant, and Chapter 11's ledger, applied to large projects, takes the overruns and delays themselves as its entries.

## 7 Self-Check: Five Questions

1. Which page of your project's founding document holds the exit clause? If there is none, is it still early enough to write one now?
2. In which meeting did you last argue for more budget with "we have already put in so much"? Did the project turn around afterwards?
3. Your project's re-forecasts — who last adjusted the assumptions, and in which direction: how many toward optimism, how many toward pessimism?
4. The last person in your organisation who called a stop to their own project — what position do they hold now?
5. The zero-based re-ask: if not a single unit had been invested today, would you start the project in your hands? Put the answer and your reasons for continuing side by side for one night, and look again in the morning.

## 6bis Installing the Devices: Order, Failure Modes, and Cost

Exit conditions written first — before the money moves; drafted after the money moves, they are the beneficiaries drafting their own exit. The external adjudicator follows (seated before the first escalation), the zero-base question third (a cadence device, running on decision rhythm), termination rewards last (a culture device, cashing in at the first clean exit). First ninety days: week one, inventory the standing long commitments (projects, contracts, positions, technical routes) and audit each for an exit clause — written, adjudicated by whom, conditions observable; three weeks to fix the missing ones; weeks two to four, seat external adjudicators for the three largest commitments (no stake, standing judgment authority, the mandate written into the charter); month two, run the first zero-base question on the oldest commitment (starting from zero today, would we still enter — answer and reasons recorded side by side); month three, draft the termination-reward clause (how a clean exit shows up in appraisal and reputation — clause first, redemption later).

Failure modes. Exit conditions go vague ("review upon major market change" — major, as glossed by whoever wants to continue) — make conditions observable: share, schedule, loss line, numbers with dates, the same falsifiability as Chapter 13. Adjudicators get infected (long tenures, long dinners, and independence starts bonding with the project chemically) — term limits and information quarantine: the adjudicator reads submissions and independently verified data, and never joins the project's working channels. The zero-base question turns rhetorical (asked yearly, answered yearly "yes," the same reasons re-cited) — audit the answers against each other: reason reuse above half triggers mandatory external adjudication. Rewards curdle into humiliation (the clause exists; the redemption is a soft exile) — the first redeemed exiter is the institution's living advertisement: honor the clause to the letter, visibly.

Cost: exit clauses and adjudicators are clause work (half a day to two days per commitment); the zero-base is half a day a year; rewards are usually a culture budget of zero — the reward tends to be reputation and re-employment. Against a rail project reset twice, a startup's $103 million swan song, or a skipper whose routine became a rut — escalation's ledger is never miscomputed, merely unappealable; these devices put a name on the appeals court.

Toolchain: the locator triggers on "sunk investment publicly visible plus exit equaling confession." Question set 27's item 1 ("which section of the charter holds the exit condition") audits the front-running device. Post-mortems with "stopped too late" draft the missing clauses. And the pre-mortem's escalation lane shares one thought with the external adjudicator: the hand an insider cannot bring itself to call stop with needs an outsider's name on it.
