# Chapter 28: Horizon Mismatch; Short-Termism

> What this chapter covers: how the structure of benefits now and costs years later manufactures failure in bulk; the criteria, two field sites, the bill handover, and making long-term liabilities visible.
> When to return: when setting terms of office and appraisal cycles, making multi-year commitments, or evaluating anything that "will only pay off under the next term."
> Reading time: 50 minutes.

Horizon mismatch requires no malice in anyone. Put any normal person into a term of office shorter than the consequence cycle, hand them a ledger that records only the current period, and let the handover carry no unfulfilled obligations: their choices will tilt systematically toward the present, and every tilt will have been rational and defensible at the moment it was made. The disease is in the structure: term of office, ledger, and handover, taken together, guarantee that cross-period costs always arrive after the decision-maker's responsibility has expired. This chapter's difficulty is that first-hand deep cases are the hardest to collect in this domain (the decision chains lie scattered across successive audits and budget documents), so the chapter is built from two skeletal field sites plus an honest evidence-gap note.

## 1 What It Is

Definition: horizon mismatch means a decision's benefits and costs fall on different time scales, while the decision-maker's term of office, appraisal cycle, or accounting frame covers only the benefit side.

The criteria (all three present, and the structure stands):

① Benefits in the current period, costs three years out or longer;
② The decision-maker's term of office is shorter than the cost cycle;
③ No one is accountable for cross-period outcomes: no signatory, no handover ledger (the interface to Chapter 30).

## 2 How It Grows

Three generating conditions. Condition one: the appraisal cycle is set by the term of office. Political terms, executive tenures, fund managers' subscription-and-redemption cycles — all shorter than the cost cycles of infrastructure, education, ecology, and maintenance. The cycle gap is not an anomaly; it is the normal condition of these domains. Condition two: accounting and statistical frames record only the current period. Long-term liabilities stay off this period's statements (technical debt, maintenance arrears, demographic structure); the "invisible liability" constrains no present decision, and accounting's matching principle proves helpless before long-cycle assets. Condition three: handover means dumping the bills. The outgoing office-holder hands over no list of bills, and the successor does not know how much debt they have received (Chapter 30's rupture in its form along the time axis); every handover resets the unfulfilled obligations to zero.

## 3 Field Sites

### Case 1 (Public): A Decade and More of Scope Swings

po-007. Britain's Northern Powerhouse Rail. NAO report (March 2026; reviewed for this book), in its own words: "it has taken over a decade to get to this stage given the significant scope changes during this period," with the delivery of benefits repeatedly deferred. The programme has spanned multiple governments and repeated strategy resets, each administration revising the scope to its own priorities. Each revision was rational and lawful in its moment; the cost is the permanent postponement of delivery day and the repeated write-off of investment already made.

Stop at the decision point: the moment each incoming administration reopens the scope. On the table: the previous administration's promise texts, the present one's priorities, and a constraining document that does not exist — no mechanism requires the new incumbent to inherit the established scope, or to pay a perceptible price for changing it. 〔Outcome〕The cycle of scope changes is recorded by the audit as a structural problem; the delivery of benefits continues to recede. Primary source: NAO, Northern Powerhouse Rail, 2026; link in Appendix B, po-007.

### Case 2 (Manufacturing): This Quarter's Sales and the Boiler Years Later

mn-002. NTI/HTP brand gas boilers (manufactured by Ariston). CPSC recall announcement (reviewed for this book): 4,726 units recalled (2,261 more in Canada), against 34 known reports of condensate trays cracking, in 3 of which the boiler cover detached, and 1 repair technician suffered a jaw fracture. A condensing boiler is installed in a user's home and runs for years: the benefit of market release (sales) falls on installation day, while fatigue cracking of the condensate tray arrives thousands of ignition cycles later — the product's cost cycle is naturally longer than the appraisal cycle of the decisions that developed and sold it. The blank stretch between the length of accelerated-ageing testing and real service life is horizon mismatch's shape in product safety.

Stop at the decision point: at design freeze, the node at which the validation duration for the condensate tray's material and cycle life was fixed. On the table: the testing frame for accelerated ageing, the launch schedule, and the fact that no mechanism required the blank between test duration and real service life to be filled. 〔Outcome〕Recall after 34 cracks; installed units of the same construction remain in users' homes. Primary source: CPSC, 2026; link in Appendix B, mn-002.

### Evidence Gap Note

First-hand deep cases of horizon mismatch are hard to collect in two ways. Political and infrastructure cases scatter their decision chains across successive audits and budget documents, making collection and assembly expensive; corporate cases almost never write their long-term costs (technical debt, maintenance arrears) into a post-mortem — writing it down means leaving the successor a bill, which is precisely this chapter's mechanism covering its own tracks. The A-grade deep cases in this book's library are currently dominated by public audit; supplementary collection directions: legislative renewal reviews and insurance-industry long-tail loss research (Appendix C).

## 4 What Shows Earliest

Six weak signals, each with the reason that usually overrides it.

1. The budget table has no "future costs" column, or one in name only. Override: "the long term cannot be estimated."
2. No comparison table exists of decision-makers' terms of office against consequence cycles. Override: "who would ever build such a table."
3. Key milestones cluster before the term-of-office boundary; every benefit node falls after it. Override: "that is how the plan came out."
4. Key posts change hands mid-project, and the handover contains no unfulfilled obligations. Override: "the process was followed."
5. "The next five-year plan will solve it" becomes the closing line of the current plan. Override: "phased delivery."
6. Maintenance and cultivation spending is listed as flexible and finishes last, year after year. Override: "protect the priorities first." (Chapter 29 plugs in here.)

## 5 How to Defend

Device one: a bill handover system. On leaving a key post, the handover is required to include a "list of unfulfilled obligations": what was promised, what is owed, when each cost falls due, and where the funding sits. The successor signs for the list; missing items are completed by the outgoing side — Chapter 30's handover, done along the time axis. The list's force depends on the seriousness of the signature: signing carries the successor's duty to confirm the list's truth, and a false list is recoverable against.

Device two: make long-term liabilities visible. Technical debt, maintenance arrears, and the long-run costs of demographic and asset ageing are converted each year into note-level items on the current statements and published. The numbers can be disputed; the absence cannot. The point of visibility is not precision — it moves "the things not done" from nothing to something, so that every deferral leaves a citable line.

Device three: a cross-period signatory. Create a post answerable for "the results five years out" (a chief long-term value officer, a heritage asset custodian) whose appraisal metrics are defined entirely beyond the term of office, with tenure protection written into the charter. The mere existence of the post is a direct rebuttal of criterion ②: the term of office may be shorter than the consequence cycle, but the period of responsibility can be longer than the term.

Device four: node-symmetry review. At plan approval, inspect the distribution of milestones: where the gap between decision nodes and benefit nodes exceeds a term of office, require an account of which mechanism guarantees cross-period delivery (a dedicated body, statutory funding, treaty-grade commitment) — and where nothing can account for it, shorten the promise. The review's question is not "can it be done" but "who shows up on the due date."

## 6 Boundaries with Adjacent Mechanisms

Against the invisibility of maintenance (Chapter 29): horizon mismatch explains why maintenance gets cut (the cost of the cut falls outside the term of office); maintenance invisibility explains why cutting it is painless (once cut, nothing shows). The two chapters read different sides of the same cases: the delay of the Westminster refurbishment (po-005) belongs to Chapter 29; the multi-term structure behind the delay belongs to this chapter.

Against sunk cost and escalation (Chapter 27): escalation pushes costs further onto successors (a reset project piles on new accounts), and horizon mismatch guarantees the successor cleans up without anyone auditing the predecessor. The criterion: if decision-makers had to "show up" in some form and bear the consequences, the structure would correct itself; what cannot correct itself is this chapter.

Against misaligned incentives (Chapter 17): a term of office shorter than the consequence cycle is misaligned incentives in the time dimension. Chapter 17's externality accounting, in cross-period settings, takes exactly the form of this chapter's bill handover and visible long-term liabilities.

## 7 Self-Check: Five Questions

1. Of your organisation's current decisions, which one's cost falls due latest, and in which year? When it falls due, where will the people deciding today be?
2. How much of your own term remains? Of the things you are pushing forward, how many deliver their benefits after your term ends?
3. At the last handover of your post, did you receive a "list of unfulfilled obligations"? Did you hand one over?
4. In your organisation's budget, in which column is "the money that must be spent five years from now" recorded today? If there is no such column, whose surprise is that money becoming?
5. Would you be willing to come back in five years and give your successor an account of the decisions you are approving now? List the ones you would not.

## 6bis Installing the Devices: Order, Failure Modes, and Cost

Making far-term liabilities visible goes first — the accounting device; unbilled debts make every later device moot. The bill-handover template follows (it keeps the debts from evaporating at transition), the cross-term signatory third (one standing name per largest bill, promising visibility across terms), and the node-symmetry audit last (a transition-season special: outgoing books and incoming books counted face to face). First ninety days: weeks one to three, build the first far-term ledger — maintenance backlogs, equipment ages, technical debt, talent gaps, promised contractual obligations, five tables; amounts and due dates filled where fillable, and where not fillable, say so; month two, stand up the handover template (a standing annex at every transition, handover, and project move: the bill list plus a receipt line); month three, appoint a cross-term signatory for the largest single bill (a governance member signing a standing commitment: "this bill stays visible through my term").

Failure modes. Liabilities get discounted to zero by optimism ("we'll deal with it when it comes") — lock the discount rate: one conservative house rule, revisions requiring a governance signature. Handovers go formal (receipt signed, list unread) — substance-test the receipt: the incomer writes "known" against each item and flags the three most urgent; no flags, no valid receipt. Signatories go nominal, the same way Chapter 18's owners do — bind the name to an agenda seat: the quarterly meeting delivers the bill update to them, and not-reading is itself on record. Symmetry audits miss the moment (held after the dust settles, books gone cold) — front-load them: start in the公示 period, the announcement window, and make the audit report the incomer's first reading assignment.

Cost: the first ledger runs one to two weeks (the data lives in many drawers; gathering is the cost); the template is clause work; the signatory is agenda cost; the symmetry audit, a day or two per transition. Against a palace whose repair plan sat thirteen years unschemed, a rail program re-scoped across a decade, or a boiler whose aging bill landed on the end user — the entire mechanism runs on one vacuum: nobody sits in the future's chair. All four devices build the future a proxy.

Toolchain: the locator triggers on "long time pressure plus a term shorter than the consequence cycle." Question set 28's item 5 ("does a bill list exist for your successor") audits the handover. Chapter 38's three-year test shares this chapter's stance: judge today's decisions with a reader three years out. And every pre-mortem's footer look-back date is this chapter's minimal device — each pre-mortem carries a cross-term verification promise in its own last line.
