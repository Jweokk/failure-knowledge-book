# Chapter 17: Misaligned Incentives; Principal–Agent Problem

> What this chapter covers: how every layer's rational choice adds up into a loss for the whole; the failure structure of the principal–agent chain, four scenes on the ground, and three ways to write externalities back into evaluations.
> When to return: when you are designing an evaluation scheme or signing an outsourcing and agency contract — or when you find that "everyone did the right thing, and the result still went wrong."
> Reading time: 50 minutes.

Misaligned incentives is the most counterintuitive mechanism in this book, because it asks you to accept an uncomfortable fact: the organization that failed may contain no bad person — it may contain no one who made an error at all. The auditor maintains the client relationship by business logic; the tax office completes its task by metric logic; the regional airline presses costs down by market logic; the marketplace seller races listing speed by platform logic. Each layer's choice is correct on its own ledger, and the loss that their sum produces is paid by people outside the chain. This chapter supplies no villains; it supplies only structure. And precisely because of that, its countermeasures are all structural: change the evaluation, change the contract, change who owns the verification — not swap the people.

## 1 What It Is

Definition: misaligned incentives is a structural disease of principal–agent chains: every participant optimizes the quantity it is evaluated on, and the sum of the parties' choices damages the whole. Its identification signature is unusually clean — three questions in place of formal criteria:

1. Take each layer's choice on its own: is it rational within that layer's own goal?
2. Has anyone violated any written rule?
3. Sum all the choices: on whose head does the loss land — and does that person hold a seat at the decision table?

The third question is the core of the diagnosis. When the loss lands outside the chain — on passengers, investors, taxpayers, users — no link inside the chain has any motive to change its behavior on that account. The people positioned to discover the problem are exactly the people who benefit from it (as Chapter 37's question set puts it): when the beneficiary runs the verification, the problem persists indefinitely in the form "verified — does not exist."

## 2 How It Grows

Three generating conditions. Condition one: the externality cannot be priced at its own layer. Your decision damages the ledger of another link in the chain — audit distortion damages investors, over-collection damages enterprises, fatigued flying damages passengers — and your own ledger cannot see it. The invisibility is not a management lapse; it is the designed result of the accounting categories. Condition two: the evaluation measures only what the layer itself controls, and cross-layer consequences have no owner. No role signs for "the outcome of the chain as a whole," so every layer's optimization contributes its share of error to the whole (Diffusion of Responsibility, Chapter 18, takes over from here). Condition three: tenure is shorter than the consequence cycle (Chapter 28). When the price is paid by successors or by third parties outside the system, the current-period rational choice keeps tilting — and the successor of the person who tilted inherits the same tilt.

Misaligned incentives is the principal disease of the incentive layer in Chapter 9's three-layer model. Almost all of its technical implementations run through metrics — which is why Chapter 12 is its standing complication. And almost all of its defenses require someone to call a halt — which is why Chapter 18 is its standing precursor.

## 3 The Pattern in Four Domains

### Case 1 (Finance): Paying the People to Be Strict — with the Auditee's Money

fi-008. The accounting firm Zhongshen Zhonghuan, in its audits of Yuebo Power's 2019–2021 annual reports. The China Securities Regulatory Commission's administrative penalty decision (〔2026〕56; the page has been verified for this book) found the audit had not been performed with due diligence and that the issued audit reports contained false records: the CSRC confiscated the audit fee income of 3,679,245.18 yuan and imposed an equal fine, and the three signing certified public accountants were fined 400,000, 300,000, and 200,000 yuan respectively. The market structure of auditing is this case's entire structure: the auditor's fee is paid by the party being audited, while the true consumers of the audit product — the readers of the financial statements — neither participate in setting the price nor can inspect the goods. At the moment "keep the client" conflicts with "hold the line against the client," the direction of the structure's tilt was already fixed — fixed on the day the engagement was signed.

Stop at the decision point: the moment some verification in the audit program is compressed, or some anomaly is waved through. On the desk: the cost of the procedure, the survival of the client relationship, and the accumulated experience that "everything waved through before turned out fine." 〔Outcome〕Confiscation plus an equal fine. The same issuer subsequently failed in the same way under a different firm (Zhongshen Asia Pacific, fi-009, Appendix B): the structural disease relapsed on the same chain, with one firm replaced in between — which is precisely what rules out the "quality of individual personnel" explanation. Primary source: CSRC 〔2026〕56, 2026; link in Appendix B, fi-008.

### Case 2 (Public Sector): Over-Collection under a Revenue Target

po-008. The National Audit Office of China, audit of central budget execution for fiscal 2025 (the report PDF has been verified for this book): tax and customs departments over-collected or prematurely collected corporate income tax, VAT, and other levies from 1,954 enterprises, in the amount of 40.427 billion yuan; 21 provinces and municipalities inflated fiscal revenue by 59.651 billion yuan by booking treasury funds as expenditures and then routing them back; and 4,976 enterprises evaded 10.935 billion yuan in taxes through irregular invoicing.

Stop at the decision point: the opening of the budget year, when revenue targets are decomposed level by level and tied to evaluations. On the desk: the revenue target, the real tax base of the enterprises within the jurisdiction, and what completing the task means for everyone's evaluation. Over-collection and premature collection violated nothing in the collector's own layer's goal — it was the over-achievement of that goal. The metric wanted numbers, and the numbers arrived. The cost was booked to the cash flow of enterprises and to the health of the economy — an account that appears nowhere in the collector's evaluation table. 〔Outcome〕The audit report named the findings publicly; rectification entered the following year's budget process. Primary source: National Audit Office of China, 2026; link in Appendix B, po-008.

### Case 3 (Aviation): The Chain Where Every Layer Saved a Little

av-032. Colgan Air Flight 3407 (Continental Connection), February 12, 2009, Clarence Center, New York. NTSB formal report (AAR-10/01; the PDF has been downloaded and verified for this book): on a night approach, an error of control followed the stall warning, and the aircraft stalled into a house; 49 people aboard and 1 on the ground died. Embedded in the report's factor chain is an economic structure: the regional carrier operated for the mainline airline under a pay-per-departure structure, with the mainline brand and the regional capacity kept in two separate ledgers. The interaction among pilot pay levels, commuting-style scheduling, and fatigue is explicitly discussed in the report: the captain had commuted a long distance to reach base that day, and both fatigue management and the scheduling rules were compliant on their face. The company optimized capacity cost; the pilots optimized their personal living arrangements; the regulator set its defenses by carrier class. Every layer's choice made sense on its own books.

Stop at the decision point: the operating day on which the scheduling and fatigue-management rules took effect for a commuting crew. On the desk: the roster, the commuting legs, rest rules met on paper — and the question that no party was required to answer: how much safety margin this combination was worth on tonight's approach. 〔Outcome〕50 dead; the accident drove revisions to fatigue rules and to captain experience requirements. Primary source: NTSB, AAR-10/01, 2010; link in Appendix B, av-032.

### Case 4 (Manufacturing): The Cost of Compliance on a Platform Shelf

mn-004. The SOOWERY eight-drawer fabric dresser. CPSC recall notice (the page has been verified for this book): about 138 units recalled for violating the mandatory safety standard for clothing storage units (the requirements of the STURDY Act), after roughly nine months of sales on the Amazon channel, with no injury reports. On third-party platforms, listing speed is the seller's lifeline, and compliance verification is a cost the seller pays out of its own pocket; the gap between the platform's spot checks and the mandatory standard is filled in by each seller's voluntary diligence. The other end of the same structure is the consumer: they cannot see the absence of a test report, exactly as the reader of an audit report cannot see the compression of audit procedures.

Stop at the decision point: the operating window before the batch goes up for listing with the compliance documents not yet complete. On the desk: the sales window's timing, the turnaround and cost of testing, the actual state of similar goods already on the platform. 〔Outcome〕138 units recalled; the structural risk of similar small-batch imported goods remains in the channel. Primary source: CPSC, 2026; link in Appendix B, mn-004.

## 4 What Can Be Seen Earliest

Seven weak signals, each with the reason it is usually steamrolled by.

1. The phrase "we acted in accordance with the contract / the regulations" appears with abnormal density in meeting minutes. The reason it gets waved off: "Nothing wrong with following rules."
2. Every piece of bad news for a given role originates in another link's ledger. Reason: "That is the division of labor."
3. The beneficiary of some problem also serves as its verifier, and the verification conclusion reads "no issues" year after year. Reason: "It is running normally."
4. The makers of high-externality decisions never come into contact with the people who bear them. Reason: "Hierarchical division of labor."
5. The evaluation metrics fight one another, and every fighting side is winning. Reason: "Multi-dimensional evaluation."
6. The same structural disease recurs across different suppliers or divisions, and the handling each time is to replace the people. Reason: "An execution problem." (If replacing people worked, the second occurrence would not happen.)
7. In contract negotiations, the time spent on risk clauses is far shorter than the time spent on price clauses. Reason: "Price is the business."

## 5 How to Prevent It

Device one: externality accounting. Write the foreseeable effects of the layer's own decisions on other parties of the chain into the layer's own evaluation. Audit firms are evaluated retrospectively on subsequent penalties and litigation (the CSRC penalty is an instance of an externality returning to account: the fine priced the consideration received for the audit fee). The quality of compliant collection enters the revenue agency's evaluation weights. Whatever cannot be written as a quantitative term is written as a qualitative item with mandatory per-instance reporting.

Device two: a signatory for the whole result. Every multi-link chain names one role answerable only to the whole — the audit engagement partner answerable to the readers of the statements, the collection quality officer answerable to the taxpayer's experience, the chief of operations control answerable to passenger safety — and that role's evaluation is tied to no layer's output. An empty title achieves nothing: this person must hold the authority to halt (Chapter 18), and the halt must not be subject to performance evaluation by any party within the layer (the protection interface of Chapter 19).

Device three: beneficiary recusal from verification. Whoever benefits from a question may not serve as the verifier of that question; where such an arrangement already exists, it is replaced by external sampling. The entire institutional history of audit independence is the implementation record of this one clause — and its failures always begin with an exception: "just this once is special; let the in-house team review it first."

Device four: a structure-review trigger. When the same failure mode recurs at two or more suppliers or divisions, mandatory escalation to a structure review: examine the contract and the evaluation scheme, not the people. Recurrence is the incentive layer speaking: a problem that yields to personnel change does not recur, and a problem that recurs will never yield to personnel change.

## 6 Boundaries with Adjacent Mechanisms

Versus goal displacement (Chapter 12): misaligned incentives is the interest structure; goal displacement is its technical implementation. The division of labor between the criteria: if what needs changing is the interest structure (who bears the consequence), go to Chapter 17; if the interest structure is sound but the measurement is crooked, stay in Chapter 12. The two often need work at the same time, and the order is: plug the structure first (beneficiary recusal from verification), then repair the measurement.

Versus diffusion of responsibility (Chapter 18): dilution is nobody being responsible (the signature chain has no owner); misalignment is someone being responsible in the wrong direction (the evaluation points the wrong way). The diagnostic question: give the responsibility to one specific person — does the situation change? If yes, it is dilution. If giving it changes nothing (the person's evaluation forces him to act against it), it is misalignment.

Versus horizon mismatch (Chapter 28): when the current-period tilt is amplified by short tenure, the horizon dimension stacks on top. The criterion question: extend the decision-maker's tenure to three times its length — would the choices change? If yes, the primary disease is in Chapter 28; if no, the primary disease is in this chapter.

## 7 Self-Check: Five Questions

1. Write down the evaluation goal of every link in your chain, and see where their sum points. Does it point at the goal of the whole?
2. In your organization, for which problems is the verifier also the beneficiary? Can you list three?
3. Has one of your decisions ever damaged another link's ledger? Did that damage appear in any evaluation document of yours?
4. If your role were given one added line — "answerable only to the result of the whole" — which existing evaluation item would you have to delete to make it fit?
5. Pick an old incident in which "every layer was blameless," write out each layer's rational choice in turn, and put the summed result on the last line. That last line is the incentive layer's physical examination report.

## 6bis Installing the Devices: Order, Failure Modes, and Cost

The structural-review trigger goes first — it is the detector that finds which clause carries the angle. Externality accounting follows (it prices the angle), the whole-outcome signer third (it gives governance a handle), and the last two are domain fittings — platform-funded spot audits for platform-shaped organizations, recusal registers for verification-heavy ones. First ninety days: week one, collect every layer's appraisal scheme and read them side by side — the first pass usually finds the first angle by itself; weeks two through four, book the single most painful externality in kind, aiming for one true entry rather than full coverage; month two, seat the whole-outcome signer (a governance-layer name unattached to line appraisals); month three, switch on the platform audit or the recusal register by organizational shape.

Failure modes. Platform-funded audits leak back (the fee is nominally platform money and really returns to the auditee through another line) — audit the budget separation annually. Externality accounting stalls on pricing disputes ("you can't price this") — decouple booking from pricing: quantity first (what was emitted, who absorbed it), price may wait, quantity may not. The whole-outcome signer degenerates into a designated fall person — bind the name to powers, not ceremonies: stop-work authority and budget veto arrive with the signature (Chapter 18's single-responsible-person clause is this device in contract form). Recusal self-assesses ("I don't think I'm conflicted") — third-party review of the register, plus random independent substitutes for recused verifications.

Cost: the side-by-side read is a one-time two to three days plus half a day a quarter; minimal externality entries run an hour or two a month; the signer is one governance resolution; platform funding is a real budget line that mostly moves money that already existed. Against CNIL's €325 million, the paired audit failures, or children's sleepwear economics, incentive governance is rounding error.

Toolchain: this is the locator's strongest single-parameter trigger (systemic misalignment lights rows 12, 17, 19, 27), and the side-by-side read is the opening move. Question set 17's item 1 audits it. Post-mortems whose three-layer chains break at the incentive layer install devices here; externalities that won't fit a device get booked in kind via Device 2. And the pre-mortem's escalation lane — the largest class of unclaimable deaths — should be addressed to exactly this chapter's whole-outcome signer: the two devices join there.
