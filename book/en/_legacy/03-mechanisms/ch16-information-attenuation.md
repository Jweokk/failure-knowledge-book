# Chapter 16: Information Attenuation; MUM Effect

> What this chapter covers: how bad news gets gently reworded on its way up through the levels; the psychology and the structure of the MUM effect, three criteria, four scenes on the ground, and the design points of an information channel that bypasses the hierarchy.
> When to return: when you notice that the leadership's read of the situation visibly disagrees with the front line's; before you write a report saying "overall under control"; when you are designing a reporting system.
> Reading time: 50 minutes.

Bad news travels through an organization in a way that one sentence captures: with every floor it climbs, it changes into a more presentable coat. "Both units have major hidden safety defects" becomes, at the department manager's level, "there is a certain degree of risk." At the director's level it becomes "worth attention." At the executive vice president's level it becomes "overall under control." By the time it reaches the person who can actually release the money, it no longer looks like itself. Organizational psychology has a cruel and accurate name for this: the MUM effect — Mutual Unwillingness to Uphold bad news upward. The cruelty sits in the word "mutual": the sender is unwilling to report, the receiver is unwilling to hear, and the two collaborate to complete every step of the softening. Not one step requires anyone to lie.

## 1 What It Is

Definition: information attenuation in hierarchy is the systematic dilution of bad news as it climbs level by level: each layer makes small wording choices, for self-protection or out of consideration for the receiver, and the layers compound until the decision-maker's version and the front-line version have drifted far apart. Attenuation requires nobody to lie — that is its essential difference from falsification, and the reason it is so hard to govern: you cannot discipline a behavior that is composed entirely of compliant word choices.

Three activating criteria (the mechanism has started if any one of them appears):

1. The same piece of bad news reaches different levels in versions of different intensity (laying the minutes of each level side by side will confirm it);
2. The decision layer's judgment of the situation has diverged observably from the front-line version (the front line laughs when it hears the leadership's assessment);
3. Bypassing levels has been defined in the organization's language as disloyalty, as immaturity — or at minimum as "talk to your supervisor first."

## 2 How It Grows

Three generating conditions. Condition one: the reporter's interests are bound to the reported. When the severity of bad news directly determines the reporter's evaluation, bonus, and prospects, the wording will necessarily tilt toward the light. This is Chapter 17's misaligned incentives projected onto the information layer: the message gets colored by the reporter's interests, not by the facts. Condition two: many layers, narrow bandwidth. Every layer is one act of compressed retelling, and compression is lossy — with the direction of the loss guided by the reporter's interests. A layer is not a relay station for information; it is a filter. Each one filters out only a little: too little for anyone to be held accountable, enough to be lethal in accumulation. Condition three: the receiving mechanism for bad news is missing. The organization maintains only channels that reward good news — victories, highlights, over-fulfilled targets — and no channel through which problems can be reported with immunity. Where the receiving end sets no place for bad news, the sending end trims to fit.

The stealthiness of attenuation deserves a second telling: every step is small. From "major hidden accident defect" to "a certain degree of risk" to "overall under control," each wording choice can survive examination in any single meeting. Only by laying five levels of minutes side by side does the landslide become visible. Side-by-side reading is the core action of this chapter's defense.

## 3 The Pattern in Four Domains

### Case 1 (Chemicals): The Information Chain after the Report Came In

ch-007. The Sinopec Donghuang products pipeline leak and explosion, Qingdao section, November 22, 2013. The State Council accident investigation team's report, in full (published on the website of the Ministry of Emergency Management; the full text has been verified for this book): 62 dead, 136 injured, direct economic losses of 751.72 million yuan. The report devotes a complete chapter to reconstructing the chain of leak reporting and response after crude oil escaped into a municipal culvert: information was passed and interpreted among the pipeline enterprise, the local government, and the on-site response forces; each party held one facet of the event; no party held the whole; and the response decisions were made on incomplete information — until sparks from a hydraulic breaking hammer perforating the culvert cover ignited the oil vapor inside it.

Stop at the decision point: the window between confirmation of the leak and the level-by-level determination and execution of the response plan. The information on the desk differed by party: the enterprise knew the leaked product was crude oil and its magnitude; the locality knew the culvert's routing and the population along it; the site knew the progress of the perforation work. The three pieces of information were never assembled on any one table. 〔Outcome〕The response operations ignited the vapor; the report carries the complete accountability list (15 people referred to judicial organs, 48 given Party and administrative discipline). Primary source: State Council "11·22" accident investigation team, 2014; link in Appendix B, ch-007.

### Case 2 (Maritime): The New Procedure That Stopped at the Paper

ma-007. The ro-ro cargo ship Laureline, July 13, 2024, Port of Purfleet, UK. MAIB report (Report 7/2026; the page has been verified for this book): a sailor was crushed by a trailer and killed during vehicle deck lashing operations. The investigation found that a new safety procedure for vehicle deck operations had not been understood by the crew and was being executed in distorted form; supervision was assessed as ineffective; and the trailer driver did not know where the crew members were. On the management side the procedure had been drafted and signed; on the execution side it had never landed. Between issuance and accident, "the procedure has been understood and is being followed" was management's default information — and that information was never once verified.

Stop at the decision point: the moment after the new procedure was published and before the first operation conducted under it. On the desk: the procedure text already distributed, training records on file, the next operation shift scheduled — and one missing item: no mechanism anywhere answers the question "did the people on board actually understand it." 〔Outcome〕One death. Primary source: MAIB, Report 7/2026, 2026; link in Appendix B, ma-007.

### Case 3 (Public Sector): The Conceptual Schedule

po-002. The U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs electronic health record modernization program (VA EHRM). GAO report (GAO-26-109393, September 2026; the page has been verified for this book): the program plans to complete deployment at roughly 170 sites by 2031; deployment was paused in 2023 after poor user reviews and restarted at the end of 2024. GAO pointed out that the VA offered Congress only a conceptual schedule, with no cost estimate and no detailed schedule documents; of 18 recommendations, only 4 had been implemented. This is attenuation in its public-administration form: the information passed to overseers is deliberately set at a "reportable" granularity. It is not concealment — it is choosing a mode of presentation that cannot be questioned.

Stop at the decision point: the reporting season in which a conceptual schedule was chosen, rather than a detailed plan, as the answer to congressional oversight. On the desk: the deployment's real risks and setbacks, the expectation that a detailed plan, once written, would be interrogated line by line — and the fact that "conceptual" is fully legitimate under procedure. 〔Outcome〕The oversight body recorded the gap in information framing in a dedicated report. Primary source: GAO, GAO-26-109393, 2026; link in Appendix B, po-002.

### Case 4 (Manufacturing): Six Hundred Incident Reports, Never Summed

mn-001. HAIARA power banks (Mitrytech, Amazon channel). CPSC recall notice (the page has been verified for this book): about 11,000 power banks recalled; the firm already knew of 600 reports of fire and other thermal incidents, among them 3 burn injuries and 9 incidents of property damage. Six hundred incidents are not one piece of bad news; they are six hundred. Each one, alone, was small (one overheating, one smoking); each one completed its own reporting path; and the action called "summing" took place at no level of the organization — until the regulator did the summing.

Stop at the decision point: the window in which the Nth incident report arrived and the sales decision went untouched. On the desk: the single-case severity of each incident, the channel pressure of the product being on sale, the cost of a recall — and one number that no one was responsible for watching: the cumulative report count. 〔Outcome〕The CPSC intervened and the products were recalled. Primary source: CPSC, 2026; link in Appendix B, mn-001.

## 4 What Can Be Seen Earliest

Six weak signals, each with the reason it is usually steamrolled by.

1. In reporting materials, the share of adjectives rises while the share of numbers falls. The reason it gets waved off: "Give leadership the key points."
2. The phrases "overall under control" and "the trend is improving" appear with rising frequency. Reason: "Stabilize expectations."
3. The same event appears at different levels' minutes with different intensity. Reason: "Each level has its own focus."
4. The front line's raw records — work orders, recordings, ledgers — do not match the reported version. Reason: "The report refined it."
5. A bypass information channel exists but has never been used; the user count is zero. Reason: "Which proves there are no problems."
6. The leadership's judgment of front-line conditions makes the front line laugh out loud. Reason: "Leaders see the big picture."

Item 3 is the operational starting point: it requires no new institution of any kind — only someone willing to photocopy two sets of minutes onto the same page.

## 5 How to Prevent It

Device one: read the minutes side by side. Once a quarter, lay the reporting minutes on the same topic from different levels out on one timeline and mark every point where the intensity shifts. This is the only way to see "every step small, the sum enormous." Execution points: the comparison is performed by someone who reports directly to the very top (they stand outside the attenuation chain), and the result is the first agenda item of the current period's regular meeting, with no pre-screening.

Device two: a raw-document channel direct to senior readers. The front line's work orders, incident flash reports, and original customer complaints get a channel that reaches designated senior readers without polishing; each month the named reader signs a reading log. The channel's value lies not in volume but in its existence: once report writers know the raw documents get seen, their wording tightens on its own. Capacity design point: the amount a senior person genuinely reads in a month is finite. Better to sample — twenty random items a month — than to pile up the full flow, because full volume equals nothing.

Device three: a reporting immunity clause. Write into policy: whoever reports bad news in accordance with the facts is not held accountable for the message itself (accountability attaches only to concealment and fabrication). Without this clause, the information collected by devices one and two dries up at the source: side-by-side reading will reveal that every layer is rewriting, and the first layer to rewrite is the layer that did not dare write things straight.

Device four: quantifier discipline. Internal reporting bans unquantified modifiers ("a certain degree," "considerable," "overall"); every modifier must carry a number or state "not quantified." The lesson of the Qingdao case is not that some particular layer misspoke — it is that the response decision was made on information that was never fully assembled. Quantifier discipline cannot eliminate that problem, but it exposes the problem's location: wherever the unquantified modifiers start appearing, that is where the attenuation begins.

## 6 Boundaries with Adjacent Mechanisms

Versus handover rupture (Chapter 30): attenuation is vertical information loss (up and down the chain); rupture is horizontal information loss (between the two parties to a handover). The Laureline's new procedure (case 2 of this chapter) and the two-ship assumption of ma-008 look like the same species; the criterion is direction: when a message fails to travel from top to bottom, that is attenuation; when it fails to travel between peers, that is rupture.

Versus whistleblower suppression (Chapter 19): this chapter is the channel problem (how messages deform); Chapter 19 is the cost problem (what happens to the person who speaks). Which to examine first? If the channel carries volume but the volume is distorted — fix the channel. If the channel carries nothing at all — examine the cost first.

Versus goal displacement (Chapter 12): metric distortion is attenuation in structured form — the step that compresses "risk" into a "rating" is one institutionalized wording choice. GAO's comparison audit of the federal IT dashboard (case 2 of Chapter 12) is likewise the public-sector version of this chapter's device one, side-by-side reading.

## 7 Self-Check: Five Questions

1. Take the three levels of minutes from your organization's most recent major matter and read them side by side: at which level does the intensity begin to shift?
2. In the last report you signed off, how many unquantified modifiers were there?
3. In your organization, who has read the original text of a raw complaint or an incident flash report? When did they last go to the front line to verify anything?
4. If tomorrow you wrote one piece of bad news, unedited, into the weekly report — what would happen? What does that answer tell you?
5. How old is your picture of front-line conditions? When did you last spend half a day on site?

## 6bis Installing the Devices: Order, Failure Modes, and Cost

Side-by-side minutes go first — they need only existing paper, cost nothing to build, and the first read usually shocks the readers enough to buy political cover for everything after. The raw-document channel follows (its form follows the organization: boards on the floor, system permissions in the office), quantity-word discipline third (a language habit, built in reviews and post-mortems), and the disclaimer clause last (it changes document templates, whose cycle is slow anyway). First ninety days: week one, the first side-by-side (the latest cross-layer report against the floor's version; the difference list comes out in the meeting); weeks two to six, open one channel with a named reader — smallest form: a monthly one-pager, written in rotation by the front line, reaching governance unedited; month two, enforce quantity-word discipline in every review (adjectives become numbers, or declare themselves unavailable); month three, convert one high-frequency template's hedges ("expected," "basically," "normal") into fill-in fields.

Failure modes. Side-by-side reading gets sample-biased toward flattering reports — governance picks the samples at random, unannounced. The channel gets pre-screened into one more filter ("that doesn't go up there"), so hold the no-preview rule with a narrow negative list (personal attacks and confidentiality violations only) plus the reader's direct receipt. Quantity discipline gets gamed by lying numbers — hedging adjectives vanish and numbers start lying instead (selective baselines, movable denominators) — so bind it to caliber registration: a number whose caliber changed without registration is processed as an adjective. Disclaimers go generic ("the usual caveats apply") and die by invisibility — require each report's disclaimer to name its own known blind spots; boilerplate is banned.

Cost: half a day per quarter for the side-by-side; one rotating hour a month per person for the channel; one extra question per review for quantity discipline; two hours one-time per template. Twenty to thirty hours a year — among the cheapest chapters in the book. Against Qingdao (seven reporting layers), the lost chest X-ray follow-up, or the VA's 4-of-18 recommendations implemented, the damage was never the attenuation itself — it was the top deciding on the attenuated version.

Toolchain: the locator triggers on "multi-team scale plus appraisals riding on reports"; the side-by-side is the opening move. Question set 16's item 3 ("which way is the adjective-to-number ratio drifting") audits the discipline. Post-mortems consume the devices directly: the visible-at-the-time column is only as good as the raw-document channel's back catalog. And the pre-mortem material pack's second item — the last three ledger entries — is this chapter's idea in action: death talk must anchor to unattenuated paper.
