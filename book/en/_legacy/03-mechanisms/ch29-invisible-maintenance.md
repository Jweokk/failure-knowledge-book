# Chapter 29: Invisibility of Maintenance; Deferred Maintenance

> What this chapter covers: why maintenance is always the first thing cut, and why the arrears explode years later all at once; four field sites, converting arrears into liabilities, and a wall around the maintenance budget.
> When to return: in budget-cut discussions, at approvals for equipment "running with a known defect," and after any "sudden" failure.
> Reading time: 50 minutes.

Maintenance is the only category of work whose success is that nothing happens. With the gear changed and without it, today's machine turns just as smoothly; with the seal repaired and without, today's wall is just as dry. Its output is "the thing that did not happen," and "the thing that did not happen" is the only cost line whose value cannot be verified in the current period. So whenever budgets tighten, maintenance is always the first line cut — not because anyone is incompetent, but because it is the only line whose cutting shows nothing today. The arrears compound at equipment age as interest; the bill is delivered years later in a lump, and the form of delivery is usually breakdown, outage, collapse, or explosion, which the public and the decision-makers call "an accident."

## 1 What It Is

Definition: the invisibility of maintenance means that between maintenance's output (availability, service life, safety) and its input lies a gap of time and probability, so that no appraisal cycle can measure its elasticity. Deferred maintenance thereby becomes the shared hidden liability of every kind of organisation — governments, companies, households.

Three trigger criteria (if any one appears, the mechanism has already started):

① When maintenance is done well, nothing shows, and the organisation has no mechanism for entering "nothing went wrong" into any book (the interface to Chapter 20, device one);
② When budgets tighten, the maintenance line is cut first and restored last;
③ The arrears' consequences arrive concentrated as sudden failures, which the public and the decision-makers call "accidents."

## 2 How It Grows

Three generating conditions. Condition one: no output metric. Between maintenance's output and its input lie time and probability, so elasticity within an appraisal cycle is zero; with no output ledger (Chapter 20), maintenance is scored as "no output" in every budget round. Condition two: external invisibility. The interior state of equipment — depth of corrosion, remaining lubrication, ageing insulation, degraded seals — is invisible to users, visitors, and auditors alike; what shows poorly is naturally weak in resource allocation, and maintenance is the least visible of all asset work. Condition three: the decision-maker's term of office is shorter than the arrears' maturity (Chapter 28's time condition): the bill for cutting maintenance always lands on the next person, whose appraisal does not cover it either.

## 3 The Mechanism in Four Domains

### Case 1 (Aviation): The Debt Loaded into the Cargo Hold

av-035. ValuJet Flight 592, DC-9-32 (N904VJ), 11 May 1996, Florida. NTSB final report (AAR-97/06; the PDF was downloaded and reviewed for this book): a maintenance contractor loaded depleted chemical oxygen generators into the hold as ordinary spares; they activated in the impact and started a fire, and the aircraft fell into the Everglades with all 110 aboard (2 pilots, 3 flight attendants, 105 passengers) lost — the report's own words confirm: "Both pilots, the three flight attendants, and all 105 passengers were killed." The maintenance debt in this accident was made physical: the "product of maintenance," which should have been safely dissipated or properly disposed of, took off with the airliner as cargo. The chain of arrears ran through three layers: the contractor's disposal process, a category gap in the airworthiness rules for maintenance residues, and the loading cross-check at handover.

Stop at the decision point: the contractor's stretch of work labelling, packing, and tendering the oxygen generators for shipment. On the table: the condition tags on the items, the process gap in the disposal of the parcels, and that day's loading sheet. 〔Outcome〕110 people lost; the report drove multiple revisions to dangerous-goods and maintenance-residue management. Primary source: NTSB, AAR-97/06, 1997; link in Appendix B, av-035.

### Case 2 (Aviation): Assembly Without a Reinstallation Record

av-030. Alaska Airlines Flight 1282, Boeing 737-9 (N704AL), 5 January 2024, Portland. NTSB investigation report (AIR-25-04; the PDF was downloaded and reviewed for this book): in the climb the left mid-exit door plug detached, the cabin depressurised rapidly, and the aircraft returned safely; 7 passengers suffered minor injuries, 169 were uninjured, and the airframe was substantially damaged (the report states on page 10 that the four restraining bolts "were not located"). The report's safety issue points directly at the state of work on that door during final assembly at the Renton factory: the plug had been opened for rivet rework, and on reinstallation the locating bolts were missing and no work record existed. The form of the arrears here is "the blank in the record": one opening of a critical section triggered no human re-check anywhere in the release chain.

Stop at the decision point: the release node before the door was closed and the aircraft passed into delivery service. On the table: incomplete work-order records, and inspections ticking items off an existing checklist — a checklist with no line reading "reinstallation after opening a critical section must be re-verified." 〔Outcome〕Fleet-wide grounding for inspection; multiple revisions to manufacturing processes and regulatory oversight. Primary source: NTSB, AIR-25-04, 2025; link in Appendix B, av-030.

### Case 3 (Public): The Palace, Thirteen Years of Repair Without Work Begun

po-005. The Restoration and Renewal programme of the Palace of Westminster. NAO 2026 update report (reviewed for this book): the two Houses established the programme in 2013 and decided in 2018 to decant for the works; at the report's publication the programme was still in the early stages of the first-phase scheme (seven years, capped at 3 billion pounds). The building's maintenance arrears have accumulated in units of a decade: every year's decision to "wait a little longer" was a money-saving decision in its own year, and the building's ageing curve does not pause for budget cycles. The building's World Heritage status means the bill for the arrears is written into every annual report — and the start of work still recedes. That arrears visible to this degree can still be postponed is the strongest form of the invisibility of maintenance: it is not that nobody knows; it is that even knowing cannot get it scheduled.

Stop at the decision point: the approval moments, in one budget cycle after another, at which "the major works will not start this year." On the table: the current fiscal room, the building's condition survey, and the political weight of the scheme. 〔Outcome〕The arrears continue to accumulate; the scheme remains in its early stages. Primary source: NAO, Restoration and Renewal 2026 Update, 2026; link in Appendix B, po-005.

### Case 4 (Construction): A Forty-Year-Old Building's Progressive Collapse

ce-009. Champlain Towers South condominium, in the early hours of 24 June 2021, Surfside, Florida. NIST investigation (reviewed for this book): progressive partial collapse of the 12-storey condominium; NIST's technical conclusion, issued in June 2026, found the collapse began at two connection failures — between garage columns and the pool deck — with load transfer then crushing other members. The maintenance arrears of this seaside concrete structure span decades: waterproofing, reinforcement cover, structural inspections — each deferral had its reasons in its own year, and the form the arrears took was the major-repair assessment deferred on every owners' bill.

Stop at the decision point: every moment in the successive repair assessments and budget votes at which structural repairs were pushed to "next year." On the table: the assessment reports' wording (between "needs attention" and "must be immediate"), the current owners' fees, and the "structural liability" line that belonged to no one. 〔Outcome〕98 deaths is the figure confirmed by county officials; the NIST page carries no casualty numbers 〔Check point: casualty wording needs verification against official county statistics〕. Primary source: NIST, Champlain Towers South Collapse Investigation; link in Appendix B, ce-009.

## 4 What Shows Earliest

Six weak signals, each with the reason that usually overrides it.

1. The maintenance budget's share falls year by year while equipment counts and ages stay flat or rise. Override: "efficiency gains."
2. Maintenance shifts from scheduled work to "fix it when it breaks." Override: "condition-based is cheaper." (It depends on the equipment, but it is often the opening move of arrears.)
3. The size of the arrears is quantified for the first time in a report (many institutions' first quantification arrives late). Override: "getting a clear picture of our position."
4. Approvals for running with known defects begin citing precedents of running with known defects. Override: "nothing happened last time." (Chapter 11.)
5. Maintenance crews are seconded to support production or business peaks, and never come back. Override: "the greater good."
6. Post-failure reports note that the failed item "exceeded its design life." Override: "over-age service is normal."

## 5 How to Defend

Device one: an auditable maintenance ledger. Maintenance moves from soft spending to a hard ledger: what, when, to what standard, at what cost — entered line by line and open to inspection (Chapter 38's audit-trail principle in its asset version). The ledger itself changes the game: when maintenance is cut, what is cut is an entry with a number and a history, not an anonymous percentage. The recommended auditor of the ledger is the insurer: arrears directly raise its payouts, and it is the only reviewer with an economic motive built in.

Device two: convert arrears into liabilities. Deferred maintenance is converted, at industry-standard rates, into a liability figure that enters the notes to the balance sheet and is published yearly (mature practice in public asset management). The arrears change from "the work not done" to "the money owed," and the semantics of the budget discussion change with them: from "should we spend this money" to "when do we repay this debt."

Device three: dual triggers of age and condition. Maintenance triggers upgrade from "calendar due" to "calendar or condition, whichever comes first"; investment in condition monitoring (vibration, thermography, corrosion survey) is part of the maintenance budget, not an optional extra. The structural inspections at Surfside, the residue disposal at ValuJet, the reinstallation records of 1282 — all three cases were owed exactly the missing "condition trigger" layer.

Device four: a wall around the maintenance budget. The maintenance line gets a floor (a fixed proportion of asset replacement value), and cuts below the floor require the consent of an external independent party (audit, insurer). The wall's purpose is not to make maintenance uncuttable; it is to make every cut confront a party with a stake in the answer — the question the insurer will ask ("who pays for this arrears later") is precisely the question absent from the budget meeting.

## 6 Boundaries with Adjacent Mechanisms

Against the failure of success (Chapter 20): slack cuts defensive investment (backups, drills); this chapter cuts sustaining investment (maintenance, overhauls). The ledger structure is the same, the line items different; building them together is recommended.

Against horizon mismatch (Chapter 28): the decision structure of cutting maintenance (the cost falls outside the term of office) is horizon mismatch; Westminster and Surfside are claimed by both chapters. The criterion: to analyse "why the cut was politically possible" use this chapter; to analyse "why the cutter bears no consequence" use Chapter 28.

Against margin erosion (Chapter 25): maintenance arrears eat the condition reserve (corrosion eats wall thickness); margin eating eats the design reserve (redundancy consumed in one-off cuts). The two ledgers are best recorded on the same page: a system's "remaining life" and "remaining margin" are one conversation.

## 7 Self-Check: Five Questions

1. Your organisation's maintenance arrears, converted into money: what is the figure? When was it quantified for the first time?
2. Maintenance budget as a share of total asset value — has the five-year curve been rising?
3. In your ledger, when is the most recent entry recording "maintenance caught a problem"? If you cannot answer, the ledger has not been built yet.
4. Is any equipment running with a known defect right now? Which precedent did its approval cite?
5. If the maintenance budget were cut by thirty percent tomorrow, how long would it take you to see the consequence in the numbers? That delay is the mechanism's entire timetable.

## 6bis Installing the Devices: Order, Failure Modes, and Cost

The auditable maintenance ledger goes first — it is the foundation; without it, the wall and the triggers have nothing to act on. Backlog-as-liability follows (it translates the ledger into governance language), the budget wall third (it protects the appropriation), the dual trigger fourth (it times the interventions), and the slope contingency last (it governs the worst case: the day the wall is climbed). First ninety days: weeks one to three, split maintenance activity out of "expense" into ledger entries — what was done, against what standard, at what interval, next due; four fields to start; month two, estimate the backlog per entry (standard interval minus actual execution, booked in both hours and money); month three, raise the wall (a separate appropriation, any draw needing a governance signature) and run the first condition assessments on the ten oldest assets — the dual trigger's first live pass.

Failure modes. The ledger turns decorative ("routine maintenance," four characters; standard, interval, next-due all blank) — completeness validation: entries missing fields do not count toward completed maintenance, and monthly reports count auditable entries only. The liability gets accountized (on the books, unread) — a one-page annual statement for governance: total backlog, year on year, top five entries; legibility is a hard requirement. The wall erodes quietly (principal untouched; line items shuffle) — publish the money map yearly: where maintenance money came from and where it went, one diagram. Condition assessments turn perfunctory (done, "good," ages rising) — third-party spot re-assessment of ten percent annually. And the slope contingency simply doesn't exist (improvised the day the cuts land) — pre-sign it: a trigger line in the budget document; cuts beyond the line auto-start the contingency review.

Cost: the first ledger runs two to four weeks by asset count with half a day per quarter after; backlog estimation is one-time; the wall is a budget-line change; condition assessments run half a day to a day per asset, rollable. Against Surfside, a dust-collection system's years of compounding debt settled in one detonation, or a ledger where the physical count never matched the paper — this section's five devices exist to move the payoff date from "some day" to "every quarter."

Toolchain: the locator triggers on "no output metric for maintenance plus budget pressure." Question set 29's item 1 ("the three-year curve of maintenance share") audits the liability translation. Post-mortems with backlog in the chain add ledger entries. And Chapter 20's defensive account is this chapter's ledger seen from the other side — output there, backlog here; governance should read one working paper: the two chapters' devices testify together at budget season.
