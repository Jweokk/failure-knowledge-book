# Preface: The Reader's Contract

## What This Book Is

Every requirement for a reference book on organizational failure can be compressed into one sentence: after reading it, before making a decision, you can name the specific ways you are most likely to die.

Around that sentence, this book does three things. First, it compresses failures scattered across hundreds of pages of investigation reports into numbered case cards, twelve fields each (defined in Chapter 5). Second, it organizes those cases into twenty-four mechanisms, one per chapter, each with activation criteria, generating conditions, weak-signal checklists, and countermeasures. Third, it provides a toolkit: a situation-assessment table, a 130-question review checklist, and a 90-minute death-rehearsal script.

All factual material comes from primary documents: investigation reports, court judgments, regulatory penalties, recall notices, audit reports, financial filings, and first-party postmortems. Every case in the text carries a full citation and link, numbered against the case table in Appendix B. This book conducts no new investigations. Investigation is a separate craft, practiced for decades by dedicated professionals — the writers at the NTSB, the CSB, and their counterparts worldwide. What this book does is circulation: extracting the reusable parts of those documents, indexing them, and making them retrievable at the moment of decision.

## The Reader's Contract

Four promises this book makes to you. Every chapter must deliver on all four. If any chapter fails, treat it as a misprint.

1. Every chapter opens with three lines: what it covers, when to return to it, how long it takes.
2. Every chapter ends not with a summary but with five self-check questions. All five require you to answer from your own work. If you cannot answer, the mechanism described is operating on you right now — you are not reading about someone else's story.
3. Every case stops at the decision point. The reader judges first, then turns the page. All cases use a uniform marker:

〔Stop point: reader judges first, then turns the page〕

1. What information is on the table right now?
2. If you were the person deciding, would you sign?
3. What do you bet happens in the next three months?

〔Outcome〕

Stopping at the decision point has a precise purpose. All postmortems are written after the outcome is known; readers of postmortems naturally search for the process with the answer already in hand, and everything looks like it "should have been obvious." The stop point withholds the answer, forces you to make a choice with incomplete information, and then lets you compare your choice with the actual one. What your choice got wrong and what the decider's choice got wrong are two separate accounts.

4. No moralising. The book writes structure, never "should." Where the structural problem was, what signal appeared first, what intervention would have changed the incentive or information flow — that is what gets written. Whether the people involved were stupid or arrogant, the book offers no verdict, because such verdicts cannot be verified and they obstruct your view of the structure.

## No Assumptions About Who You Are

This book assumes no industry, no seniority level. An aviation maintenance-release case and a hospital disinfection case sit in the same chapter because the mechanism is the same; if the mechanism is the same, you will find its isomorph in your own world. Every term is explained on first use and registered in the bilingual glossary.

If you encounter case details you cannot follow, skip the details and read the structure. Every case comes with a "decision point" and a "minimal counterfactual"; neither requires domain expertise.

## Evidence Grading

Every case carries an evidence grade from A to E: A is the full text of an independent investigation report; B is an official bulletin or regulator notice; C is a court judgment or penalty decision; D is a first-party postmortem; E is media reporting, which may be used only as a lead and never as a factual source. Content like escalation points and minimal counterfactuals can only be supported by Grade A material and some B/C material; cases below that threshold go into the appendix matrix, not the main text.

One thing to say at the outset: every number in this book must carry a four-part provenance — who measured it, when, what the sample was, and what the scope was. Any number that cannot meet all four gets rewritten as a mechanism description. When you encounter a paragraph with no numbers, most of the time it is because discipline kicked in.

## What Is Not in This Book

Three things. Emotional consolation about failure — the market is saturated. Attribution of blame and litigation — that is what lawyers are for. Prediction of which company will collapse — this book provides a taxonomy of death, not fortune-telling.

One type of failure is deliberately confined to Part 6: exploratory failure. Without it there would be no new drugs, no new routes, no new technology. Mixing it with preventable operational failure and treating them the same way amounts to teaching people to refuse all attempts. Part 6 provides the boundary criteria.

## A Reading Route

If you are in a hurry, read three chapters: Chapter 2 (how your case sample has been filtered), Chapter 35 (the situation-assessment table), and Chapter 37 (the review question checklist). If you have a project to review, add the two mechanism chapters your situation maps to. If you write postmortems, read Chapters 5 and 38. Everything else is reference.

All chapters are self-contained; you can start from any of them. The only requirement: when you hit a case stop point, actually stop. Answer the three questions before reading the outcome. Skipping every stop point to read the whole book means you have read half of it.
