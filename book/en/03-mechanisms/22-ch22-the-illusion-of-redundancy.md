# Chapter 22 The Illusion of Redundancy

> What this chapter covers: why two backups fail together, the three tests for common-cause failure, its shape in four domains, the arithmetic of redundancy, and the validation standard of systems that are different and have actually been rehearsed.
> When to turn back: when reviewing a redundant design, buying a backup system, or when someone answers a single-point challenge with "we have two".
> Estimated reading time: 50 minutes.

"We have two" is the sentence in an engineering conversation that most needs a follow-up. The follow-up is not "where is the second one". It is three more expensive questions. How does it differ from the first? Who has verified that it can work on its own? Has the switch to it ever been made under real pressure? Ask all three and a good number of "two" shrink to one and a half, or to one with a manual. This chapter is about that shrinkage: how redundancy loses its independence at each stage, in procurement, in design, in rehearsal, until the day the common cause arrives and every set fails at once.

## 22.1 What it is

Definition: the illusion of redundancy describes several lines of defence that stand side by side on paper and are in fact one line. They share a power supply, a vendor, a drawing, a crew, or an assumption. When the common cause arrives, all of them fail at once. Engineering calls this common-cause failure. The word illusion marks the cognitive side: an auditor counts the number of lines, an attacker counts the kinds.

Three tests for activation. Any one of them means the mechanism is already running.

1. Two "independent" lines share a common item: power, vendor, algorithm, drawing, people, location, or one shared assumption.
2. The redundancy has never been verified on its own. Nobody has ever shut the primary down and tried the backup.
3. The switchover has never been rehearsed under real pressure. Every rehearsal has been run in a planned window, at low load, with a way back.

## 22.2 How it grows

Three conditions generate it.

Condition one, the scale logic of procurement. A second system from the same supplier is cheaper, integrates more easily, and the operations team does not have to learn a second product. Heterogeneity means twice the learning and twice the maintenance cost, so the budget moves rationally toward the same source. Nobody decides "we want a single source". Each local optimisation points there.

Condition two, design reuse. The backup inherits the primary's drawings and assumptions, and the errors in the design are copied faithfully into the backup. Even the errors are redundant.

Condition three, rehearsal cost avoidance. A real switchover carries risk, and real pressure carries business impact, so the switchover is scheduled as a ceremony both sides know: planned window, low load, a retreat available at any moment. The ceremony completes, and the rehearsal record says successful.

The arithmetic of common-cause failure is the centre of this chapter. Let p be the annual failure probability of one line. Two independent lines fail together with probability about p², so the return on redundancy comes from multiplication. If the two lines share a common cause with probability c, the joint probability is about c + p², and the order of magnitude drops back from multiplication to addition. The return evaporates. Until the common cause is removed, the arithmetic of redundancy is fiction, which is why test 1 asks about shared items rather than about the number of sets.

The family tree of common causes is worth listing in full, because the register (Device 1) is searched by family. The environmental family: one power supply, one server room, one flood, one electromagnetic environment. Arkema's flood is the specimen for this family. The design family: one drawing, one algorithm, one assumption. The assumption in the map case is that the screen carries all the traffic. The operational family: one maintenance team, one configuration change, one monitoring stack. The common item in the monitoring cases is people. The behavioural family: one habit of use, one allocation of trust. The sanding case rests on an assumption about how automatic and manual sanding divide the work.

Beyond those four sits a fifth and the most concealed: certification. Two systems passed the same certification to the same standard, and the blind spot in the certification method becomes the shared blind spot of both. The evidential assumption in case four of this chapter has the same origin as case four in Chapter 20. Register by family and the result is more reliable than scanning by intuition, because intuition misses environment and certification most often: neither is in view on an ordinary day.

One step further into the arithmetic gives the qualitative version of the beta factor, which reliability engineering knows well. Split the failure probability of the pair into an independent part and a common-cause part. The share held by the common-cause part, beta, decides what the redundancy is worth. Beta near zero and the pair behaves almost multiplicatively. Beta near one and the pair behaves almost like a single line. Beta comes from a list rather than from theory: count the shared items under test 1, and the contribution of each is roughly visible. This chapter keeps no formulas, only one conversion for managers. The discount rate on the redundancy you bought is beta. More shared items means a deeper discount. The heterogeneity you saved on in the purchase contract is the price of a higher beta.

## 22.3 The shape in four domains

### Case one (aviation): A map treated as a collision-avoidance system

av-022. A Bell 206L-4 (N193AL) and a float-equipped DHC-2 (N97EL), 4 September 2023, about a thousand feet over King Salmon, Alaska. NTSB final report (ANC23LA071, downloaded and read by this book): the two aircraft collided in flight. One person in the helicopter was slightly injured; seven people in the aeroplane were unhurt. The helicopter crew was using an ADS-B electronic map as its reference for traffic and collision avoidance, and the DHC-2 it struck carried no ADS-B at all, so the traffic picture on the helicopter's screen could not contain that class of target. The paper redundancy for collision avoidance, eyes outside plus electronic map, was down to one leg in reality: the crew's visual scan had been partly ceded to the screen, and the screen stayed silent about the traffic it could not see.

Stop at the decision point: the stretch of flight in which the helicopter crew treated the map as its traffic picture. What was on the table: the target list on the screen, the operating habit of seeing and avoiding, and a question nobody had checked, which is how much the screen does not show. (What happened: mid-air collision.) Primary source: NTSB, ANC23LA071, 2024, link in Appendix B av-022.

What case one adds to a redundancy list is perception as a line of defence. Looking out and an electronic map should be two independent sensing lines, since eyes and screen fail in different ways. After the cession, the presence of the screen weakens the scan: the screen says there is no traffic, and the eyes stop working at it. Branches of a redundant system have a behavioural dependence as well as a problem of physical independence. The existence of one branch changes how hard the other is used. Human factors calls the aviation form perceptual complacency. The list needs a line for use-dependence between branches: does the presence of A reduce how much B is used. Automatic against manual sanding in the sanding case, and the purple syringe against the double check in the healthcare case, are recorded on that same line.

### Case two (construction & infrastructure / rail): Two sanders fall silent together

**Case ID**: ce-001

**When and where**: 21 October 2024 at 19:26; the single line west of Talerddig passing loop, Powys, Wales, United Kingdom. The collision point lay about 890 m beyond the exit points at the west end of the loop, and 1,080 m beyond block marker MH1078. The train had been braking into the east end of the loop since 19:22.

**What and who**: Transport for Wales Rail Limited (TfWRL), operator of passenger services on the Cambrian lines. The unit was Class 158 diesel multiple unit 158841 (leading car 57841, rear car 52841). The driver qualified on that line and type in March 2022, after 13 years in other railway roles. The infrastructure, the Talerddig loop points and the Machynlleth signal control centre belong to Network Rail's Wales and Borders route; the signaller on duty had 8 years of experience. The rolling stock is owned by Angel Trains. ERTMS signalling on the Cambrian lines was supplied by Ansaldo STS, now Compagnie des Signaux. Vehicle maintenance, fuelling point checks and sanding tests were carried out by TfWRL at Machynlleth depot.

**What they were doing**: In the 24 hours before the collision the unit was working its seventh diagram of the day, and its sanding system had just passed three "serviceable" confirmations in a row. In the early hours of 18 October, 158841 spent the night at Holyhead depot and three 5 kg bags of sand went into the underfloor sand boxes (recorded on CCTV). Overnight on 19 to 20 October the unit had a fuelling point check at Machynlleth depot, which falls about every 1,500 miles: the technician pressed the sanding test button on the sand box, saw sand come out, and recorded a pass. On the morning of 20 October the driver instructor and a trainee carried out the preparation, pressed the test button again, and sand came out again (p. 19). Through 20 October the unit ran four routes; disruption meant the planned overnight at Crewe became a night at a platform at Chester. On the morning of 21 October at Chester the driver on duty could not reach the underfloor equipment, so the sanding test was not done, and the procedure did not require it (p. 19). On 25 September the annual sanding rate test had been recorded as completed, though TfWRL's maintenance planning system had no field for the weight, only a tick box (p. 90). On 12 October a B exam, about every 7,500 miles, passed its wheelslide protection test (pp. 50, 90). On the evening of 21 October, 158841 coupled to 158828, was uncoupled at Shrewsbury and went forward as 1J25, departing at 18:33 two minutes late with 31 people aboard. The opposing service 1S71 (158824, 6 people aboard) left Machynlleth at 19:08. Both ran on time and were booked to pass at Talerddig: the signaller put 1J25 into the Up Loop to stop, and 1S71 through the Down Loop at 95 km/h (p. 20). At 19:22 the train approached the loop at 125 km/h; the driver closed the power controller, made two applications of brake step 1 within 39 seconds, and the speed fell to 94 km/h. At 19:23 he selected step 2, and three to four seconds later applied full service braking 640 m from the stopping point, with ETCS applying as well; the driver states that he applied too. About a second later the WSP intervened. Six seconds after that, ETCS commanded emergency braking, at about 500 m from the stopping point (p. 21).

**The decision point**: In January 2008, *Overrun Risk Assessment ERTMS Level 2 Movement Authorities Cambrian*, EDSCL-DES-004000, version 0.2 issue 10, 7 pages, covered the whole Cambrian line and was not location-specific. The document cited GI/RT7006 clause 4.4 b i, on the grounds that the standard for lineside signalling does not apply to a cab signalling system and that Cambrian ERTMS carries no AWS, and on that basis dispensed with a detailed overrun risk assessment. It set an overrun rate of 2×10⁻⁹ per train per hour, which is to say that a train under ETCS supervision will not pass the overlap (p. 72). In September 2009 the ERTMS project obtained a derogation on that basis: beyond MH1078 there is only 81 m of overlap where the standard requires 180 m (p. 71). Once the overlap was shortened, the margin against overrun rested on the braking performance of the train, and braking performance at low adhesion depends on sanding. RAIB found no evidence that any party to the project recognised that dependency (p. 89). The document made "ETCS supervises continuously, so no train passes the overlap" a premise for the whole line, and because the detailed assessment was waived, the combination beyond MH1078 of a low-adhesion high-risk area, a 1:56 descent and the entry to a single line was never assessed at all (p. 72).

On 25 September 2024, at Machynlleth depot, a technician carried out the annual sanding rate test. A bag went over the sand delivery hose, the test button was pressed for 30 seconds, and the procedure required 0.75 to 1.0 kg from each hose. The system records only a tick for completed, with no weight (p. 90).

The fuelling point check in the early hours of 20 October, on the night shift of the 19th, and the departure preparation on the morning of 21 October confirmed "sanding serviceable" twice more, both times using the button on the sand box. That button has its own supply and bypasses the sanding isolation switch (SIS) and the low-speed relay (LSR4) (p. 90). The same bypass circuit had been used in the B exam of 12 October.

Between 19:23 and 19:26 on 21 October, in the cab of 57841: ETCS applied full service braking and then emergency braking. The driver called the signaller on the ordinary GSM-R and reported that he was slipping through Talerddig and expected to pass the block marker (p. 55). At 19:24 the train passed MH1078 at 54 km/h, and 190 m later it ran through the points at the west end of the loop at 33 km/h into the 1:56 descending single line. The emergency sanding button was never pressed (pp. 55, 56). Emergency sanding takes effect whenever emergency braking is set, and that condition already existed (p. 51).

**What was visible then**: The cab before 19:22. The planning area of the DMI showed a four-step speed profile of 130, 115, 95 and 50 km/h, with the 50 km/h step for entering the Up Loop falling at the loop entrance (pp. 56, 61). A lineside sign at MH1078 read low adhesion (p. 23). It was dark, and the driver had seen no rain on the approach (p. 36). The TfWRL *Autumn Seasonal Risk Guide*, version 2 of September 2023, marks Talerddig as a low-adhesion high-risk area (p. 67). The fixed items of departure preparation include two health indicator lights for emergency sanding, and both were lit that day (p. 52).

From 19:23 onward. The 95 km/h advisory sequence had not finished when the DMI switched to 50 km/h; the pre-indication was masked by the previous step and the display jumped straight to yellow (p. 62). The driver's statement is that he registered the need to slow further from the yellow speed hook pressing toward the needle, and that he does not recall an audible warning (p. 63). The overspeed message and the ETCS brake symbol followed. The WSP intervened about a second after the first full service brake application, and this appears only in the data record, with no separate cab display.

On the desk, the only sanding-related items were the emergency sanding button and the two health lights, which at night sit behind a shroud and were visible lit beneath it. Nothing told the driver that automatic sanding was delivering sand (pp. 29, 52).

The paper record for that day: 15 kg of sand added on 18 October, two tests with sand flow on 20 October, a B exam passed on 12 October, the annual measurement completed on 25 September, and no test on the morning of 21 October. What was known only afterwards: the SIS contact showed high resistance in the on position, the LSR4 relay coil was intermittently open inside its base, and both sand delivery hoses on the leading car were blocked with leaf-like organic matter (pp. 43–48). The adhesion peak derived from WSPER data fell from 0.06 before MH1078 to 0.035 on the descending section (pp. 39, 67). Had automatic sanding been delivering 1.5 to 2.0 kg per minute, the train would have stopped before MH1078 or just beyond it (p. 50).

**What happened**: One passenger died (Tudor Evans). On 1J25, 3 people were seriously injured, including the conductor, and 18 received minor injuries; the driver of 1S71 was seriously injured and the other 5 people on board received minor injuries (pp. 7, 12). Neither train derailed. The leading car was severely deformed, and the cab of 1S71 was the worst deformed. 1J25 ran through the points at the west end of the loop, which were not set for it, and damaged them. The line reopened on 28 October, after 7 days closed (p. 12). RAIB made 9 recommendations, 3 safety observations and 1 learning point (p. 9).

The three questions, looking back. The most common answer to the first is "press the emergency sanding the moment slip is detected", and its cost is one shot of sand and a refill at the depot; the report calculated the effect: pressed at the moment ETCS commanded emergency braking, the train stops just beyond MH1078 (p. 54). The item most easily missed under the second question is the one that reads "test button passed": on paper it looks most like safety evidence, and its circuit happens to bypass the two failure points. The third question lands on the design of the sanding test. What has to be proven is that the circuit is live, the valve opens and sand reaches the rail, not that there is sand in the box.

**Options ruled out**: Trap points at the west end of the loop. They were installed in 1968, removed in 1986 when the line changed to RETB, and not restored when ERTMS came in; Network Rail limits their use to depots, yards and siding exits, because a trap point works by derailing a train deliberately (pp. 70, 71). A location-specific detailed overrun risk assessment: the 7-page document of 2008 had covered the whole line with a simple assessment (p. 72). Restoring the MH1078 overlap to the standard 180 m: derogated in September 2009 on the grounds of continuous ETCS supervision (p. 71). Applying an adhesion modifier after the RHTT wash: Wales and Borders route policy is not to, because the effect is uncertain and because the modifier may glue leaves to rail that has just been cleaned (pp. 77, 95). An automatic stop command to other trains in the event of an overrun: ERTMS has no such function, it is a customer requirement item, and Network Rail did not ask for it; the signal box has a regional emergency stop, but procedure puts a GSM-R call first (p. 73). A cab indication that automatic sanding is delivering sand: this needs design and certification work, and no modification had been proposed before the accident (p. 108). Monitoring of the automatic sanding delivery rate: Angel Trains began a technical review only after the accident (p. 108).

**Early signals**: ① The Stonegate accident of 2010 (RAIB report 18/2011), in which another train's automatic sanding was not working, with the sand box almost empty, and the train ran 4 km past its planned stop; on a section treated with adhesion modifier, deceleration was better by about 0.5%g than on untreated track. Why it was pressed past: single data point, not quantifiable (p. 77). ② *Braking Using ERTMS for the Cambrian Project*, version 5.0, September 2010, section 4.1, which states that very low adhesion requires the driver to adjust driving to keep the speed safe. Why it was pressed past: the applicable standard places low adhesion outside its scope (pp. 87, 88). ③ GI/RT7006 of 2000, which explicitly excludes overruns caused by low adhesion from the overrun protection standard, with three supporting reasons: low adhesion is hard to predict, it will disappear over the years, and other measures can deal with it, namely sanding, rail treatment and vegetation management. Why it was pressed past: if other measures cope, overrun protection need not (p. 88). ④ During early ERTMS testing, ORR raised the point that closely spaced cascading speed restrictions let one indication mask another, and recommended avoiding the arrangement; RAIB could find no record of a formal response from the project (p. 64). ⑤ A driver may meet an ETCS intervention about once a week, and 158824 had 17 service brake interventions in the 21 days before the accident. TfWRL had no software to read the JRU and could only look for traces in the OTDR, so it identified far fewer than occurred (p. 92). Why it was pressed past: interventions were treated as the system working normally. ⑥ Talerddig had been listed as a low-adhesion high-risk area for years, over a section 5.9 km long, and the original reason was that uphill trains could not climb it. Why it was pressed past: being registered as high risk was taken as being handled (p. 67).

**The point of escalation**: The fuelling point check in the early hours of 20 October, on the night shift of the 19th. The item sits in TfWRL's maintenance plan at about every 1,500 miles, is carried out by a technician at Machynlleth, and consists of pressing the test button on the sand box and watching the hose. The test passed and the box was ticked. After that day, the next ground check that could possibly find a fault in automatic sanding was the next B exam, up to two weeks away (p. 90). The two-week window comes from the B exam interval of about 7,500 miles: a fault can appear on any day inside the window, and checks happen only at its two ends. The bypass circuit let this check pass with the system already failed. The departure preparation on the morning of 21 October was the second line, and it was not done because the platform position put the underfloor equipment out of reach, which the process permitted. After two confirmations on paper, the train went into its seventh diagram of 21 October carrying an automatic sanding system that delivered nothing. From that moment to the collision, no ground check would touch the system, and no cab display would expose it.

**Failure chain**: Technical layer. The delivery command for automatic sanding needs four conditions to hold at once: SIS closed, the LSR4 contact closed, the SWR contact closed, and the direction and brake step selection relays in position (p. 45). After the accident the first two were found not to hold: the SIS contact showed high resistance in the on position, and the LSR4 coil was intermittently open between coil and base terminal, either of which would stop the sand valve energising (pp. 43–47). Both delivery hoses on the leading car were blocked with leaf-like organic matter at the same time, and normal working back pressure would be enough to clear a blockage of that kind (pp. 44, 48). The cab had no delivery indication (p. 29). Wheelslide protection itself worked normally, and for that very reason the fact that the WSP operated cannot be used to argue that sanding was working (p. 42).

Organizational layer. The test button circuit has its own supply and bypasses SIS and LSR4 (p. 90), and the same button is the main evidence at the fuelling point check, at departure preparation and at the annual measurement, so every test succeeded. The annual measurement procedure required weighing, and the maintenance system had no field for weight (p. 90). The JRU recorded every ETCS intervention; TfWRL had no software to read it and had to route data requests through Network Rail (p. 92). On the overrun protection side: 81 m of overlap, no trap points at the west end, and the simple whole-line assessment of 2008 (pp. 71, 72).

Incentive layer. The RHTT only washes and applies no adhesion modifier, on the grounds that the effect is uncertain and leaves may stick to the rail (p. 95). Monitoring of the sanding delivery rate was classed as a technical review whose starting point came after the accident (p. 108). Trading 180 m of overlap for 81 m bought line capacity and left the existing signal layout untouched.

The break sits at the seam between the organizational and incentive layers. The test circuit was designed to prove that sand comes out, not that the circuit is live. That cheap choice kept two silent technical faults permanently invisible to a check that ran once a fortnight.

**Minimum counterfactual**: At 19:23, at the moment ETCS commanded emergency braking, press the emergency sanding button on the desk. One action, and the conditions held at that moment: emergency sanding is available only with emergency braking set, and it was set; the button is on the desk, both health lights were lit, both pressure bottles were in the green, and the system delivered sand normally when tested after the accident (pp. 52, 53). No luck is involved: the button is fixed equipment in the cab, the sanding pressure and quantity had been inspected, and RAIB's simulation calculated the action: pressed at that moment, the train stops just beyond MH1078 and does not enter the single line (p. 54). The cost is one shot of sand, refilled at the depot. The action was available every second after 19:23, and its effect decays with distance: pressed while passing MH1078, the train stops beyond the loop exit points; pressed about 18 seconds after leaving the loop, it stops near MH1081; pressed 75 seconds after passing MH1078, the collision still happens, at about 14 km/h (p. 54).

**Outcome and sources**: One passenger died, 4 people were seriously injured (3 on 1J25 and the driver of 1S71), and 23 received minor injuries (18 on 1J25, 5 on 1S71). Neither train derailed. The Talerddig loop points were damaged and the line reopened on 28 October (pp. 7, 12). RAIB published the report on 18 June 2026, with 9 recommendations addressed to RSSB, Angel Trains, Network Rail and TfWRL, plus 3 safety observations and 1 learning point on safety-critical communications (p. 9). RAIB also wrote that had 1S71 been stopped by a railway emergency call (REC), the collision would not have been avoided but the collision energy would have been lower (p. 8). The report leaves room in its wording on the key judgements: the cause of the automatic sanding failure is written as possible, and the blocked hoses would probably have been cleared by a working system (pp. 7, 48). TfWRL issued two national incident notices after the accident, one on the hose blockage and one on the electrical fault, and changed the sanding test button circuit on the Class 158 so that it passes through SIS and LSR4 (p. 108). Primary source: RAIB, Report 08/2026, *Collision between two passenger trains near Talerddig, Powys, 21 October 2024*, June 2026, 140 pages, link in Appendix B ce-001.

The division of labour between automatic and manual in this case deserves one more cut. An arrangement where the automatic system is primary and the manual system catches it is reasonable while warnings work. Its gap is the scenario in which the automatic system fails quietly: the system is broken and does not warn, so the signal the driver reads, which is silence, contradicts the true state. Silence read as normal is the general trap of automation, the silent failure mode. A common-item register for redundancy therefore needs an entry for silent failure: mark each system's failure as loud, meaning it warns, or quiet, meaning it does not. A quiet system needs periodic active verification, a check of state rather than a wait for a warning, because a monitoring strategy that waits for alarms has no purchase on silent failure at all.

Monitoring has a common cause of its own, and the recursion is worth stating. The monitoring of two systems can share a source: one collection script, one alerting platform, one on-call judgement process. A common cause in monitoring makes the failure of the backup itself invisible, because the alarm paths for primary and backup die together and you never get the chance to learn that the backup is dead. Verifying monitoring redundancy is the meta level of verifying redundancy: first prove the alarm path is alive by injecting a test alarm, then prove the redundancy works by a real switchover. The order cannot be reversed, since a switchover rehearsal on a dead path is a photograph of nothing. The meta level is verified more often than the object level, monthly injection against annual switchover, and costs far less, so it should be the first routine action in any redundant system.

After the routine action, one piece of the redundancy picture is still missing: retirement management. A superseded system, the old primary after a cutover or the previous generation of equipment, tends to stay on the register for a while, and readers of the register keep counting it as a line of defence. An old system that is not maintained, not upgraded and not rehearsed is a line of defence on paper only. The rule has to be written hard: from the date of formal retirement the system comes off the defence count and the register is updated with it. For the transition period when old and new run in parallel, state which one counts as defence and which is a shadow. The reader of a register is always someone in the future, an auditor, a successor, a regulator, and honesty toward that reader is the last exit for every device in this chapter. The ledger has to be true before redundancy counts.

Redundancy that counts still faces one last opponent: time eroding heterogeneity. Heterogeneity is not a property acquired at purchase, it is a property that has to be managed. The second supplier gets acquired and becomes the same source. The second team is rotated and merged into the same team. The second algorithm converges as its framework is upgraded and becomes the same stack. This decay is a slow variable, observed on a five-year cycle: each year, when the common-cause register is updated, compare the heterogeneity dimensions against last year and mark the entries that lost a dimension as heterogeneous erosion. The remedy depends on the dimension, either re-heterogenise by changing supplier or accept the downgrade and discount that pair in the defence count. Slow variables tend to reach zero in silence, and the annual comparison is how this chapter gives heterogeneity a place where it cannot go quiet.

The chapter now belongs in the book's larger picture. Most mechanisms in Part III are many causes producing one effect; this one is the mirror image, one cause producing many effects. The common cause arrives, several lines of defence fail together, and several mechanisms get their stage at once: once redundancy collapses, normalization of deviance, invisible maintenance and the slack of success each pay their bill inside the same accident. That mirrored structure makes this chapter a key to many accident reports. Where a report speaks of multiple defences failing, most of the time the single cause is found here. The method for finding it is the five-family list: pull out every failed line of defence in the report, look for their intersection, and the intersection is the common cause. Intersection is the tool this chapter gives readers of accidents, and the mirror it holds up to designers: every line of defence you design will one day stand in front of a common cause you did not think of, and whether it is ready has been written in the ledger for a long time.

One page of that ledger belongs to the buyer: acceptance criteria for redundant procurement. Acceptance does not count deliverables, meaning two systems arriving, it counts three things. A difference statement, with the list of differences between the two systems archived alongside the delivery. An independent validation report, in which the supplier tests the second system alone under agreed conditions. A switchover script, including the worst-condition version. Put all three into the acceptance clause of the purchase contract and the supplier's quote rises a little, which is the true cost of testing and documentation. That is exactly the price signal this chapter wants: the real cost of redundancy is validation, and copying is the cheap part. Redundancy sold without validation is cheap because what is being sold is the appearance of redundancy.

The last hiding place of appearance is insurance and compliance paperwork: redundancy clauses in policies, defence lists in compliance reports. Both are concentrations of appearance, and both have one good use. Take the defence list in the insurance and compliance documents and reconcile it against this chapter's ledger; the entries that differ are the complete roster of paper defences. The roster is usually embarrassing, since the insurer believes you have things that measurement shows you do not, and the value of the embarrassment is that it prices the gap between two sets of books. A policy is priced on paper, an accident is paid out in reality, and the difference is shared on the day it happens between shareholders and the families of the dead. Reconcile once a year by taking the insurance declaration line by line against the rehearsal records. The other name for that action is repricing the policy against reality.

With pricing done, the redundancy story closes its loop inside this chapter. It starts as a verbal promise, "we have two", and ends as a ledger: difference statements, independent validation, rehearsal records, common-cause registration, heterogeneity maintenance, retirement management, policy reconciliation. No single link is expensive, and once the links are joined the verbal promise acquires an engineering meaning for the first time. The keeper of the ledger has to be written into a post, following the owner clause of Chapter 20: the ledger has an owner, and the owner has a performance measure, which is the on-time rate of each link. The redundancy ledger, combined with the dependency map of Chapter 21 and the output ledger of Chapter 20, makes three books for an organization's defence system: where the defences are (dependency map), whether they are alive (redundancy ledger), and whether they do any work (output ledger). Once a year the three books consolidate into a defence annual report, whose first page follows the Chapter 20 format: what we caught this year, what we validated this year, what we downgraded this year.

The annual report is also where the attacker's view gets its last page, because that view is where counting kinds comes from. An attacker here means any force looking for the weakest path through a system: fire, flood, a market shock, a hostile intrusion. Such a force never defeats defences one at a time. It looks for their greatest common denominator, the one condition that kills several at once. Safe design mirrors the attacker: write down the greatest common denominator for each group of defences, which is the five-family list, then decide either to eliminate it through heterogeneity, segregation and dispersal, or to price it as a budget line for residual risk. An organization that has done both is helped by thinking like an attacker, since every condition capable of killing several defences at once is on the register, and the thickness of the register is the real safety of the system. Where the register is thin, the likely reason is that nobody has counted yet.

The feel of the thing changes once you have counted. In a review meeting, a reader trained by this chapter hears "double assurance" or "multiple layers of protection" and no longer relaxes; the reflex is to take the phrase apart. The standard move is three questions, difference, independent validation, worst condition, as in section 7. After three questions, double assurance usually shows one of two colours: real redundancy, with difference and validation both present, or paper redundancy, same source and never rehearsed. The two colours lead to different places, real redundancy into the asset column of the annual report, paper redundancy into the downgrade list and the gap line of the policy reconciliation. What this chapter wants to leave in the reader's hands is the craft of turning an adjective into a ledger entry. The word safety is worth exactly as much in this trade as the number of times it has been taken apart. If two sanding systems can both fail in silence, the shared item is the same one: waiting.

#### Case five (aviation): One rupture and two hydraulic systems (full card)

**Case ID**: av-007

**When and where**: Pushback at 21:10 on 12 August 2024, takeoff at 21:39, landing at 01:12 on 13 August. Narita International Airport, Chiba Prefecture, Japan, near 35°44′25″N 140°23′34″E. The tyre rupture happened near taxiway A between W6 and W5. Takeoff and landing were both on runway 16R, and the destination had been Los Angeles International. The first officer, 55, was PM in the right seat, with 3,830 hours total and 161 hours 14 minutes on type. Five supernumeraries were on board. After this point the heading had no second possibility, and the only open question was weight.

**Options ruled out**: Stopping on the taxiway and asking maintenance to look at the wheels from the ground. The report does not record the option being discussed, and its cost is a delay and taxiway occupancy. Returning immediately after takeoff: the report states that weight had to come below maximum landing weight first, so the aircraft flew toward Los Angeles (p. 2). Landing overweight: the report records no evaluation of the option, only the constraint that weight had to come down (p. 2). Lowering the gear normally: the checklist required the alternate method, and the crew followed it (p. 3).

**Early signals**: ① On 21 June 2024 a second retread went onto position No. 7 of the body gear, and it ran 89 sectors to the event (p. 6). Why it was pressed past: preparation followed the company maintenance procedure (p. 5), and the procedure at the time did not prohibit retreads at positions No. 7, 8, 11 and 12; it prohibited them only after the accident (p. 9). ② Tyre pressure check results had not been kept as records in the maintenance management system, and recording them in a traceable form became a requirement only after the accident (p. 9). Why it was pressed past: paper records existed and could be read, but could not be assembled into a trend. ③ The daily check had no requirement on when to measure, and only after the accident was a cooling condition added: in principle after the brakes have cooled and the temperature has stabilised, more than 2 hours after block-in (p. 9). Why it was pressed past: following the job card was compliance, and the fact that a hot tyre reads high was not on the card. ④ The daily work instruction contained a clause allowing a tyre or brake that needed replacement to be deferred where replacement might delay the service (p. 9, deleted after the accident). Why it was pressed past: the clause authorised it. The report does not say the clause was used in this case. ⑤ No cockpit visibility of tyre pressure (p. 7). Why it was pressed past: TPIS is optional and airworthiness does not require it.

**The point of escalation**: 21:39, runway 16R, rotation. Before rotation the only place that could see the wheels was off the aircraft: the photographer beside the taxiway recorded the bang and the tread block turning with the wheel (p. 7). After rotation nothing on board could see the tyres, and the aircraft lifted off with a wheel already stripped. This escalation has no document and no signature; its carrier is one push of the thrust levers. The report can give only two time anchors: the 21:39 takeoff and the second warning 13 minutes later (p. 2). Inside those 13 minutes the record holds two things, the No. 4 checklist and the request for a runway inspection (p. 2); the aircraft climbed to 12,000 ft, and the presumed track is drawn on figure 2 of the report (pp. 2, 3). The warning level did not match the damage. Two brake hydraulic systems damaged at the same location were reported at the lowest level, Advisory, and the only Warning pointed at another system, cabin pressurisation. The third came back as an Advisory, two and a half hours later (pp. 2, 3).

**Failure chain**: Technical layer. The pressure loss in the No. 7 tyre, cause undetermined (p. 8), collapsed the contact patch, loaded the junction of tread and sidewall, and the tread cracked and separated (p. 8). The separated tread stayed on the wheel and turned with it, slapping the same shock strut repeatedly during the taxi, and the No. 1 and No. 4 brake hydraulic lines on that strut were ruptured as a result (p. 8). The white mist during the takeoff roll marks the point at which the piping is presumed to have failed (p. 8). The bare wheel kept rolling, hub components fractured and flew, and the debris punctured the pressure bulkhead as the gear retracted (p. 8). One source, three damages: No. 4 hydraulics, No. 1 hydraulics, cabin pressurisation.

Organizational layer. The two brake hydraulic systems run along the same shock strut. The report's own wording is that the No. 1 and No. 4 brake hydraulic systems on the same main gear shock strut were damaged (p. 8); their functions are primary and backup (pp. 2, 3), and their physical location is shared. The cockpit had no tyre pressure display and TPIS was not fitted (p. 7). All four inspections happened before the damage, and between the damage and liftoff there was no ground inspection at all (pp. 2, 5). Pressure records never entered the system, so no trend was visible (p. 9). The sensation of running over something during taxi had no corresponding handling clause, and the report contains no quantified trigger for one.

Incentive layer. The daily work instruction said in writing that a replacement which might cause a delay could be put off (p. 9). Tyre pressure was measured hot, so the reading ran high and the check passed (p. 9).

The break sits at the seam between the organizational and design layers. Design and maintenance procedure each complied. One put two lines on a single strut; the other left tyre pressure to a daily reading and a walkaround. Neither was answerable for the possibility that one physical event could reach both.

**Minimum counterfactual**: After the one vibration, stop the aircraft on the taxiway and ask maintenance to look at positions No. 7 and No. 8 from the ground. One action, taken before 21:39, at the cost of a delay and some taxiway occupancy. No luck is involved: the separated tread had not come off at that point and was still turning with the wheel, and the video shows it (p. 7); the place where the crew felt the vibration and the place where the video records the bang are the same stretch (pp. 2, 7). The action needs no luck and no new basis for judgement. It needs the sensation of running over something to be treated as real damage rather than a bump in the surface. The report's only judgement about early detection is the possibility it raises about TPIS (p. 8); it does not evaluate a stop and a look. The second half of the action is also in the report: once tread separation is confirmed, the response is a wheel change, and the post-accident corrective action made it explicit that a radial tyre whose tread has separated must be replaced (p. 9). The job card at the time did not carry that line.

**What happened**: The aircraft landed on 16R at 01:12 on 13 August, stopped after the runway centreline marker on manual braking, and the runway stayed closed for about 7 hours until the aircraft was moved to a stand (p. 3). The airframe damage was substantial: the pressure bulkhead at the top of the main wheel well had an opening about 25 cm (10 in) fore and aft and about 38 cm (15 in) across, within the scope of a major repair; the No. 7 and No. 8 wheel assemblies were damaged; hydraulic components on the shock strut were fractured and buckled; parts of the gear door were missing; and there were about 80 cracks and dents on the lower left fuselage skin and the left horizontal stabiliser (pp. 3, 4). The 7 people aboard were uninjured (p. 1). On the ground, the runway surface carried straight and curved scrape marks and damaged light fittings; door fragments and tyre pieces were found on taxiway A, and multiple pieces including tyre and brake parts on the runway (p. 5). The report was decided on 11 July 2025 and runs to 9 pages (p. 1).

The three questions, looking back. The most common answer to the first is "monitor the hydraulics closely after takeoff", and monitoring cannot change a physical fact that has already happened; the stop and look is the action that would have caught the event on the taxiway. The item most easily missed under the second is the scope of the confidence carried by "if one fails there is another": it holds for independent failure and does not hold for common-cause failure, and the wheel well is precisely where the two systems physically coexist. The third question lands on the handling standard for a taxi anomaly. Abnormal vibration plus a suspicion about a tyre should mean stopping to look.

The cause section uses presumed throughout, and the cause of the No. 7 pressure loss is stated as undeterminable (p. 8). Preventive measures come in two parts. Section 5.2 lists the operator's 8 changes to the daily work instruction, among them measuring after cooling, printing hot and cold permissible pressures directly on the job card, deleting the clause that let a delay take precedence, prohibiting retreads at certain positions, entering records into the system, and making gauge numbers and calibration dates traceable (p. 9). Primary source: JTSB, AA2025-6-2, 2025, link in Appendix B av-007.

### Case three (healthcare): The purple syringe and the second pair of eyes

me-003. Paediatric care in the United Kingdom, sedation before an elective renal biopsy. HSIB report (*Inadvertent administration of an oral liquid medicine into a vein*, 2019, archived snapshot read by this book): oral midazolam was drawn into an intravenous syringe and given intravenously by mistake. Two lines of defence existed. A purple syringe reserved for oral medicines, which is device-level segregation, since the colour makes oral and intravenous equipment physically hard to confuse. And a double check before administration, which is process-level segregation. Both failed in the same event: the syringe was substituted, and the double check degraded into a formality. The child came to no obvious harm. The report cites a national figure of 61 wrong-route administrations and makes four safety recommendations (2019/028–031).

Stop at the decision point: the preparation room, just before administration, with the double check about to begin. What was on the table: the purple syringe out of stock or not to hand, two busy colleagues, and two signature boxes on the check sheet. The shared item behind both lines of defence was normal conditions, meaning a purple syringe available and a second person free. Break that premise and both fail together. (What happened: wrong-route administration, no harm.) Primary source: HSIB, 2019, link in Appendix B me-003 (archived link, grade A−).

The shared item in case three, normal conditions, has a generalisation worth keeping: a line of defence depends on resources. The purple syringe is a consumable and can be out of stock; the second person is labour and can be busy; both lines rest on an unstated premise of adequate resourcing. Adequate resourcing holds on most days, and the longer it holds the less visible the dependency becomes, which is the mechanism of Chapter 20 projected onto redundancy. The remedy is a stress register for the resource dependency of each line: how much of this line remains when consumables run out, when staff are short, when an outsourced provider changes hands. The output of that register is usually brutal, since most multi-line designs have a real defence count of one when resources are tight. The brutality has one use, which is to pull the language of double insurance back to arithmetic. The premium for insurance, meaning heterogeneity and redundant resources, has to be affordable on the tightest day, or the policy is a weekend edition.

### Case four (chemical & process): A fallback on the same power supply

ch-003. Arkema's organic peroxide plant at Crosby, Texas, late August 2017. CSB report (pages read by this book): the flooding from Hurricane Harvey cut power to the site's refrigeration, the stored organic peroxides warmed, the first trailers self-ignited on 31 August, and the rest were deliberately burned on 3 September. The emergency plan made transfer to refrigerated trailers the fallback after a power loss. The fallback sat under the same common cause as the primary system: the flood took out refrigeration in the buildings and also the refrigeration in the trailers and the site roads. On paper the backup was independent. In the topology of a flood it shared one life with the primary.

Stop at the decision point: the moment when the emergency plan named the refrigerated trailers as the fallback for a power loss, and was approved. What was on the table: a list of trailer refrigeration capacity, and an assumption that the site had never flooded. A common-cause review has one question: will the cause that takes out the primary act on you as well. The flood answered yes. (What happened: serial fires and explosions, with evacuation of the surrounding area.) Primary source: CSB, *Arkema Inc. Chemical Plant Fire*, 2018, link in Appendix B ch-003.

The flood in case four is worth a second reading from the angle of how plans are written. Plans are written by scenario, with the power-loss scenario and the flood scenario kept apart, and when the fallback was approved inside the power-loss scenario its authors stayed inside that scenario, asking what to do when the power goes. The common-cause question, which is why the power went and what else that cause will touch, belongs to the territory between scenarios. The remedy in plan writing is a cross-scenario reading step: when a fallback is finished, go back to the physical cause that triggered it and read the other scenarios along that cause. If the flood both causes the power loss and floods the trailers, then the power-loss fallback has to be reread inside the flood scenario. The step costs half a day per plan, and what it stops is the common cause that lives in the seams between scenarios. Most common causes do not live inside a scenario. They live on its boundaries.

#### Case six (maritime): Two emergency items fail together (condensed)

ma-009. The stern trawler *Ocean Way*, November 2014, United Kingdom waters. MAIB report (2015, pages read by this book): running before a following sea, the deck flooded and the vessel capsized, killing 3 and leaving 2 survivors. The redundancy in the emergency equipment failed in pairs. The liferaft was trapped by its cradle and never released. The EPIRB, the emergency position indicating radio beacon, had no GNSS positioning, so the search and rescue datum took 47 minutes to establish. Two emergency lines, one for escape and one for location, were each present on paper and each failed in the real scenario. Their shared item is that neither had ever been validated in that scenario, neither the cradle release nor the beacon's positioning. Minimum counterfactual: annual checks of emergency equipment under real conditions, one real release and one real positioning test. Primary source: MAIB, 2015, link in Appendix B ma-009.

#### Case seven (aviation): Recurrence after the required actions were done (condensed)

av-027. A 767 freighter, October 2025, United Kingdom. AAIB report (2026, pages read by this book): the aileron control jammed during climb and the aircraft landed safely. The type has a history of ice-induced jamming, and both the mandated modification and the mandated inspection had been completed. The failure came back after the defence, modification plus inspection, was complete on paper, which shows a gap between the scope in which a defence is effective and the scope in which it is exposed: the scenarios the modification covers do not fully overlap the icing conditions actually met. The illusion takes this form here, the assumption that doing the required actions is safety. The actions were done, so the defence was recorded as present. Minimum counterfactual: bring the recurrence conditions into the control set used to validate the modification. Primary source: AAIB, 2026, link in Appendix B av-027.

#### Case eight (aviation): A performance error that no defence caught (condensed)

av-028. A Boeing 737-8 (G-CRUX), April 2025, London Luton. AAIB correspondence investigation (2026, pages read by this book): the crew computed performance for the full runway and took off from an intersection, the climb was extremely shallow, and the aircraft crossed the runway end at 13 ft. The airport's dispatch defences were several, a performance sheet recheck, a runway threshold confirmation and post-hoc FDM monitoring, and none of them caught the error on the spot. Only the FDM data found it afterwards. The shared blind spot of several defences is the assumption that the airport reference point used in the calculation matches the one used in the takeoff, and the independence between the defences did not cover that assumption. Minimum counterfactual: a consistency check between the performance sheet and the actual takeoff point, made a fixed dispatch item. Primary source: AAIB, 2026, link in Appendix B av-028.

## 22.4 What shows up earliest

Six weak signals, each with the reason usually used to press past it.

1. The backup and the primary share a supplier, an implementer or an operations team. Reason: "it integrates well and they respond fast."
2. Every switchover rehearsal has been done at night in a low-load window, never at peak. Reason: "we can't take the risk with the business."
3. The alarms from both systems feed one duty desk and one person. Reason: "centralised monitoring is more efficient."
4. The backup's specification was adapted from the primary's drawings. Reason: "to guarantee consistency."
5. The fallback in the emergency plan never lists the failure conditions it shares with the primary. Reason: "the plan is written by scenario." (Arkema's flood is the common variable between scenarios.)
6. The full version of "we have two" has never been questioned: two of what, different in what way, validated by whom. Reason: "why go into that much detail."

Put the six signals back against the eight cases. Signal 1, the same supplier, matches the trailers in case four: fallback and primary sit inside one procurement radius. Signal 2, rehearsals in low-load windows only, matches all eight cases, since not one of them went through a real switchover or a real-scenario validation. Signal 3, one duty desk, matches the sanding in case two: automatic and manual state feed a single judgement in one cab. Signal 4, an adapted specification, matches the hydraulics in case five: two lines routed under the constraint of one general-arrangement drawing. Signal 5, a common variable between scenarios, matches the flood in case four. Signal 6, the missing follow-up question, is the problem in case one: the "two" of ADS-B map and eyes was never questioned as to whether the other aircraft carried the equipment at all.

Sort the eight cases by the five families. Environment: the flood in case four. Design: the line routing in case five and the reference point assumption in case eight. Operational: the coverage gap between modification and inspection in case seven. Behavioural: the cession in case one, the division of labour in case two, normal conditions in case three. Certification: the paper validation in case six, and the dual certification of device and process in case three. The distribution suggests an order for registering. Environment and certification are the hardest to audit from inside, because they sit outside the organization's field of view, so bring in an outside view for those two first. Then self-audit the behavioural and design families. Operational comes last, since daily familiarity covers it most easily.

## 22.5 How to defend

Device 1: the common-cause register. Every redundant pair is made to register its shared items: power, network, vendor, algorithm, site, people, assumption. Each item carries the note that says whether both halves fail at once when this common cause arrives. The register is updated every year, and it and the dependency map of Chapter 21 index each other. The review item in the Arkema case comes from here: the register for a fallback must contain a line for the failure conditions it shares with the primary system.

Device 2: the heterogeneity standard. Critical redundancy has to be heterogeneous in at least one dimension: a different principle, a different vendor, a different team. Automatic and manual sanding, the syringe colour and the double check, the map and the lookout: in every one of them the failure happened where the two sides were alike. The procurement cost of heterogeneity is real, and its consideration belongs on the purchase order. What the extra money buys is that the common cause is not shared.

Device 3: the true switchover. The validation standard is fixed in advance. A switchover must shut the primary down and be carried out under real load, at least once a year. Redundancy that cannot be switched for real is relabelled from redundancy to paper backup and dropped from the defence count. What that downgrade does is put the arithmetic back from multiplication to addition. The numbers get worse, and they are the true ones.

Apply the devices to this chapter's cases. Device 1 on case four: the register line for shared failure conditions gets flood level, site roads and the offsite grid. Device 2 on case three: the substitute procedure for when purple syringes are out of stock, temporary independent preparation by two people with a verbal read-back, is written in advance as the heterogeneous alternative rather than invented under pressure. Device 3 on case two: the real rehearsal of manual sanding is staged on wet rail, a genuine simulation of abnormal adhesion, not a button press on dry rail. Device 4, whose statement comes at the end of this section, on case six: the annual checks of liferaft release and EPIRB positioning are run under a simulated capsize rather than on a calm deck.

The four devices have four ways of dying. The common-cause register dies on granularity: it records same vendor and misses the same product line of the same vendor, where the common cause actually sits; the patch is to take entries down to model and batch. The heterogeneity standard dies on formal heterogeneity: the vendor changed, but both vendors buy the same upstream chips or the same steel, which is second-order same source; the patch is to ask about second-tier suppliers for critical components. The true switchover dies on scope shrinkage: the core function is switched and the edge functions stay on the primary; the patch is to tick the switchover scope item by item against the function list and keep the evidence. The fallback review dies on being a one-off: it was reviewed at approval and later revisions never were; the patch is to bind the plan version number to the review record. All four deaths answer to one health question: when did this redundancy last work under bad conditions. Where the answer is never, the status drops automatically to paper backup, under the downgrade clause of Device 3.

Redundant procurement and design carry one economic point worth writing down. The heterogeneity premium is real: the learning cost of a second supplier, the integration cost, the double line of spares. Every decision that suppresses it raises beta. The honest way to build the budget is to put two columns side by side, whole-life cost for the same-source option and for the heterogeneous option, and a third column for the difference in risk, meaning the difference in common-cause exposure. Most organizations compare only the first two columns, and with the third missing, saving money wins on paper every time. The third column does not need precision, a range is enough. What it needs is someone required to fill it in, booking the externality to the account as writing three in Chapter 17 directs, and from then on common-cause risk has a price.

Once the price exists, one psychological obstacle remains: the comfort of homogeneity. An operations team's familiarity with same-source systems is real efficiency, with faster fault-finding, common spares and shared on-call training, and heterogeneity is worse on every one of those counts. The true benefit of heterogeneity, that the common cause is not shared, is probabilistic, delayed and invisible. Trade certain efficiency for probabilistic safety and an organization will pick efficiency again and again, unless the probabilistic side is put in front of it often. The carrier for that reminder is the quarterly review of the common-cause register, where each shared item is read aloud to the operations team itself. The cost is trivial. What it maintains is the presence of the heterogeneity benefit in the mind of the decision-maker, and the rate at which that presence decays is the rate at which beta climbs back. The quarterly review is best understood as the frequency at which probabilistic safety is renewed.

The history of the idea is worth a paragraph, because it explains where the tests come from. The golden age of redundancy design was aviation and nuclear power, with fault trees, dual channels and diverse design, and the engineers of that era were close to obsessive about common cause, since Three Mile Island and Chernobyl were industry memory. Software took the practice over later, with multiple availability zones and multi-active architectures, and its sensitivity to common cause lagged behind. Cloud availability zones that share one control plane, with cases two and five of Chapter 21 both sitting in the control plane, show that a zone in a multi-zone architecture delivers physical redundancy while leaving logical redundancy untouched. The handover between the two generations has one clear point: the physical era looked for common causes in the environment, the logical era looks for them in the control plane and in the assumptions. Today's reader has to look in both, because the system holds cables and server rooms alongside algorithms and default semantics, and the five families were assembled from the failure history of both generations.

Device 4: same-cause review of the fallback. Every fallback in an emergency plan answers one question: will the cause that takes out the primary act on you as well. The question costs one line of text. What it buys is an Arkema-type scenario stopped on paper.

## 22.6 Boundaries with neighbouring mechanisms

Against hidden coupling (Chapter 21): coupling is the hidden line between independent systems, where nobody knows they are connected. The illusion is same-source inside a redundant pair, where everyone knows they are connected and nobody takes it seriously. The hidden lines that Chapter 21's mapping draws become this chapter's common-cause entries the moment they enter a redundancy register.

Against the slack of success (Chapter 20): slack lets a backup go unvalidated, because nobody feels the need to rehearse. The illusion lets a backup go un-heterogenised, because it was never independent at the point of purchase. Slack is a disease of time and the illusion is a disease of structure, and an organization that has been slack for long enough will see its redundancy register drift toward same-source on its own.

Against compliant and unsafe (Chapter 32): a compliance check counts the number of defences. This chapter counts the kinds. Domains where regulation requires redundancy and acceptance looks only at existence are where the illusion has the highest incidence.

## 22.7 The five self-checks

1. Write down three redundancies your organization is proud of. Where is the shared-item list for each one? Without a list, put quotation marks around the word redundancy.
2. When did your backup system last run independently of the primary? Who validated that it was independent?
3. At the last switchover rehearsal, was the primary actually shut down? Was the traffic real?
4. In your emergency plan, are the failure conditions shared by the fallback and the primary written down? Run one flood or one power loss through it.
5. How many defences exist on a screen, in a register or on a drawing but have never been validated on their own? Count them now.
