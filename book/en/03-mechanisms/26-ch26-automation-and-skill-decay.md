# Chapter 26 Automation and Skill Decay | Automation-Induced Skill Degradation; Out-of-the-Loop

> What this chapter covers: how human skill and takeover ability decay once automation moves people out of the control loop; the criteria, two deep field cases, and an honest account of the evidence gap.
> When to come back: when an automation project starts, when simulator and hands-on training hours get cut, or when "the system will handle it" becomes the reason not to practise.
> Reading time: about 50 minutes.

Automation and skill decay form a closed loop. The more automated a system becomes, the more a person needs to hold the skill for the rare takeover moment; and automation itself supplies the reason to cut the training that would keep it: "the system will handle it." Each turn of the loop leaves the person in the room more out of practice when the takeover moment arrives, and the takeover moment is designed to get harder, because the system absorbs every routine and leaves the entire residue of exceptions to that one person. This chapter's case library has an honest weak spot on this mechanism: the grade A cases that record a takeover failure caused by skill decay cluster in aviation. The cause of that weakness is part of the mechanism, since other domains do not have a long enough automation history and takeover failures have not yet entered their investigation systems. The gap is marked as a gap rather than filled with two or three thin cases.

## 1 What It Is

Definition: once automation has taken over most of the system's running time and most of its routine actions, the human's understanding of the system and the human's ability to run it by hand decay together. The moments when automation leaves the person in the loop are precisely the hardest ones. The operator degrades into a monitor, the monitor becomes a taker-over after a long quiet stretch, and takeover becomes the action with the fewest repetitions in a working life.

Three criteria start the mechanism. Any one of them and it is running:

1. The person has not performed an action for a long time, counted in months and years, and that action is still required in an accident.
2. The takeover moment is the hardest moment: automation covers the routine and leaves the whole non-routine residue to the person.
3. The manual and the training assume the person can do it, and no mechanism verifies that the person can.

Where the mechanism sits in the research literature is worth setting down. Lisanne Bainbridge's 1983 paper "Ironies of Automation" put the paradox at its root: what automation does best is the part the human least needs to do, what is left to the human is the part the human finds hardest, and the human spends the years of waiting for the hardest moment losing practice on all the others. Research forty years later added two pieces. The first is the out-of-the-loop problem: work on situation awareness shows that a person's grasp of system state on a monitoring post decays systematically with time, and re-entry at takeover needs a substantial stretch of cognitive rebuilding. The second is automation complacency: reliability research shows that the higher an automation's historical reliability, the looser the calibration of trust in it and the later the response to its alarms. Together the two give criterion 3 a measurement basis. Takeover ability is a stock you can measure, through quota, refresher and simulation. Measuring it is cheap. Not measuring it is expensive.

Two research lines meet at the workstation with a combined effect. Someone out of the loop, already decayed, meets a trust calibration leaning toward complacency, where fewer alarms mean more trust, and the real start of the takeover moment comes later than either factor alone would put it. Case One shows the combined effect most clearly: the crew did not fail to know where the flight mode annunciator sat. A calibrated trust had made "throttle in automatic" the default background, and a change in the background does not become a target for attention. Defence against the combination therefore runs on two tracks. The cure for out-of-the-loop is to pull the person back into the loop, with scheduled manual cross-checks of key parameters. The cure for complacency is to build trust calibration into the ordinary week, with periodic automation-anomaly drills that let people watch the system be wrong on a schedule. Neither cure is expensive. What is expensive is their absence, which costs nothing on any day before the accident.

## 2 How It Grows

Three generating conditions. Condition one: the level of automation rises. Nothing here is pathological. Higher automation is a choice, and usually the right one. The disease is in condition two. Condition two: training hours get compressed, and the compression is justified by the claim that the system handles it. Automation creates the training requirement, which is takeover skill, and supplies the reason to cut the training. The loop closes here, and every turn of it saves money. Condition three: rare scenarios. Takeover scenarios come up in real operations on a scale of years. Without institutionalised practice, proficiency in them only falls, and every system upgrade changes the takeover interface and the takeover sequence, depreciating whatever stock the last practice left.

Depreciation deserves its own arithmetic. Take the annual upgrade rate of the system and the annual decay rate of takeover skill; the two together give the total depreciation rate of takeover ability. Both are percentages, and added up they usually exceed the growth rate of the organization's training spend. The reason depreciation stays hidden is that no single item trips an alarm. Upgrades get tested. Training gets recorded. The composite, the present value of takeover ability, has nobody accounting for it. The report that would account for it is sketched in this chapter's Device 1, the quota, and Device 2, the simulation. The quota measures the stock, meaning whether the person can still do the thing. The simulation measures the increment, meaning whether the person can do it on the new interface. Draw both numbers as a curve by year and the slope is the depreciation reading. An organization whose reading is not negative, whose stock is rising, hardly exists. The management target is not zero decay. It is to make the slope of training spend catch the slope of depreciation.

One asymmetry in the economics of the loop has to be written down. The training cost saved is current, certain and small. The cost of a failed takeover is distant, probabilistic and large, and the bill often goes to people outside the system: passengers, users, patients. That asymmetry makes the loop survive every budget table, because each cut looks correct on its own. Put together they are the lesson aviation bought with decades of accidents: hand flying is a skill that costs money to maintain. Money spent on simulators is billed by the hour. Money spent on sea walls is billed in lives. The gap between the two prices is the budget argument for every defence in this chapter.

## 3 In the Field

### Case One (Aviation): The Autothrottle on a Visual Approach

**Case ID**: av-031.

**When and where**: 6 July 2013, about 11:27:50 Pacific Daylight Time; the sea wall outside the threshold of runway 28L at San Francisco International Airport, California, USA. The accident happened in the last 3.5 nautical miles of the descent, with the cockpit about 1,500 feet above the ground.

**What and who**: Asiana Airlines flight 214, a Boeing 777-200ER, registration HL7742, Incheon to San Francisco, operating under 14 CFR Part 129. The left seat held a trainee captain (PF, the pilot flying). The right seat held a captain acting as instructor and pilot-in-command (PIC), who was also the pilot monitoring (PM). The jump seat held a relief first officer acting as observer. The flight was the trainee captain's operating experience (OE) training flight and the first leg of his two-day schedule (p.20). On board were 291 passengers, 12 flight attendants and 4 flight crew, 307 people in total (p.31).

**What they were doing**: The trainee captain's 777 transition training began on 25 March 2013. He passed a simulator proficiency check on 18 May and a line-oriented flight training check on 30 May (p.32). From 14 June he flew observation legs, logging 9 hours 35 minutes. From 16 June he flew OE with an instructor (p.32). Of the 20 segments and 60 hours Korean regulation required, he had completed 8 segments and 33 hours 31 minutes by the day of the accident (p.33). Every earlier OE approach had been an ILS approach, and the syllabus did not require a visual approach during OE (p.33).

The day's actions, in cockpit voice recorder order. 09:38, back in the cockpit (p.21). 10:42:28, approach briefing: the 28L glideslope was out of service, they would hold lateral track on the localizer and manage the vertical profile with the automatic flight system (p.21). 10:47:28, descent checklist, Vref 132 knots (p.22). The 28L glideslope was out for construction, a notice to airmen had gone out, and the crew knew it from the briefing (p.95). 11:12:33, the relief first officer came back to the cockpit and sat in the jump seat to observe (p.23). 11:21:49, clearance for the visual approach, with the aircraft at 6,300 feet and 211 knots, the autothrottle in HOLD, and the automatic flight guidance system in FLCH SPD pitch and HDG SEL roll (p.23). 11:23:17, the controller asked them to slow to 180 knots and hold that to 5 nautical miles from the runway; the PM acknowledged (p.23). 11:23:57, pitch mode changed to vertical speed and the autothrottle to speed mode, with -900 fpm selected and changed to -1,000 fpm a second later (p.24). 11:25:55, crossing 5 nautical miles at 2,085 feet and 174 knots, 400 feet above the 3-degree profile (p.25).

**The decision point**: 11:21:49, on the radio, the PM answered "runway in sight" and the crew accepted the visual approach clearance (p.23). Accepting gave up the vertical guidance of an instrument approach. Only the localizer was available that day, and the localizer approach's minimum descent altitude was 460 feet (p.21, p.95).

11:23:17, on the same frequency, the crew accepted the restriction of 180 knots to 5 nautical miles (p.23). Under FLCH SPD the selected speed is held by the elevator, and selecting 180 knots from 212 pitches the nose up and drops the sink rate to about 300 fpm (p.24, p.96). 11:25:43, the PF set the MCP altitude window to 3,000 feet, the go-around altitude (p.24). 11:26:25.7, the pitch mode changed to FLCH SPD and the autothrottle went to THR (p.25). With 3,000 feet sitting above the aircraft, the system began to accelerate and climb; the thrust levers moved forward and the nose came up slightly (p.25). Working from the flight data recorder, the PF's interview and the actions that followed, the report judged that the person who pressed FLCH was most likely the PF (p.99), and that the selection was not called out as Asiana's 777 pilot operating handbook required (p.99).

11:26:28.8, the PF disconnected the autopilot with a double click of the yoke switch, and the aural warning was suppressed (p.100). 11:26:33, he pulled the thrust levers back to idle by hand, cancelling the climb the system had just begun, and the autothrottle went to HOLD (p.25, p.99). 11:26:40 he called "flight directors off", and at 11:26:43 turned off his own side's switch (p.26). 11:27:14.8, passing 500 feet with a sink rate of about 1,200 fpm and thrust at idle, N1 about 24%, he had called neither "500" nor "stable" and had not gone around (p.28, p.101).

The organizational layer holds an earlier decision point. Asiana's pilot operating handbook said to use the autopilot and autothrottle to the maximum extent, and to use the autothrottle even when hand flying (p.120). In 2012, 77.7% of the company's 777 landings were manual landings, but that figure does not include the altitude at which the autopilot was disconnected (p.122). The design layer holds another. In 2010, during 787 certification flight testing, the FAA and EASA raised that this logic provides no minimum speed protection in FLCH or VNAV SPD combined with HOLD; Boeing substituted a note in the 787 airplane flight manual for a change to the logic, and the FAA accepted that outcome (p.114).

**What was visible then**: The flight mode annunciator at the top of both primary flight displays, showing autothrottle mode, roll mode and pitch mode from left to right. When a mode has just activated, its column carries a green box for 10 seconds (p.43). After 11:26:33 the autothrottle column read HOLD, with the green box (p.100). At 11:26:38 the MCP speed window was set to 137 knots (p.26). Between about 1,500 feet and 500 feet the thrust levers did not move once (p.106). The navigation display carried a green altitude arc showing where the aircraft would reach the MCP-selected altitude (p.96); the localizer 28L procedure had computed a profile, and the vertical path deviation indicator was on the screen as well (p.117).

Precision approach path indicator readings, in time order: 11:26:36 four white (p.26); 11:27:14.8 three white one red (p.28); 11:27:19.8 two white two red (p.28); 11:27:24.1 one white three red (p.28); 11:27:31 four red (p.28). The speed tape and the vertical speed indicator were equally in front of them: at 500 feet, 137 knots and 1,200 fpm (p.28); 11:27:23.3, below Vref 132 knots (p.28); 11:27:31, 122 knots (p.28). In the seconds where speed held briefly at 134 knots, the trend arrow still pointed down (p.106).

The observer called "sink rate sir" twice, at 11:26:58 and 11:27:05 (p.27). He said afterwards that his basis was the company operations manual, which set a sink rate not greater than 1,000 fpm below 1,000 feet (p.101). The stable approach criteria and the 500-foot callout requirement were on the table too: the PM was to call "500" and the PF to answer "stable" or "go around" (p.101).

Information that only came out afterwards. None of the three crew remembered seeing HOLD displayed (p.25). In his interview the PF's account of the autothrottle mode logic did not match the manual: he said that after a manual override the autothrottle resumes control of speed (p.111). If both flight director switches are in the off position, the modes are cleared and the autothrottle goes to SPD and holds 137 knots (p.100, p.120). The autothrottle engage function is not available in FLCH SPD, and low speed protection did not exist at that moment (p.45, p.112). On performance modelling, beginning the go-around 11 seconds (124 feet) earlier would likely have avoided ground impact (p.108).

**What happened**: 3 passengers died, one of them after being taken to hospital, and 49 people were seriously injured: 40 passengers, 8 flight attendants and 1 flight crew member. The remaining 248 passengers, 4 flight attendants and 3 flight crew members were lightly injured or uninjured (p.15, p.31). Of the 307 people on board, 99% survived (p.15). The aircraft was destroyed by impact and fire, and the sea wall and the indicator lights were damaged (p.14, p.32). The NTSB determined the probable cause as: the crew's mismanagement of the descent profile during a visual approach, the PF's inadvertent deactivation of automatic speed control, the crew's inadequate monitoring of airspeed, and the delayed execution of a go-around after the deviation was detected (p.147).

Going back to the three questions. The most common answer to the first is "go around at 500 feet". That action's cost in this case lay in the record rather than in the flying: four red on an OE flight would very likely have been reported to the company's training supervisor (p.29, footnote 23), and industry statistics show 97% of unstable approaches continued to a landing (p.101). The item most often missed in the second question is the word HOLD at 11:26:33. It appeared less than a second after a thrust reduction the crew performed themselves, it lit for 10 seconds, and the fact that the thrust levers then stopped moving matched the expectation that speed had been set to 137 knots and the aircraft was decelerating (p.106). The right place to put the checkpoint, for the third question, is the gate at 500 feet. The manual already carried the callouts and the go-around criteria. What was missing was execution and an independent cross-check.

**Options ruled out**: Speedbrakes to increase the sink rate; the report records that he did not use them (p.96). Flaps 20 at 8.5 nautical miles: speed was 188 knots, 7 knots below the flaps 20 limit, and the report records that he could have done it but did not; selecting flaps earlier would have allowed a steeper descent and saved deceleration time later (p.96). Vertical navigation: the flight management computer had already computed a 3-degree profile on the localizer 28L procedure and displayed vertical path deviation on both navigation displays, and the PF said in interview that he never used it (p.117). Flying the localizer approach: a minimum descent altitude of 460 feet was available, steadier than working out the whole profile by eye (p.21). The PM taking control: he had 3,208 hours on the 777 (p.34), but the role confusion created by two captains in the same cockpit kept that path from starting (p.106). Turning off both flight directors: Asiana's informal practice required turning both off and then turning the PM's one back on; in fact only the PF's was turned off (p.26, p.120). The PF saying out loud that he was uneasy about the missing glideslope: that is not on the cockpit voice recorder, and the report judged that if he had said it, the PM might have suggested flying vertical navigation (p.105).

**Early signals**: 11:23:50, sink rate fell to about 300 fpm (p.24). This was explained away as complying with the controller's speed restriction. 11:25:46, crossing DUYET at 2,250 feet, 450 feet above the 1,800-foot reference for that point (p.25); this was overridden by the note that a visual approach does not require a crossing altitude (p.23, footnote 10). 11:24:32, the observer reminded them "180 knots to 5", the PF answered "huh?", the observer repeated, and only then came "okay" (p.24). 11:24:53, the PM said "this looks a little high", the PF said "yes", the PM said "should be a bit high", the PF asked "you mean too high", then said "I'll descend a bit more" and changed the vertical speed to -1,500 fpm (p.24). The conversation turned a deviation into an adjustable parameter. 11:25:31, vertical speed went from -1,500 back to -1,000, a change the report judged inappropriate because the aircraft was still several hundred feet high on the profile (p.97). The observer called sink rate at 11:26:58 and 11:27:05, the PF answered "yes sir", the sink rate briefly decreased, and the observer stopped calling (p.101). 11:27:14.8, passing 500 feet at 1,200 fpm and N1 24%, with speed sitting exactly at 137 knots (p.28); compliance on speed masked thrust not being in place. On the training side: in a 2012 joint survey by Boeing, the International Air Transport Association and the International Federation of Air Line Pilots' Associations, 42% of the transitioning pilots surveyed said training on automatic flight systems was minimal or insufficient, 23% were only marginally comfortable on their first line flight, and 62% needed more than 3 months to become comfortable (p.113). On the design side: in 2010, at 787 certification, the FAA and EASA had already raised the hazard in the same logic (p.114), and a note in a manual overrode it.

**The point of escalation**: 11:26:33. The thrust levers went to idle and the autothrottle went to HOLD. From then on it no longer moved the thrust levers, and speed was managed by the human alone. The only function in the system that restores speed by itself is autothrottle engagement, and it is explicitly excluded in FLCH SPD pitch mode (p.45). HOLD has five exit conditions: reaching the MCP target altitude, selecting a new pitch or autothrottle mode, turning off the autothrottle switch, manually advancing the levers past the thrust limit, and disconnecting the autopilot with both flight directors off (p.45). At 11:26:43 only the PF's was turned off, so the fifth condition was not met (p.26, p.100). At 11:26:36 the PM said "speed", the PF answered "target speed 137", and at 11:26:38 set it in the speed window (p.26), which confirmed once more that speed still had a target. From the autopilot disconnect to impact was 81 seconds (p.121); HOLD lasted until 11:27:44.7, when movement of the thrust levers cleared it, 71 seconds in all (p.30). By 11:27:14.8, passing 500 feet, the chain had no way back: idle thrust, N1 24%, 500 feet above the ground, 1,200 fpm (p.28). Nobody approved that step. The report attributes the action to the PF's hand (p.99). What approved it was an operations manual that wrote "maximize the use of automation" as policy (p.120).

**Failure chain**: Technical layer: the target altitude sat above the aircraft and FLCH SPD climbs by its own logic; the manual override of the thrust levers triggered HOLD; HOLD provides no minimum speed protection (p.114); HOLD triggers no aural warning, because the warning only sounds when the autothrottle is disconnected (p.115); the interface cue is a 10-second green box (p.43); speed at 500 feet happened to be back at 137 knots and held there for about 4 seconds (p.101, p.106).

Organizational layer: the training material came from Boeing's flight crew training manual and did not explain the cases in which the autothrottle will not re-engage by itself (p.112); a stall protection demonstration in the simulator left the impression that the autothrottle wakes up on its own, and the report writes that this very likely gave him false confidence (p.112); a ground instructor had met "anomalies" three times and mentioned them only verbally in class, never in the syllabus (p.112); the callout discipline was not followed at several points on this leg (p.105, p.106); two captains in the same cockpit produced role confusion (p.106); the right seat was flying as an instructor for the first time (p.35); the PF believed only the captain could call a go-around, while the manual says the first officer may also call it when flying (p.110); all three were on duty in a circadian low, and the PF had slept 5.5 hours in the 24 before the accident (p.103, p.104).

Incentive layer: OE is the check before release to the line, and four red would be reported to the training supervisor (p.29, footnote 23); the manual required maximum use of automation (p.120); 77.7% of manual landings were concentrated below 1,000 feet (p.122), so the quota number looked good while the scenarios it covered were the wrong ones; 97% of unstable approaches were continued (p.101).

The junction breaks between the organizational layer and the incentive layer. Policy wrote "use automation" as the correct practice. The check wrote "traces of instability" as a deduction. Both incentives pointed the same way, so no layer had a reason to go and look at the silent change called HOLD.

**Minimum counterfactual**: at 11:26:43, before turning off the PF's flight director, the PM turns off his own first, or both at the same time. The report's own words: if both flight director switches are in the off position, the automatic flight guidance system's modes are cleared and the autothrottle goes to SPD and holds the 137 knots selected on the MCP (p.100, p.120). One action, 14 seconds after the autopilot disconnect, needing no extra information and no luck: both switches sit on the MCP, the PM's hand was already there, and this was Asiana's own informal practice, merely executed in the wrong order (p.120). From 11:26:43 to impact there were 67 seconds left, enough for the autothrottle to hold 137 knots down to 500 feet. The report issued recommendation A-14-54 on that basis (p.150). The cost is zero and the division of labour does not change: the action falls to the same hand. Set a different counterfactual beside it: "go around earlier" would have to overturn the check, and "more manual flying" would have to change the syllabus, and neither of those is a single action.

**Outcome and sources**: 3 passengers died, 49 were seriously injured, 99% of the 307 on board survived; the aircraft was destroyed (p.14, p.15, p.31). The NTSB issued 21 new recommendations (A-14-37 to A-14-57), addressed to the FAA, Asiana Airlines, Boeing, the airport firefighting working group and the City of San Francisco; 4 went to Asiana and 2 to Boeing. The four directly relevant here are: enhance 777 autothrottle training (A-14-37), a special certification design review of the 777 automatic flight control system (A-14-42), guidance on shutting down flight directors after disconnecting the autopilot on a visual approach (A-14-54), and a revision of automation policy to increase manual flying (A-14-55) (pp. 148–150). After the accident Asiana added straight-in approaches and speed-constrained approaches to its 777 transition syllabus (p.118). Primary source: NTSB, Aircraft Accident Report NTSB/AAR-14/01, July 2014, official PDF, 207 pages, link at Appendix B, av-031. Page numbers in this card follow the PDF page order.

One general lesson about mode awareness translates for readers outside aviation. Every automated system has a board that says which mode it is in, and the characters on that board have to pass three checks: whether they can be noticed, whether they can be read correctly, and whether someone outside the system can verify them independently. The three checks are the routine checkup for automation, and most systems only do the second, which is writing the characters correctly. The FMA on Asiana 214 lost points on all three, each for its own reason: the change was too quiet, the characters too small, and there was no external verification. Turning the three checks into a list gives the monthly sheet for an automated workstation, and the cost is one walkthrough of the workstation.

One detail of a decision point in Case One deserves its own paragraph: the delay before the go-around decision. Between the obvious anomaly of a sinking glideslope and the execution of the go-around sits a stretch of crew consultation, and that delay is the readout of how long the last link of the takeover chain took, the chain being recognise the anomaly, reach agreement, execute the action. The go-around itself is well covered in training, since it is a standard manoeuvre. What jammed the chain was the middle step, the social cost of saying "go around" from an unequal seat. The skill problem meets the problem in Chapter 19 here: however fluent the technical links of the takeover chain, a chain that jams at the speaking link still misses.

In Case One the miss cost three lives; in an ordinary organization the same miss costs a delayed process. The size of the cost does not change the conclusion: speaking up is part of the takeover chain, and practising takeover means practising speech. The drill design for it is cheap. Fix one role in every exercise, the most junior member present, and make that person responsible for calling the anomaly out loud; the consequence of a wrong call is one line in the exercise log. After ten repetitions the muscle for speaking grows alongside the muscle for taking over. In organizations where it does not grow, the problem is usually in the incentive rather than the muscle, because the person who spoke up was once answered with a question, and the repair of incentives belongs to another chapter (Chapter 19). This chapter's job is to put speaking on the list.

Beyond the list, one everyday device for calibrating trust in automation is worth writing down: let the system fail on purpose where people can see it. It sounds like a prank and is a mature practice in reliability engineering, the source of the idea behind chaos engineering. Inject real failure shapes into the exercise environment on a schedule, and real means shapes copied from postmortems rather than textbook failures, so that users see periodically what automation failure looks like. Someone who has watched it fail moves the anchor of trust from historical reliability to the possibility of failure, and the start of takeover moves earlier with it. The cost of injection is exercise environment resources. The return is written on Case One's bill: the price of one silent mode change would fund ten years of injection.

One discipline in the design of injection deserves emphasis: the faults injected should come from real postmortems, your own industry's or an adjacent one's, and should not be invented. An invented fault tests the system's theoretical weaknesses. A real fault tests its actual weaknesses, and the two distributions rarely overlap, since faults in postmortems are always stranger than designers imagined. The dark-line archive of Chapter 21 supplies material here directly, because a dark-line fault carries both realism and surprise. The two chapters' devices mesh at this point: a mapped dark line is a topic in the injection script library.

A second supply route for the library comes from the user side. Automatic client behaviour such as retries, caching and local queueing is a new failure source in the automated era, and the retry storm in Case Two is user automation fighting platform automation. Injection of client behaviour, simulating thousands of users retrying at once, has mature tooling in the software industry, and its equivalent outside software is simulation of collective demand-side behaviour, with passengers rebooking at once or patients arriving at once. Adversarial drills with automation on both sides, the platform injecting faults and the client injecting a storm, are the full-stack rehearsal of the automated era. The era of rehearsing the server side alone is over.

Alongside that, one new regression has to be guarded against: automating the drill itself, with one-click exercises, generated faults and automatically scored results. A fully automated drill saves labour and quietly reproduces this chapter's mechanism, because the hand feel of the drill decays too and the people who design drills stop taking faults apart by hand. The right place for drill automation is the execution layer, where scripts run the routine injection. The design layer has to stay human, with each quarter's script written by hand once, because the writing is the designer's own takeover practice. Execution can be outsourced to a script. Understanding cannot. That is the general discipline of the automated era, and the general demand this chapter's mechanism makes of every tool.

That discipline has another face, which is an opportunity. The spread of automation has pushed the marginal cost of drills to a historic low, with simulation environments billed by the hour and fault injection copied by script, so the bottleneck in training supply has moved from resources to willingness. The organizational answer on willingness sits in budgets and appraisals, which are this chapter's Device 1 and the audit list. The cultural answer sits in narrative: tell takeover drills as capability building rather than as a cost centre. The fastest way to start that shift is to write up every successful takeover as a case, who held what under what conditions with which combination of actions, and put the case in the internal knowledge base and on the meeting agenda. The visibility of successful takeover is the fuel of a takeover culture: an organization that can see its heroes trains heroes.

A second half of the hero story has to follow at once, or the story spoils. Visibility of heroes does not mean dependence on heroes. The healthy shape of a takeover culture is that everyone has practised takeover, with the quota covering the whole staff, and the unhealthy shape is a few heroes covering the field while the rest watch. An organization built that way resets to zero on the day the hero leaves (the mobility mechanism of Chapter 28), while an organization where everyone practises has a substitute when any one person is absent (the cover matrix of Chapter 18). The watershed between the two cultures is visible in the drill roster: does the list rotate, with everyone on it, or is it fixed, with the specialists on it? Distributed takeover ability is part of organizational redundancy, and the principle of real rehearsal in Chapter 22 applies to redundancy in people as well: the real rehearsal of redundancy is a rotating drill roster.

Rotation carries a second function, across skill generations: veterans and newcomers drilling on the same floor. A mixed roster lets a newcomer learn sequence from a veteran's takeover, who throttles first and who rolls back first, and lets a veteran re-examine habits under a newcomer's questions about why that step comes first. Transmission between generations is the original channel by which skill survives, which is apprenticeship, and the drill institutionalises it into a transmission with a ledger. The ledger does not record who mentored whom, which is a personnel matter. It records the content transmitted, as in which three sequencing judgements this drill passed on, and the list goes into the knowledge base as teaching material for the next generation of newcomers. Organizational archiving of skill closes the loop at that point: individuals practise through quota, teams rehearse through rotation, the organization stores through the ledger, newcomers read through the material. Only when all four turn does takeover ability become an organizational asset rather than an individual gift.

Superstition about gifts is this mechanism's last stronghold at the cultural layer, and it deserves one deliberate demolition. A person who takes over well is often described as naturally calm, or quick to react. Temperament contributes, certainly, and close reading of postmortems gives a plainer answer: people who take over well are people fluent in sequence, who have rehearsed the order of the combination in their heads, and fluency in sequence comes from the density of practice and the quality of the review. Temperament sets how far performance decays under pressure. Practice sets the height of the baseline, and an organization with a high enough baseline still clears the pass mark after decay. An organization that attributes takeover to gift therefore skips the most important task, which is rostering the practice, and does the most useless one, which is recruiting for gift. The position here matches Chapter 14: skill can be measured and practised, gift can be neither, and the budget goes to the half that can be practised.

Within the practicable half, one principle borrowed from sports training belongs in the design of practice: layer the scenarios by realism. The base layer drills decomposed actions, the correctness and speed of a single step. The middle layer drills combined sequence, the order and trade-offs of several steps. The top layer drills the whole scenario under pressure, with incomplete information, time pressure and concurrent alarms. The recommended time split is an inverted pyramid, with the top layer most expensive and least frequent, since one top-layer exercise returns more than ten base-layer ones. The reason for layering is the transfer rule of skill: fluency in decomposed actions does not transfer by itself to combination, and fluency in combination does not transfer by itself to pressure. Transfer is bought one layer at a time, and the currency at every layer is exposures. The whole economics of this mechanism is buying enough exposures at each of the three layers.

One invisible opponent sits in the budget for those exposures: the training package sold with the automation. Procurement of an automation system often comes with training, a vendor engineer walking through the operation, and that training is positioned as an introduction to operation rather than takeover training, since the vendor has no postmortem library for your industry and cannot design scenarios at the takeover level. An organization that counts it in the training budget gets a falsely good reading on the first item of the audit list, the training hours curve. The definition of the training budget therefore has to be written into the rules: takeover training hours count only exercise hours built on scenarios from your own industry's postmortems, and vendor training is listed separately and not counted. One line of definition guards against substitution in the budget and forms the foundation of audit credibility.

On that foundation, an annual report on takeover ability is worth establishing as a fixed document. Four pages, one thing on each: quota completion, drill coverage, red and amber audit items, and the closure rate of the jam ledger, one page one chart one table, ten minutes for the governance layer to read. The design of the readership has a hidden use: the report is ammunition for budget negotiation, since red items map straight onto budget requests, and it is evidence of where responsibility was discharged, since an organization that reported a red item and had its budget cut stands in a very different position at the next accident review. Combined with the margin ledger of Chapter 25, the annual report becomes a joint document, a double annual report on defence and skill, and the organization that produces it gives the governance layer its first complete view of defence: what the system can do, what the people can do, and how much each can still absorb.

The double report's final page is left blank for the reader, as the book's convention requires. Write down three numbers: the automation coverage of your system, as the proportion of actions completed automatically; your own count of takeover practice this year; and your subjective rating of familiarity with manual mode. The combination reads in two lines. High coverage with little practice means every risk in this chapter is waiting for you. High coverage with plenty of practice means you are already the person this chapter set out to shape: automation works for me, and I stand watch on its boundaries.

The watch roster can be refined down to the four links of the takeover chain: recognise the anomaly, exit automation, execute by hand, restore automation. Each link has its own way of being practised. Recognition drills alarm triage, picking the true signal out of noise. Exit drills mode operation, the button sequence that hands the system back to human control. Manual execution drills psychomotor skill, the old subject the quota governs. Restoration drills handback, the correct posture and confirmation actions for returning the system to automation. Practice on the four links is often merged into one run, a single exercise from recognition to restoration. A merged run measures the whole; a layered exercise trains the links. An annual plan needs both, merged runs to measure the composite and layered exercises to fix the weak link, and the information about which link is weak comes from the jam ledger of each merged run: whichever link jammed most often becomes next quarter's layered priority.

The jam ledger carries one recording discipline worth writing down. Classify the cause of the jam when you record it, as not knowing what to do, knowing what to do but not being able to do it, or being able to do it but not daring to. The prescriptions for the three differ completely: the first is a problem of understanding boundaries and goes to Device 4, the second is psychomotor skill and goes to the quota, the third is a problem of speaking and authorization and goes to Chapter 19. A ledger that mixes them keeps every drill review at the surface, as in "it jammed for five minutes here". A ledger that classifies them takes the review straight to the prescription, as in "this is the third kind, go and look at the incentive". The label costs one field at recording time and buys a direct channel from drill output to training action.

The channel has one more station at its end: verification that the training worked, meaning whether the point that jammed last time still jams at the next drill. The logic is as plain as any training evaluation, since practice should produce a change, and the action is a closure rate statistic over the jam ledger, the share of entries that no longer jam on retest. Low closure means either the method is wrong, with the layering badly chosen, or the dose is too small, with too few exposures, or the target is wrong, with the wrong people trained. Each of the three has its own diagnostic path, change the layer, add volume, change the people, and the finding goes back into the ledger. The ledger then becomes the training system's own feedback loop, and this mechanism's ironic loop, in which automation eats training, is offset by a loop running the other way, in which training eats review. The long-run result of the two offsetting is a dynamic equilibrium of takeover ability: automation coverage rises, training supply rises with it, and the balance point is set jointly by budget and will.

Turnover is the final variable at the balance point, and it links to Chapter 28. The faster people move, the higher the training supply has to be, since the cost of rebuilding the stock in newcomers is continuous. High-turnover organizations, in consulting, outsourcing and rotation-heavy industries, therefore have to design takeover training as a production line: practise from the day of joining, quota by month, and a recorded handover drill at departure. The line costs more than a stable organization's annual plan, and its return is written in the handover checklist of Chapter 28: the people who leave do not take the organization's takeover floor with them.

Documentation standing in for people is the final line of that floor. Once the key knowledge of takeover is archived organizationally, meaning the scripts, the jam ledger, the four-link list and the one-pager, the organization's dependence on any individual drops to a substitutable level. The test of substitutability is a random name drill: assign any member to the takeover seat, once a quarter, and whoever is named takes it regardless of seniority. The deterrent value of the random name drill exceeds its training value, because it keeps everyone in a state of being nameable, and the by-product of the deterrence is that the three levels of practice become normal for everyone: knowing, being able to do, daring to step in. A team with all three is the best finished product this chapter's defences can buy. The price is a few hours of drill time per quarter, and the warranty is written in the result of every real takeover.

At this point the chapter's two main cards, Asiana in aviation and Cloudflare in software, have shown both faces of the mechanism: one is the heavy tuition bought by decades of automation history, and the other is a bill from the new generation of automation that has only started arriving. The blank between them, the other domains, is held by three extrapolations, and the honesty of the placeholder is written in the note on the evidence gap. The hope attached to the placeholder is that a later edition can put three real cards in this section. The precondition is that those domains start accumulating postmortem libraries, and the first entry in such a library can start with the reader's organization's next takeover drill.

Beyond drills, the chapter owes readers a word to the designers of automation, because half the responsibility for keeping takeover ability fresh sits on the design side. Three points for designers. Treat manual mode as a product, a formal function rather than a debugging entry point, tested for usability to the same standard as the automation. Treat state changes as events, presented conspicuously, verifiable, traceable, with the three checks run before the factory gate. Write the takeover plan into the deliverable, with every function carrying the manual takeover path for its failure and one page for it in the delivery checklist. All three costs land in the design phase, which is the cheapest moment. All three absences land at the takeover moment, which is the most expensive. The faster automation iterates, the more these three matter: the system changes every year, and the human takeover path cannot break every year.

The closing word goes back to the human side. The two words in this chapter's title, automation and skill decay, are two halves of one multiple-choice question. Automation is the compulsory part, since the gains in efficiency and safety are real and going back to manual is not an option. Skill decay is the elective part, since how far it decays, at which link, and what replaces it are all designable. An organization that answers the question well can summarise its relation with automation in one sentence: machines do what machines are good at, people do what people cannot be replaced at, and the handover between them has a set of actions both sides have practised. That set of actions already has a name in this chapter: takeover.

One footnote remains, for the time dimension of takeover. Takeover moments are unevenly distributed across a system's life, frequent early after launch, rare in the stable period, frequent again at replacement, and the practice roster should follow that distribution: drill exit and manual control at launch, recognition and alarms in the stable period, plan review and full-chain drills at replacement. The reading of the distribution comes from the change calendar, the schedule of upgrades, migrations and refactors, and binding the roster to the calendar makes training supply follow the rhythm of change automatically: the system changes, the practice changes with it, and takeover ability stays pointed at the current version of the system. This is the only mechanism among this chapter's defences that needs continuous maintenance, since the rest run for a long time once installed, and the maintenance is one action: after each change, look at the roster and change two lines.

Authority to change those two lines should sit with the shift leader, the person who knows the current version's hand feel best, and the record of the change goes into the attachment to the change ticket, under the same clause as the takeover plan review. Devolution is justified because the hand feel of a version is known only on the line. The tightening is justified because a devolved change must leave a trace, and an untraced change is new drift (Chapter 15). Between the loosening and the tightening, takeover training stays in step with the system's evolution. This is the least conspicuous and most durable of the chapter's devices, and its name can simply be: change two lines.

Among the two lines changed, one is best left unchanged forever: the first person responsible for takeover ability is always the person on shift, not the system, not the procedure, not the score of the last drill. What the system can do is supply the conditions that keep that person's hand feel fresh, which are quota, drills, documentation and budget. The suppliers of those conditions and their consumers are two different groups, management and the line, and every conversation between them is a translation of this chapter, with management reading audits and budgets and the line reading quotas and scripts. The quality of the translation decides the quality of implementation, and implementation is inspected in the five questions of Section 7. All five ask the line, because takeover finally happens in the hands on the line. However good the upstream design, unfamiliar hands leave everything as it was; with familiar hands, takeover arrives in time even under a rough procedure.

Redundancy of hands on duty, at least two pairs, is what guarantees that takeover arrives in time. The worst shape of a takeover moment is the only experienced person being absent, on leave, departed, or already inside another fault, and two experienced people on shift is the staffing floor for takeover, with the minimum headcount on the roster computed from the division of labour in the takeover chain rather than from normal operations. The line that writes that floor into the rostering rules is this chapter's last line: staffing in the automated era is set by the worst shape of the takeover moment, not by the ordinary shape of the day. The extra pairs kept on in quiet times look like idle posts and read as lives at the takeover moment. The defence against it sits in Chapter 19's armoury: the right of any seat to call upward without going through the chain, where calling a go-around needs no justification, and blameless recording afterwards. The training list for takeover ability therefore gains one soft subject: practice at speaking up.

### Case Two (Software): Takeover in a Retry Storm

**Case ID**: sw-004.

**When and where**: 12 September 2025, 16:32 to 19:12 UTC. The impact window ran from 17:57 to 19:12, 75 minutes in all, in three segments: a first segment of 20 minutes, a middle segment of 41 minutes and a last segment of 14 minutes. The location is Cloudflare's global control plane: the dashboard and a set of APIs. Tenant Service is a Go process running on Kubernetes in some of the data centres (postmortem, Timeline, How we responded).

**What and who**: Cloudflare's Tenant Service. It sits on the authorization path of API requests: every request's authorization is decided by it, and a request it cannot decide returns a 5xx (postmortem, What Happened). The parties involved were the dashboard frontend team, the API platform team, the on-call SRE, and the automated alerting service that pulled responders into a call. The affected surface was the dashboard and a set of APIs. Isolation between the control plane and the data plane is strict, and services on the edge network were unaffected (postmortem, How we responded).

**What they were doing**: at 16:32 a new version of the dashboard went live (postmortem, Timeline). This version contained logic that used a React useEffect to manage calls to the /organizations endpoint. useEffect decides whether to run again from its dependency array: when a value in the array changes, the function executes once more. In this version the dependency array held an object that was recreated on every state or prop change, so React treated it as new every time, reran the function on every render, issued several calls within one render, and retried when a call failed (postmortem, What Happened).

From 16:32 to 17:57 that version ran in production for 85 minutes and produced no visible failure on its own. At 17:50 a new Tenant API Service was deployed, and 7 minutes later the service was overwhelmed (postmortem, Timeline). The postmortem's wording for that stretch is that the Tenant API Service was overwhelmed during the deployment of the new version.

On release tooling, Cloudflare was already migrating services to Argo Rollouts, which watches deployment errors and rolls back automatically. Tenant Service had not yet been migrated, and the migration was scheduled (postmortem, Reducing impact). On capacity, the Tenant Service quota was set for normal conditions and not for a spike of this kind (same section). On monitoring, API usage was visible at the time, but whether a request was a retry or a new request could not be seen (postmortem, Improving visibility).

**The decision point**: 16:32, the dashboard version released, carried on a release ticket (postmortem, Timeline). The defect took the form above. The postmortem records neither the reviewers, the approvers nor the sign-off for that release, and does not record whether it went through a canary.

17:50, the Tenant API Service deployed, carried on a deployment ticket (postmortem, Timeline). The postmortem does not state what that version changed, only that the service was overwhelmed during the deployment. The two releases were 78 minutes apart and each went through its own process independently.

After the overload at 17:57, the first step in handling it was to reduce load and add capacity. The postmortem writes the basis for that step as a principle: the first goal in an incident is to restore service, and fixing the root cause is the common path but need not be the first step (postmortem, How we responded, original text "Our first goal in an incident is to restore service. Often that involves fixing the underlying issue directly, but not always."). At 18:17, after scaling, the API was back to 98% availability and the dashboard had not recovered (postmortem, Timeline).

At 18:58 they removed some erroring code paths and released a new Tenant Service (postmortem, Timeline). The basis was inference: the service reported healthy and the API had largely recovered, but large numbers of errors were still being logged, and the team inferred that those errors were the cause of the dashboard's continuing unavailability (postmortem, How we responded, original text "We theorized that these were responsible for the ongoing Dashboard availability issues"). That release affected the API a second time, and the timeline marks IMPACT INCREASE at that point.

At 19:01 a temporary rate limiting rule was published, and at 19:12 the problematic Tenant Service change was rolled back and the dashboard returned to 100% (postmortem, Timeline). One inconsistency in the account should be noted: the narrative places global rate limiting alongside scaling as load reduction in the same round, while the timeline records the rate limiting rule at 19:01. This card follows the timeline.

The design side holds an earlier decision: Tenant Service was not enrolled in automatic rollback. Its time carrier is the migration plan, and the postmortem writes only that the migration was scheduled, with no date (postmortem, Reducing impact). The external postmortem records no approver for any change, so that column has to stay empty.

**What was visible then**: at 17:57 the dashboard availability curve began to fall (postmortem, Timeline). The Tenant Service usage curve rose abnormally (postmortem, How we responded). The API's 5xx ratio, and the 98% reading at 18:17 (postmortem, Timeline). "Substantial" errors in the service logs (postmortem, How we responded). The pod count and throughput before scaling. The automated alerting brought the right people into the call early in the incident (same section).

One phenomenon that showed up during the handling: after Tenant Service was restarted, every user's dashboard re-authenticated to the API and the API went unstable again (postmortem, Reducing impact). One gap was visible in the same way: monitoring could not distinguish retries from new requests (postmortem, Improving visibility). The postmortem records no action between 17:57 and 18:17 other than scaling, so the only verifiable readings in those 20 minutes are the curves. In the 3 minutes after 18:58, the 19:01 rate limiting rule landed, and at that moment the curve was still heading down (postmortem, Timeline).

Information that only came out afterwards: the root cause lay in the dependency array of that useEffect in the dashboard (postmortem, What Happened); the amplification once repeated calls and retries on failure were compounded (the postmortem gives no figure); and that if Argo Rollouts had been in place, it would have rolled back the second Tenant Service update automatically, "limiting the second segment" (postmortem, Reducing impact).

**Stop point**

> **Stop here. Decide before you turn the page.**
>
> 1. What would you do? Write the action, not "pay more attention".
> 2. Which item here are you most likely to miss, and why does it look unimportant?
> 3. If you may set one checkpoint, when is it and what does it check?

**What happened**: the dashboard and the API were affected for 75 minutes, and the API was severely affected in two of the segments, corresponding to the two periods when Tenant Service was unavailable (postmortem, Timeline). Taken apart, 17:57 to 18:17 is 20 minutes, 18:17 to 18:58 is 41 minutes, and 18:58 to 19:12 is 14 minutes. The last segment was manufactured by the response itself: the only change in those 14 minutes was the 18:58 patch. At 18:17, when the API returned to 98%, the dashboard still showed zero recovery; after the 19:12 rollback the dashboard returned to 100%. The data plane was unaffected, and users who did not change configuration or use the dashboard were unaffected (postmortem, How we responded). The postmortem reports no data loss.

Going back to the three questions. The most common answer to the first is "scale first". That is what the case did, and the postmortem's assessment of it is that it helped availability but was not enough to restore normal service (postmortem, How we responded). The cost of the action was that the first 20 minutes of the response went into adding resources, with root cause work queued behind it. The item most often missed in the second question is that retries could not be distinguished from new requests. It reads like technical debt in a log field, and it is in fact the only entry point to the root cause: the postmortem says that had they known they were looking at a sustained flood of new requests, they would have identified the loop in the dashboard sooner (postmortem, Improving visibility). The right place to put the checkpoint, for the third question, is 18:17, and the thing to check is the divergence between the API curve and the dashboard curve. Authorization is decided by Tenant Service across the board, so the server side as a whole returning to 98% while the dashboard shows zero recovery means the source pressing on it is still there, and is not on the server side.

**Options ruled out**: locating the root cause before restoring service; the postmortem writes that fixing the root cause is the common path but need not be the first step (postmortem, How we responded). At 17:57 the root cause had not been located, so this path needs a conclusion that did not exist at the time. Rolling back the dashboard version from 16:32: that step is not in the timeline, and the 19:12 rollback was of the Tenant Service change (postmortem, Timeline). Relying on automatic rollback to catch it: Tenant Service was not enrolled in Argo Rollouts (postmortem, Reducing impact). Scaling without rate limiting: scaling was done, and the postmortem judges it insufficient (postmortem, How we responded). Treating a service restart as the recovery method: it was done, it triggered a thundering herd of re-authentication and pressed the API again (postmortem, Reducing impact). Locating the fault from logs: with retries indistinguishable from new requests, the logs cannot show the shape of the loop; the postmortem says knowing they were seeing a sustained flood of new requests would have made the dashboard loop easier to identify, and that sentence is exactly the conclusion the logs of the time could not supply (postmortem, Improving visibility).

**Early signals**: the 16:32 version would "trigger far more calls", a sentence written in the timeline (postmortem, Timeline). It was overridden by "new version traffic ramping up", and the 85 minutes that followed with no consequence let the signal suppress itself. The two changes were 78 minutes apart, at 16:32 and 17:50, which was overridden by "each reviewed independently". The deployment at 17:50 to the overload at 17:57 is an interval of 7 minutes (postmortem, Timeline), overridden by "jitter during a deployment". Automatic rollback was not yet migrated and the migration was scheduled, overridden by "the migration is in progress". Retries were written into the release content, and the backoff and jitter that belong with retries were absent (postmortem, Timeline, original text "including retries in the event of failure"). Capacity was not configured for a spike and there was no capacity alert (the postmortem writes that monitoring was being improved so as to "proactively alert before reaching capacity limits", which means none existed then), overridden by "it has never happened". The divergence at 18:17, with the API at 98% and the dashboard at zero recovery, was overridden by the good news that the API had recovered.

**The point of escalation**: 18:58. Removing the erroring code paths and releasing a new version rested on inference, not verification (postmortem, How we responded). In the 61 minutes before it, the curve had one direction, backwards, and after it the API fell a second time (postmortem, Timeline, IMPACT INCREASE). The action acted on errors, while the input to the fault was call volume: removing erroring code paths did not reduce call volume at all. The step turned a single rollbackable point into two impacts, and the remedy for the second impact was the same thing: rollback. Only after the 19:12 rollback did the dashboard return to 100% (postmortem, Timeline). From 18:58 to 19:12 is 14 minutes, and those 14 minutes produced no new information, only new failure surface. The postmortem's characterization of that update is "a bad change" (postmortem, Timeline, original text "This was ultimately a bad change"), and it shares a timestamp with IMPACT INCREASE. The approver is not recorded.

**Failure chain**: Technical layer: the dependency array contained an object rebuilt on every render, producing multiple calls to /organizations within one render; a failed call was retried, with no backoff and no jitter; Tenant Service was overloaded and authorization could not complete, so the API returned 5xx across the board; the 5xx responses triggered more retries, a positive feedback loop; and the restart made every user re-authenticate, producing a second impact (postmortem, What Happened, Reducing impact).

Organizational layer: two changes, frontend and backend, went live separately with no joint review; a service sitting on every API call path was configured at the capacity of an ordinary service; it was not enrolled in automatic rollback; monitoring could not distinguish retries from new requests; and the patch went out before the root cause was verified (postmortem, How we responded, Improving visibility).

Incentive layer: the benefit of release frequency is current and the risk of coupling is deferred; investment in capacity and automatic rollback had no bill before the incident; and "the service reported healthy" was treated as evidence that the job could be closed (postmortem, How we responded).

The junction breaks between the organizational layer and the incentive layer. Tenant Service's position on the request path means its capacity, release tooling and monitoring should have been rated as a core component. It was rated as an ordinary internal service, and that rating cell has no owner.

**Minimum counterfactual**: before 17:50, enrol Tenant Service in Argo Rollouts automatic rollback. The postmortem's own words: if that mechanism had been in place, it would have rolled back the second Tenant Service update automatically, "limiting the second segment" (postmortem, Reducing impact). One action, with no luck involved: the tool was already in use, only one service was missing from it, and the migration was already scheduled at the time. It shortens the incident rather than eliminating it: the first segment still happens and the second does not, the 14 minutes of the last segment come off the bill, and the responders do not have to change production again at the end of their most tired hour. The cost is migration hours, and the bill before the incident was zero. The same fault with a dependency-array fix as the counterfactual covers a wider scope, but it demands distinguishing retries from new requests within 75 minutes, and monitoring could not make that distinction.

**Outcome and sources**: 75 minutes, two segments of API impact, the dashboard severely affected throughout, the data plane unaffected (postmortem, Timeline, How we responded). The postmortem divides the improvements into two kinds: reducing the impact of similar changes, and improving observability. It also records two things that limited the impact: automated alerting found the right people quickly, and control plane and data plane isolation kept edge services unaffected (postmortem, How we responded). Five remediation items: migrate Tenant Service into Argo Rollouts and raise its priority; add randomized delay to dashboard retries so that retries spread out (the hotfix went out after the impact ended); scale up and add capacity alerting; tag requests as retry or new; deduplicate dashboard calls (postmortem, Reducing impact, Improving visibility). Primary source: Cloudflare Blog, "A deep dive into Cloudflare's September 12, 2025 dashboard and API outage", published 13 September 2025, author Tom Lianza, link at Appendix B, sw-004. That postmortem is an external account and attaches no change tickets, approvers or request volume figures; this card writes only the verifiable timestamps and the sections in which they appear.

One organizational observation about the combination of actions in Case Two belongs here. The decision chain in the response, the order of rate limiting, scaling and rollback, is assembled on the spot in a real incident by the individual on call. That assembly depends on experience, meaning what was done last time, and the full set of combinations, meaning which orders work, has never been institutionalised as a script. Scripting costs the work of sorting the order and effect of past responses into a one-page decision tree, and it buys a takeover moment that starts from a lookup rather than a recollection, the same move as the triple index in Chapter 21. Script plus game day is the complete training loop for takeover ability in the software industry: the script supplies the lookup, the game day supplies the muscle, and the outputs of the two calibrate each other.

### Note on the Evidence Gap

Verification cases for criterion 1 outside aviation, meaning marine autopilots, medical device default parameters, and operator takeover in industrial control, are missing from this book's collection scope in full grade A form. The cause of the gap is the mechanism itself: events of this kind have not yet entered the investigation systems of domains where automation penetration is shallow in large numbers. Directions for further collection are listed in, which covers regulatory reports and accident libraries on automation by domain. The gap is marked as it stands and does not affect the generality of the criteria or the defences, which come directly from the long literature on human factors in automation and from the cases above.

Beside the gap, the chapter offers extrapolations in three domains, marked explicitly: what follows is mechanism extrapolation, not case cards, and will be replaced by real cards once collection catches up. Extrapolation one, marine autopilot: after the autopilot takes over routine steering, the helmsman's manual steering practice decays by the month, while narrow channels and collision avoidance demand immediate hand feel; the check form for criteria 1 and 3 here is a quarterly quota of manual helm and simulated takeover drills. Extrapolation two, medical device default parameters: after infusion pumps and monitors handle routine configuration through their default parameters, nurses' familiarity with what the parameters mean and how to adjust them decays, and the manual parameter change in an anomaly happens at the busiest moment of the shift; the check form for criterion 3 is a drill on changing parameters outside the defaults, written into training assessment. Extrapolation three, industrial control: the automation coverage of DCS operators rises year by year, and the execution frequency of high-load manual sequences such as plant start-up drops to once in several years under long-cycle operation; the check form for criterion 1 is simulator refresher training on the start-up sequence and archived operating video of veteran operators. The three extrapolations share one chain of criteria, and turning them into real cards is an open item on this book's collection list.

Beyond those extrapolations, one kind of automation now in progress deserves early monitoring: AI-assisted judgement automation, in aided diagnosis, aided writing and aided coding. It differs from classical execution automation in one key respect: what it takes over is judgement rather than hands, so the decay happens in the texture of judgement, in the feel for the right answer and the smell of a wrong one. Takeover ability under this kind of automation is harder to measure than under execution automation, since the question is how to set a quota for judgement, and a workable start is to keep a control sample for manual review, taking a number of cases each month and running them entirely by hand beside the AI output, where the difference between the two is the stock reading for judgement skill. Measurement methods will be forced into existence by the spread of AI automation, and the framework of this chapter's criteria, a measurable stock and a loop that can be broken, is directly usable as their first draft.

## 4 What Shows Up Earliest

Six weak signals, each with the reason it usually gets overridden.

1. Simulator and hands-on training hours fall year after year, and the stated reason tracks automation coverage. The reason: "training follows the job."
2. "The system will handle it" appears where a training document leaves something out. The reason: "focus on the increment."
3. A minimum requirement for manual execution, such as a pilot's manual landing quota, exists but carries no assessment record. The reason: "we have a procedure."
4. The handling of a takeover scenario is rated in a postmortem as correct but slow. The reason: "the outcome was good." The slowness is itself the decay reading.
5. New employees find the system's manual mode unfamiliar, or do not know it exists. The reason: "they will not need it."
6. After an automation change, a version upgrade or a logic adjustment, the takeover plan is not updated with it. The reason: "functionally equivalent."

Put the six back against this chapter's two cases and three extrapolations. Signal 1, falling training hours, matches Case One: what the training side of Asiana 214 shows is an industry-wide footnote to this signal. Signal 2, omissions in training material, matches extrapolation two: training in the era of default parameters is left with nothing but introductions to new features, and the manual path for a parameter has disappeared from the teaching material. Signal 3, a minimum requirement with no assessment record, matches Case One: organizations that hold a manual landing quota and keep no record of assessing it are not few, and they are more common outside aviation. Signal 4, correct but slow, matches Case Two: slowness is the measurable reading of decay, and a postmortem should record the time each step of the response took and align it against history. Signal 5, unfamiliarity with manual mode, matches extrapolation three: on plants running long cycles, the DCS manual mode is close to being lost. Signal 6, a plan not updated after a change, matches all of them: it is the high-frequency companion symptom of automation, and every upgrade should trigger a review of the takeover plan.

Order the six by the cost of finding them. Signals 2 and 3 are cheapest, since they mean reading training material and quota records. Signals 4 and 6 are middling, since they mean reading postmortems and change tickets. Signals 1 and 5 are most expensive, since they need training ledgers and interviews on the floor. The recommended start is signal 3: pull out the list of minimum manual requirements, look for an assessment record against each one, and treat a requirement with no record as a requirement that does not exist, which is criterion 3 carried out in plain words.

## 5 How to Defend

Device 1: a manual quota. Set a minimum frequency quota for manual operation of key systems, with aviation's manual flying quota as the mature model, and make quota completion an assessable metric: whether a person can still do the thing is a quantity to be measured, not an assumption. The quota's scenarios have to cover the whole takeover chain, recognising the anomaly, exiting automation, executing by hand and restoring automation, and all four are required.

Device 2: simulation of takeover scenarios. Rewrite takeover scenarios from postmortems into simulated exercises, with the software industry's game day and chaos engineering as the same class of thing, and cover the takeover list once a year. The exercises use the real material of a postmortem, at a cost far below the real accident, and the output of an exercise, where it jammed and who did not know which button, goes into the ledger and becomes input to the next round of training.

Device 3: thresholds and cues for takeover. An automated system should give a clear entry signal for the state in which a human is needed, through a mode change, a fall in confidence or a combination of concurrent alarms, and the takeover plan is updated in step with the signal. After every change to the system, the takeover plan is reviewed under a mandatory clause, which is the cure for weak signal 6. The lesson of Asiana 214 takes this shape here: a state change has to be presented in a form human attention can catch, rather than as a line of annunciator characters that can be reasonably read past.

Device 4: write automation knowledge back into people. Every automation function carries a one-pager on what it does, what it does not do, and what you do when it fails, and training and refresher training follow that page. A person's understanding of the system is the base of takeover ability. Without an understanding of where automation stops, takeover starts after the correct moment. With it, takeover can start early, at the system's limit.

Set the four devices against this chapter's cases and extrapolations. Device 1 on Case One: quotas for visual approaches and manual flying assessed separately for captains and first officers, with the quota starting at the first officer's first line release. Device 2 on Case Two: the combined handling of the retry storm rewritten as a game day script, with the decision tree for rate limiting, scaling and rollback entered in the register. Device 3 on extrapolation two: mode changes on the infusion pump presented with a conspicuous cue, and the training on the parameter change path sourced from the same place as the cue. Device 4 on extrapolation three: a one-pager for the start-up sequence, covering what it does, what it does not do and what you do on failure, stored with the operating procedures and entering refresher training.

Now the failure modes of the four devices. The quota dies on watered quota, with manual operations completed in unstressed periods, the easiest landings and the calmest sea states, so the quota is met and the difficulty of the takeover scenario has never been practised. The patch is to grade the quota by scenario, with difficulty tiers and a minimum share for the high tier. Simulation dies on the gap between script and reality, with game day faults that are too clean and too single-threaded. The patch is to rewrite scripts from real postmortems into compound faults, with concurrent alarms and incomplete information. Thresholds and cues die on alert fatigue, since a conspicuous cue that appears every day becomes background, the cry wolf effect. The patch is to grade the cues, keeping the ones that genuinely need takeover rare and denoising the routine ones. Writing knowledge back dies on a one-pager that is never updated, so that after an upgrade the page still describes the previous version. The patch is to bind the one-pager to the change ticket, with the ticket forcibly triggering a review of the page under the same clause as the takeover plan review. The four patches share one check sentence: in the most recent takeover practice, were the steps that jammed fed back into training?

An order among the devices is also worth fixing: Device 4 first, which is understanding the boundary, then Device 1, which is practising the action. The reason sits at the cognitive layer. Manual practice without an understanding of the boundary is blind practice, and a person who does not know when to take over practises perfect actions at the wrong moment. Once the boundary is understood, quota and simulation have the correct target, which is starting the takeover at the correct moment. Organizations whose training budget is growing should hold this order most strictly: buy the one-pager first, since it is cheap, and simulator hours after, since they are expensive. The inverted order spends half the budget on blind practice.

## 6 Boundaries with Neighbouring Mechanisms

Against practical drift (Chapter 15): drift is practice departing from the text, an active divergence, while decay is capability departing from the requirement, a passive fading. In an automated setting the two cause each other, since the text updates more slowly than the system, which is drift, and people leave practice as the system moves on, which is decay. Once decay has set in, the distance between practice on the floor and the text is wider than any deliberate deviation would produce.

Against the illusion of redundancy (Chapter 22): automation is often counted as redundancy in people, on the reasoning that the system will handle it and people need not learn it, and the moment a person is crossed off the defence list this mechanism has every condition it needs. Whether people count as redundancy is a question for Chapter 22's verification standard: has a real switchover ever been made, and has takeover ever been rehearsed for real?

Against where intuitive expertise breaks down (Chapter 14): decay is the time version of the failure zone. Once feedback is cut off by automation, the preconditions of intuition, which are regularity and feedback, are systematically dismantled. Chapter 14's admission table should trigger on every row by default in an automated environment.

## 7 Five Self-Checks

1. When was the last composite fault in your system that a human took over? When was that person's previous practice?
2. Is your organization's training hours curve rising or falling? Which reason is written next to the fall?
3. Does a takeover scenario list exist? When did you last practise using real postmortem material?
4. In your automation documentation, is "what it does not do" written down? Pick a function at random and ask one of its users.
5. If every automation stopped tomorrow morning, who in your team could start the plant? How many names are on the list, and what is their average age?
