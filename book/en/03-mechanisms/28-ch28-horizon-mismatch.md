# Chapter 28 Horizon Mismatch; Short-Termism

> What this chapter covers: how a structure with the benefit in the current period and the cost years later mass-produces failure; the criteria, two field sites, the handover of the bill, and making the forward liability visible.
> When to turn back: when setting a term of office and its assessment cycle, when making a commitment that spans years, or when assessing work whose payoff arrives in the next term.
> Estimated reading time: 50 minutes.

Horizon mismatch needs no ill will from anyone. Put an ordinary person into a term shorter than the consequence cycle, give him a ledger that records only the current period, and let the handover carry no unfulfilled obligations. His choices will lean toward the present systematically, and each lean is rational and defensible at the time. The disease is structural: term, ledger and handover together guarantee that the cross-period cost arrives after the decision-maker's period of responsibility has closed. The evidentiary difficulty is real. First-hand deep cases here are the hardest to collect, since decision chains are scattered across audit and budget documents from several administrations, so the chapter is built from two skeletal field sites and an honest account of the gap.

## 1 What It Is

Definition: horizon mismatch describes a decision whose benefits and costs fall on different time scales, while the decision-maker's term of office, assessment cycle or accounting basis covers only the benefit side.

The criteria (all three present, and the structure stands):

① The benefit lands in the current period, the cost three years out or later;
② The person making the decision serves a term shorter than the cost cycle;
③ Nobody is accountable for the cross-period result: no one signs for receipt, and no handover ledger exists (the interface with Chapter 30).

One intuition about units comes before the criteria. Terms are counted in years, infrastructure in decades, ecology and population in generations. The failed conversion between the three is the meta-structure of horizon mismatch: the literature on term politics works the same ground (electoral cycles against governance cycles), and corporate governance runs a gentler version (average executive tenure well short of the payback period on strategic investment). Nobody is at fault for the failed conversion. It is the original parameter of institutional design, and the preventive work of this chapter is a patch on that parameter: extend the period of responsibility past the term, and pull the cost from outside the ledger into it.

Feasibility has cross-institutional evidence behind it. Pension and sovereign fund governance, with fiduciary duty reaching decades ahead. Generational balance clauses in environmental law, some constitutions writing a duty toward future generations into the text. The trust structure of perpetual funds, where trustees change and the trust does not. Together they prove that a period of responsibility longer than the term can be built, and the common parts are three: a fiduciary structure (duty written to the post, not the person), statutory funding (money locked into a dedicated account when the commitment is made), and a public ledger (progress against each commitment published annually). Those three parts are the public-administration prototype of this chapter's Devices 1 to 3, and a reader designing cross-period accountability can copy them directly.

## 2 How It Grows

Three generating conditions. Condition one: the assessment cycle is set by the term. Political terms, executive terms, the subscription and redemption cycle of a fund manager, all shorter than the cost cycles of infrastructure, education, ecology and maintenance. The gap is not an anomaly; it is the normal state of these fields. Condition two: accounting and statistical bases record the current period only. Forward liabilities sit outside the current statements (technical debt, deferred maintenance, demographic structure), and a liability nobody can see does not constrain this period's decision. The matching principle has little purchase in front of a long-cycle asset. Condition three: handover is where the bill is dropped. The outgoing holder does not hand over a schedule of bills, and the incoming one does not know what debt he has received (Chapter 30's rupture, drawn along a time axis). Every handover resets unfulfilled obligations to zero.

The accounting of that reset deserves one more layer. Forward liabilities stay off the statements for two reasons, basis (Chapter 2's condition) and measurement: the amount is hard to estimate, since deferred maintenance depends on future material and labour prices, and technical debt on the future shape of the business. Measurement is genuinely hard, and its corollary gets misused: from cannot estimate well, to do not record. Accounting has a mature answer to poor estimation, interval estimates with sensitivity disclosure, and the answer was never transplanted onto forward liabilities because nobody was required to transplant it. Device 2 asks for that transplant. Figures reported as intervals, sensitivity disclosed every year, error corrected against earlier disclosures: from cannot estimate to corrected annually, a forward liability turns from an impossible task into an ordinary estimation problem.

The organizational side of the reset has a finer mechanism, the direction of handover. An exit handover is usually led by the outgoing holder (what to hand over, how, in what depth), and the incoming one starts from a weaker information position. Unfulfilled obligations are exactly what the outgoing holder has least incentive to write clearly, since writing them down is a public admission of shortfall. The answer is to transfer ownership of the document to the incoming holder: he demands documents against a list, and the outgoing holder completes against it. Device 1's receipt clause is the documentary form of that transfer. One execution detail follows. The template has to exist in advance, because a list improvised on the day of receipt will be missing items, and its maintainer is the post itself rather than whoever holds it in a given term.

**Failure chain**:

Technical layer: the engineering itself did not fail. The break sits between the engineering breakdown and the commitment. In the three-phase plan, phase 2's parliamentary authorisation will not complete until around 2030, and the breakdown assumes the preceding section's scope will not move (NAO p.31). The interface is specific down to a platform: in November 2021 DfT widened the scope and funding of the Transpennine upgrade to include elements this programme would later use, among them a possible extra platform at Huddersfield and a freight passing loop (NAO p.34).

Organizational layer: ministers decide the scope; DfT and two arm's-length bodies deliver; the benefits have to be realised by the housing and communities department, local government and the combined authorities (NAO p.17). As of January 2026 the formal structure that would bring these parties together did not exist (NAO p.29), the delivery committee does not decide (NAO p.29), the sponsor board's effectiveness was challenged in writing in September 2024 (NAO p.27), and the team managing the interfaces explicitly holds no decision rights (NAO p.34).

Incentive layer: each term's books run on financial years, and the spending table is laid out by year (NAO p.25); the commitment runs on the whole programme, and the price base switched from 2019 to 2025 (NAO p.22); the £45 billion ceiling counts from 2026-27 and leaves the £410 million of earlier design spend outside it (NAO p.25). Three sets of units that do not convert: annual spend cannot show a cross-term commitment, a change of price base makes the years incomparable, and a moved start date puts earlier money outside the allowance.

The junction breaks between the organizational and incentive layers. The cost of a change has no one to sign for it, and the organizational layer lacks exactly the structure that could record a signature.

**Minimum counterfactual**: move the start of the £45 billion ceiling from 2026-27 back to 2015-16, and count both the money already spent and the investment written off by each scope change against that ceiling. One accounting action, inside the same January 2026 decision, requiring no luck: the year-by-year spend data sits in DfT's finance system, and the table on page 25 is its product. With the change, a change of the October 2023 kind would eat into the ceiling in the current period, and scope change would carry a price for the first time. The same ceiling would move written-off design fees from a historical footnote onto a line of the decision table. It does not settle who signs; it settles how much one change costs, a question nobody had answered. The ceiling is an instrument that already exists, and moving its start date needs no legislation, only a different date in the same decision.

Every scope change in Case 1 can also be filed term by term against this chapter's criteria: which term made it, the reason given, the previous term's investment written off, the new position of the delivery date. Four columns joined together make a chronicle of horizon mismatch, and the value of the chronicle is its symmetry. Each term's change had an adequate reason inside its own period (strategic adjustment, fiscal constraint, reordered priorities), and read in sequence they show one machine turning: every term changes the scope, and every change pushes the date back. The fuel is at criterion ③, since the cost of a change has no signatory. The previous term's investment is written off with nobody answerable for it on the books, the delivery date moves with nobody signing for the new position. Adding a column for the signatory, with "none" entered, is an action an audit can take immediately. On the day that column comes back empty in run after run, the mechanism is written in ink.

Evidence in ink has a multinational reference to borrow: the UK's national infrastructure commitments mechanism, where commitments are published as a register on a five-year cycle with a delivery status against each one, and the statutory funding of long-term projects in several countries, where the budget source is locked when the commitment is made. Both turn delivery from political will into a registration obligation. The register makes each term's commitments comparable and lets an unfulfilled commitment be named. Naming is not punishment, which needs procedure; its function is to keep a slipped delivery date in public view. A date may slip, but it may not slip out of sight.

A lockout is a state the service procedure can handle on site; reporting it as a fault requires a different protocol. Why it was passed over: a lockout is the most common boiler service call, and a reset is standard practice.

② The tray cracks only after delayed ignition, and a single event leaves a crack in a plastic part visible only with the unit opened up (CPSC announcement). Why it was passed over: handled as a component replacement, inside the warranty terms.

③ Three front cover detachments. That is no longer a component-level fault, since the cover flies off and injury outside the boiler room is plausible. Why it was passed over: 3 events across an installed base of several thousand is about 0.05% (the book's calculation from the announcement figures), and the ratio reads as a one-off.

④ The same manufacturer's safety notice channel had been used three times before, with the retrospective boundary always written as a manufacturing date: the TFT seal in July 2018, the TRX burner door studs in March 2025, the FTG upgrade kit in February 2026 (manufacturer's recall page). The channel works. What is missing is not a channel but a metric that counts resets.

**The point of escalation**: 29 October 2026, the moment "new controller plus revised software" was chosen as the remedy. Before that, the risk sat in the strength of the tray across 6,329 units. After it, the risk sits in one execution condition, that every unit has been visited once. The announcement can guarantee only two rounds of channel notice and the wholesalers' stock quarantine (Ariston recall notice); reaching the end user still runs through a contractor, and all four roles act on their own initiative. After the recall the units do not move, so the risk scatters across thousands of homes at their installation addresses (CPSC announcement). The announcement also leaves a flexibility clause: some units will keep running until they are repaired (Ariston recall notice). The clause exists to make the recall executable, and its cost is that the risk deadline moves from the recall date to the day the last unit is visited, a day in no document. This step has a date, a text and a signatory, and no way back.

**Failure chain**:

Technical layer: on delayed ignition the unburned gas accumulates in the sealed chamber, the pressure releases through the front cover when it ignites, and the tray cracks first along that stress path. The remedy changes the ignition control logic and not the tray's strength: between the number of delayed ignitions the controller allows and the number the tray can take, there is no calibrated figure (CPSC announcement, Ariston recall notice). The judgement is by eye, since a technician's visual inspection decides whether the tray is damaged and the announcement sets no quantified criterion (Ariston recall notice). Confirmation after the repair is also a one-off: a combustion test with an analyser and a label, with no further sampling (Ariston recall notice).

Organizational layer: manufacture in Canada, import into the United States, service through an independent contractor network, and a defect information path that runs contractor to wholesaler to manufacturer to regulator. To reach the end user the recall has two rounds of channel notice and nothing else; the requirement on installers is at least two rounds, two weeks apart (Ariston recall notice).

Incentive layer: the current period is measured in units and unit price, at $3,200 to $7,700 a unit; the cost in ignition cycles and years installed. The warranty counts years, fatigue counts cycles, and the two rulers have different sources. The recall's accounts count units and service visits, and they open only after the recall date (CPSC announcement).

The junction breaks between the technical and incentive layers. Because the reset count was never recorded, per-unit accounting looks entirely sound, and the 34 reports are the shape that blank accumulated into.

**Minimum counterfactual**: before the first production run, make "lockout reset count" a factory-recorded field in the controller, shipped with the unit. One action, taken before June 2021, requiring no luck: the controller already handles the delayed ignition and lockout decision, and the 2026 remedy changes exactly that software (Ariston recall notice). With the record in place, a cumulative reset count reaching a threshold would bring the tray into view without waiting for 34 reports. The same record makes "which batch fails first" into comparable data, and nobody can make that comparison today. The cost is one data field and one software addition; the return is turning 34 reports from a count into a rate. It does not change the strength of the tray, only the date on which it is found. This card does not claim the action was considered at the time.

Where the two disagree (4,726 plus 2,261 against about 6,329) the figures are recorded side by side without conversion, and no media retelling was used.

The material for Case 2 stops at the announcement, and the protocol and duration of accelerated ageing tests are not in the public record; this passage covers only the general method. An accelerated test compresses time by amplifying stress (high temperature, high humidity, high cycling), and the conversion depends on an ageing model that extrapolates the relation between stress and life. The model's assumptions are the gap between test and reality. The management answer to that gap is settled practice in reliability engineering: keep a real-time tracking sample alongside the accelerated test, a batch of early product held for long-term observation, with measured life backfilling the model's parameters. The cost of the sample is storage and follow-up; the return is a conversion factor for the next generation that stands on measurement. From Case 2's angle, the 34 rupture reports were themselves a late backfill, paid for with a user's stove and a technician's jaw.

Backfilling has a public version in consumer products, in the relation between the CPSC's incident report database (the public report stream at saferproducts.gov) and the trigger for a recall. The stream aggregates use across millions of households into a statistical signal (the six hundred power bank reports in Chapter 16's fourth case were its product), and the timeliness of the signal sets the lag before a recall: a defect with few, scattered reports, like the 34 here, waits longer. Consumer products therefore have a public route out of horizon mismatch. The aggregation speed of defect reports is the error-discovery speed of the whole product line, and the firm-side counterpart is to monitor the stream and aggregate by its own model numbers (Chapter 20's two-way flow of the industry library, on its third appearance here).

### The Evidence Gap

First-hand deep cases in horizon mismatch run into two difficulties. In political and infrastructure cases the decision chain is scattered through audit and budget documents from several administrations, and the cost of collection and reassembly is high. In corporate cases the forward cost (technical debt, deferred maintenance) is almost never written up as a postmortem, because writing it up hands the successor a bill, which is exactly the self-concealment this mechanism relies on. The A-grade deep cases in this book's library are currently mostly public audits, and the directions for further collection are renewal reviews by legislatures and research on long-tail losses in insurance.

Beside the gap, four extrapolations, labelled mechanism extrapolation rather than case card. Extrapolation one, software technical debt: every expedient implementation borrows time from the future, since the benefit of shipping lands at once and the maintenance cost accrues like quarterly interest; the interest is paid by everyone who takes the code over, the principal by a rewrite years later. The check under criterion ③ is a technical debt ledger (what is owed, at what interest, due when) with a receipt at handover. Extrapolation two, infrastructure maintenance: roads and pipe networks are designed in decades and maintained on annual budgets, and deferred maintenance shows up year by year in the surface and in burst mains (Chapter 29's home ground), while on the books it stays a discretionary line. The check is a deferred maintenance ledger and statutory funding. Extrapolation three, pensions and population: the generational mismatch between contributors and recipients is the century-scale version, with this period's contributions supporting this period's pensions and the generational cost paid by a population structure decades away; the check is actuarial disclosure, together with the political difficulty of a contract between generations. Extrapolation four, the asset management industry: the quarterly cycle of subscriptions and redemptions is mismatched against the multi-year cycle of value investing, and managers are pushed into trading to the rhythm of flows, setting the return on current assets under management against the long-term return. The check is a longer lock-up and a longer performance reconciliation period. The four cover four time scales, from code to civilization, and share one chain of criteria; converting them into real cards is also on the collection list.

The common solution across the extrapolations has a cross-scale abstraction worth stating. The terminal form of governing horizon mismatch is to turn the future cost into today's cash flow (statutory funding, depreciation and amortization, provisions), so that the future line appears in this period's cost table. Cash flow is the only language both markets and politics hear, accounting and budgeting being the common inputs of both systems, and visibility (Device 2) is the forward post of that conversion: first the number, then the money, then the liability. The order cannot be scrambled. Visibility without a number is a slogan, visibility without money is a ledger, visibility with money but no liability is a bad debt.

## 4 Earliest Signals

Six weak signals, each with the reason it usually gets passed over.

1. The budget table has no line for future cost, or the line exists in name only. Reason given: "the distant future cannot be estimated."
2. No table compares the decision-maker's term against the consequence cycle. Reason given: "who would make such a table."
3. Key milestones cluster before the term boundary, and every benefit milestone sits after it. Reason given: "that is how the plan is staged."
4. A key post changes hands in mid-project, and the handover contains no unfulfilled obligations. Reason given: "the handover was done to procedure."
5. "The next five-year plan will deal with it" becomes the closing sentence of this plan. Reason given: "we are phasing delivery."
6. Maintenance and capability-building spend is classed as discretionary and finishes last in the queue year after year. Reason given: "protect the priorities first." (Chapter 29 picks up here.)

Put the six back against this chapter's cases and extrapolations. Signal one, the missing future cost line, matches Case 1: across the many scope changes on the rail programme, written-off investment was recorded in history and never on the current decision table. Signal two, the absent term comparison, matches everything: a table setting term against consequence cycle is the one table this chapter should standardise, and the absence of that table is criterion ② admitting itself. Signal three, asymmetric milestones, matches Case 2: the launch milestone sits inside the assessment period, the defect exposure milestone outside the warranty. Signal four, a handover without obligations, matches Case 1: the handover between administrations carried no statement of what was being taken on from the previous term's commitments. Signal five, the next five-year plan, matches extrapolation one: every entry in the technical debt ledger is annotated "to be repaid at the next refactor". Signal six, maintenance last, matches extrapolation two: the discretion in a discretionary line is exactly the exit horizon mismatch reserves for itself.

Reorder the six by cost of evidence. Signals one and six are cheapest, needing only the budget table. Signals three and four are middling, needing the plan and the handover documents. Signals two and five are most expensive, needing comparison across terms and textual analysis. The starting recommendation is signal one: open the budget table and look for a column on future cost. Its absence can be confirmed without any permission, and the act of confirming it is the first image of this mechanism inside your own organization.

One snapshot mechanism on the time axis deserves a rule of its own: an interim bill at the midpoint of the term, when the forward liabilities of the current term are made visible and published once. It gives the term a reconciliation point, so the hidden cost of the current period is exposed once in the middle rather than saved as a surprise for the successor, and its institutional prototype is the mid-year budget review in the public sector. The interim bill and Device 1's handover list are the same list at two moments: half at the midpoint, all at exit. The difference between the two, the liability added in the second half of the term, is the trace left by the final-year sprint, and the trace is itself a soft constraint on sprinting.

## 5 Prevention

Device 1: a bill handover system. When a key post changes hands, the handover is required to contain a list of unfulfilled obligations: what was promised, what is owed, when the cost falls due, where the money is. The list is signed for by the incoming holder, and gaps are completed by the outgoing one, which puts Chapter 30's handover onto a time axis. The force of the list depends on how seriously the signature is taken: signing means the incoming holder confirms the list is true, and a false list can be pursued.

Device 2: make the forward liability visible. The forward cost of technical debt, deferred maintenance, and ageing population and assets is converted each year into a note to the current statements and published. The figure can be disputed; its absence cannot be. Visibility does not buy precision. It buys the change from nothing to something, which gives every postponement a line that can be cited.

Device 3: a cross-period signatory. Create a post accountable for the result five years out (a long-term value officer, a steward of legacy assets), with every assessment metric defined outside the term and the protection of the post written into the charter. The post answers criterion ② directly: the term is shorter than the consequence cycle, and the period of responsibility can still be longer than the term.

Device 4: a milestone symmetry review. At plan approval, check the distribution of milestones: where the gap between a decision milestone and a benefit milestone exceeds the term, require an account of the mechanism that will carry delivery across the gap (a dedicated body, statutory funding, a treaty commitment), and where no account can be given, shorten the commitment.

The four devices against this chapter's cases and extrapolations. Device 1 on Case 1: make the handover between administrations carry a schedule of commitments assumed, with every commitment of the previous term marked carried over, revised or dropped, and a signed reason for each drop. Device 2 on extrapolation two: convert deferred maintenance into an interval figure in the notes to the annual budget, and publish the curve of the backlog year by year. Device 3 on extrapolation three: place the signatory for actuarial disclosure outside the current budget department, with protection of the post in the institution's charter. Device 4 on Case 2: bring the exposure period into the decision table at the product freeze, aligning the launch schedule with the exposure cycle and calibrating the warranty terms to it.

The failure modes of the four devices. The bill handover dies for want of competition over the completeness of the list, since the outgoing holder submits a template of empty lines and the incoming one signs it. The patch is a two-source list: the incoming holder builds his own from his own enquiry, the two are read together, and the differences are the points of suspicion. Visibility of the forward liability dies of drift in the basis, with the method changed every year and the history made incomparable. The patch is a frozen basis with a change register (the same as Device 4 in Chapter 12). The cross-period signatory dies of the ornamental post, where the holder attends ceremonies and never vetoes. The patch is a statutory veto list, naming which decisions require that signature, written into the charter. The milestone symmetry review dies of rhetorical justification, where the account of the cross-period mechanism fills up with vision language. The patch is hard fields: the establishment number of the dedicated body, the account code of the statutory fund, the clause number of the treaty text. If none of the three can be filled in, shorten the commitment. All four patches share one check-up question: at the most recent cross-period commitment, who was in the room on the due date.

The order of installation matters too: Device 2 first (visibility, since the number is the foundation of everything after it), then Device 1 (handover, hanging the list on an existing process), then Device 4 (milestone review, applied to new plans at once), and Device 3 last (the cross-period post, the largest piece of institutional engineering). The order runs opposite to Chapter 27, which starts with the zero-based question, and the reason lies in where each mechanism is cut open: escalation's lesion is in the argument for a decision, mismatch's in the measurement of one. Change the question first in one case, change the ledger first in this one. Neither operation should wait, since the cost of mismatch grows every quarter, so the real answer is to schedule both first steps in the next quarter: one quarter to install the zero-based question and the forward liability note, at almost no cost. The review question is narrower than feasibility: who will be in the room on the due date.

## 6 Boundaries with Adjacent Mechanisms

With the invisibility of maintenance (Chapter 29): horizon mismatch explains why maintenance gets cut, since the cost of the cut lands outside the term, and the invisibility of maintenance explains why cutting it does not hurt, since the cut cannot be seen. The two chapters use different faces of the same cases: the delay at the Palace of Westminster (po-005) belongs to Chapter 29, and the multi-term structure behind the delay belongs here.

With sunk cost and escalation (Chapter 27): escalation pushes the cost further onto the successor, since the reset project accumulates a fresh set of bills, and horizon mismatch guarantees that the successor can clean up without ever holding the predecessor to account. The criterion: if the decision-maker has to be present in some form to bear the consequence, the structure corrects itself. What fails to correct is this chapter's territory.

With misaligned incentives (Chapter 17): a term shorter than the consequence cycle is misaligned incentives in the time dimension. Chapter 17's accounting for externalities, seen across periods, takes the shape of this chapter's bill handover and forward liability disclosure.

## 7 Five Self-Checks

1. Of the decisions your organization is making now, which year is the latest in which a cost falls due? Where will the person making the decision be when it does?
2. How much of your term remains? Of the things you are pushing forward, how many pay off after the term ends?
3. At the last handover of a post, did you receive a list of unfulfilled obligations? Did you ever hand one over?
4. In your organization's budget, which line holds the money that must be spent five years from now? If there is no such line, whose surprise is that money turning into?
5. Would you be willing to come back in five years and give your successor an account of what you are signing off now? List the items you would not.
