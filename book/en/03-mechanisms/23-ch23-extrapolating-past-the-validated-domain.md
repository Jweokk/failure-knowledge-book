# Chapter 23 Extrapolating Past the Validated Domain

> What this chapter covers: the mechanism that carries "validated at A" straight across to B; three criteria, four field sites, the mandatory format of the difference list, and graded release.
> When to turn back: when a pilot becomes a rollout, when a method is reused across models, when an old instrument is put to a new use, when "we have always done it this way" is being written into a new setting.
> Reading time: about 50 minutes.

Every piece of knowledge has an edge. What varies is whether anyone has written down where it falls. A validation report that says a method works is, strictly read, a broken sentence. The whole version always reads: works under these conditions, within this range, on these objects. Extrapolating past the validated domain is the name for the default repair. Whoever cites the conclusion supplies the missing clause himself, silently, and reads "valid here" as "valid everywhere". Cognition does this by default. What this chapter asks for is a declaration fee charged on each act of carrying a conclusion across an edge. Abolishing extrapolation would abolish action, and this book is not going there.

## 1 What It Is

Definition: using a conclusion that holds inside a boundary as though it were a boundary-free law, while the boundary itself is never listed and never knocked down item by item.

Machine learning calls the same problem out-of-distribution use. Engineering and medicine have older names for it: off-label use, and use past the stated limit.

Three criteria open the mechanism. Any one of them is enough.

① The environment of use differs from the environment of validation, and the difference has never been drawn up as a list.
② "We validated it at A" appears in the argument as a reason to skip inspection.
③ Someone once pointed the difference out, and it never reached the decision record.

Extrapolation is not always wrong. An organization that bans it outright cannot move, and the exploratory failure in Chapter 40 of Part Six is extrapolation under control. What this chapter puts on trial is the missing list. Carry a conclusion across an edge without declaring the difference and you have the disease. Declare the differences, work through the effect of each, run the verification they demand, and you have a rollout. Do none of it and you have haulage: the box left the depot, and nobody checked the contents against the new address.

Out-of-distribution, off-label, past the limit: three names, one structure. The structure is worth spelling out before the cases arrive. Validated knowledge lives in a box held up by five dimensions: object (whom it works for), conditions (under what), duration (validated for how long), scale (at what magnitude), population (who applies it). Extrapolating is the reach from inside the box to outside it. Accidents happen when the reaching hand still believes it is inside.

Of the five dimensions, duration and population are the ones most often left unreported. Differences in conditions can be seen (dry ground against wet ground). Differences in population hide inside the statistics, as the share of children did in the raw-milk case. Differences in duration hide in the slow variables, as cumulative damage did across reprocessing cycles. The five fields on this chapter's declaration form, object, conditions, duration, scale, population, are cut from that box. Filling the form in is not a paperwork ritual. It is how the box gets drawn.

The cost of the declaration deserves its own paragraph, because the tuning decides whether the mechanism lands anywhere. What is required is a friction added to each act, since prohibition amounts to banning action. The size of the friction has to be chosen with care: too small (a verbal declaration) and it is not there; too large (full re-validation) and it is a ban. This chapter's form sits deliberately between half a day and a day. Expensive enough that a casual extrapolation stops being casual. Cheap enough that one worth doing can still be afforded. The tuning rule is general across the book's defences: friction has to sit in the narrow band where it changes behaviour without forbidding it. A defence placed outside that band gets walked around, or repealed.

Calibration inside the band leaves one more problem, which is who reviews the form. The intuitive answer, a specialist from the same field, reproduces condition two exactly. His analogical intuition and the declarant's come from the same source, and neither will catch a difference that sits underneath both of them. The review structure therefore has to cross domains. A specialist in the field signs the technical rows (conditions, scale). A risk function from outside the field signs the cognitive rows (population, duration, object). Both signatures, or the form does not clear. The reason is the cognitive shape of the mechanism: the blind spot in an extrapolation is not technical, it is the cell nobody believes needs looking at, and the second pair of eyes exists to force that cell open.

A cell that has been looked at needs somewhere to go. Archived forms go into a searchable library, indexed by object and by dimension, and the next declarant of a similar extrapolation checks the library first. The earlier form and its outcome, whether the thing worked and whether anything went wrong, are the cheapest prior on offer. A dimension with no entry in the library is itself a red mark on the new form: this organization has never validated anything on that axis. The library compounds in the way the dark archive of Chapter 21 does. An organization's extrapolation intelligence accumulates through declarations rather than being paid for in accidents. Upkeep costs one indexing rule, and the library gives "we have done it this way before" an evidence form that can be checked for the first time.

Beyond its evidence form, extrapolation has a public exit worth gathering in, which is the citation chain of regulations and standards. An industry standard cites the standard above it, that one cites a research report, and the report's conclusion carries its own box. Every hop is an extrapolation, and most citations never re-read the boundary of what they cite. Cases 2 and 6 both run a chain of this kind, one protocol citing a device-class framework, one relief design citing an estimated heat release rate. The chain has a single self-discipline: at each citation, copy the cited conclusion's scope of validity into your own document, and the boundary travels down the chain with the citation (Device 3). The cost is one line of text. What it buys is that the boundary is not dropped at every hop.

The end of the chain is the person doing the work, which is where a boundary is most useful and hardest to deliver: the syringe in the nurse's hand, the exhaust on the tractor, the wind limit table in the tug pilot's cockpit. The field form of a boundary is an action criterion. Do not draw a dose when the graduations do not match. Check the exhaust after working wet ground. Stop when crosswind and turbulence together pass the limit. The difficulty sits in the parameterisation. Box dimensions are continuous (concentration, wind speed, cycle count); field criteria are discrete (do it, or do not). The method is to round toward strictness: cut the continuous variable into whole steps a person on site can read, and put the cut on the safe side of the true value, which is the margin convention of Chapter 25. The field version is this mechanism's last mile. Forms and conclusion sentences are paper. Only a field criterion acts in the second before the event.

That second needs calibrating in the field, repeatedly. Once the field boundary is issued, every event where a criterion fires (a job stopped, a dose refused) earns ten minutes of review, with two questions: did it fire at the right point, and is the boundary set tightly enough. The answer is written back into the criterion. Write-back keeps the field version a live document. Criteria that are too strict get loosened gradually, so the alarm keeps its credibility. Criteria that are too loose get tightened in time. Calibration records merge with the form archive, and the organization's extrapolation ledger ends up with three levels: the conclusion sentence (knowledge), the declaration form (decision), the field criterion (action). All three are cut from the same box, and each serves a different reader.

## 2 How It Grows

Three generating conditions.

Condition one: the pilot and the rollout sit in different organizations. The pilot team knows where the boundary is (which users, which city, what weather, who was watching). The rollout team is handed the conclusion with the boundary stripped off. Boundary knowledge is tacit, and it stays behind with the team that earned it.

Condition two: the analogical pull of transplanting across domains. The similarity is on the surface (all insulin, all jet display flying, all dairy). The difference is underneath (concentration scales, aircraft performance, microbial ecology). Surface similarity carries the intuition, deep difference decides whether the conclusion lives, and intuition is what speaks in the decision meeting.

Condition three: the cost of validation is carried on one side. Writing the boundary down and re-validating item by item is paid for by the user. The speed is enjoyed by the decision-maker. Nobody writes the list.

## 3 The Shape in Four Domains

### Case 1 (Aviation): An Old Jet Inside a Display Framework

av-042.

**When and where**: 22 August 2015, 12:22 UTC (13:22 local time, British Summer Time); north of Shoreham Airport, West Sussex, England, at the junction of the A27 Brighton bypass and the Old Shoreham Road. The impact point lay outside the airfield boundary, in ground the display organizer did not control (AAR 1/2017 p.17, p.211).

**What and who**: owner and operator Canfield Hunter Ltd; a Hawker Hunter T7, registration G-BXFI, built in 1955, converted from single seat to two seats in 1959, airframe at 5,976 hours at the time of the accident (p.38). The display was staged by the company that ran the Shoreham RAFA Airshow, formed for the event and leasing the airfield (p.99); the flying display director (FDD) ran the flying and applied for the permission, and a four-person flying control committee (FCC) helped monitor it (p.38, p.100). The regulator was the General Aviation Department (GAU) of the UK Civil Aviation Authority (p.192). The pilot was alone on board, 51 years old, 14,249 hours total, 43 hours on type, 115 hours in the previous 90 days (p.28).

**What they were doing**: this was his fifth Hunter display of the 2015 season (p.187). After displaying at Bruntingthorpe on 8 August he flew 9 more displays inside two weeks, 7 in an RV-8 and 2 in a Jet Provost (p.35). The Shoreham sequence was a pass, a left-hand Derry Turn, then a bent loop, a loop with roll in it so that the exit track is offset from the entry track. He had flown the same manoeuvre at the same venue in 2014, and to reduce the roll the bent loop needed he had then taken the left turn west of Lancing College; for 2015 he planned to turn between the airfield and the college (p.23). A hand-drawn map with the sequence numbered was found in his pocket afterwards (p.23). An engineer did the daily inspection on the afternoon of 21 August; the pilot did his own pre-flight check that day and asked for full tanks (p.24). Take-off was on runway 02, which slopes downhill and had a tailwind; with the high temperature and the tailwind the ground run was longer than usual and he rotated at 112 KIAS against a normal 120 (p.24). He was airborne at 12:04 (p.24). Before the display he held over the sea near Brighton at 1,800 to 2,500 ft; at 12:20 Shoreham reported wind 120° at 12 kt, temperature 24°C, QNH 1013 (p.25). He descended to a 100 ft pass on the display line, took the Derry Turn to an apex of 1,800 ft, then rolled into a descending left turn down to about 185 ft above ground, approaching the display line at about 25° (p.25). By the accident he had 19 hours 25 minutes of display and transit flying on the Hunter, the last sortie on 8 August (p.34, p.35). On 30 August 2014 he had flown the same manoeuvre at the same venue, and the exit track that day ran along the display line, parallel to the crowd line (p.118).

**The decision point**: four moments.

① 3 June 2011, the fifth Hunter conversion sortie, which was the final handling test; that day's log was endorsed "solo 'Standard' aerobatics DA" (p.31). The five training sorties before it came to 4 hours 5 minutes, three of them formation, the fourth a transit, only the fifth solo (pp. 30–31); follow-on training up to September 2011 added 2 hours 40 minutes (p.32). The test sortie's log lists handling, slow flight and "emergencies"; low-level recovery is not on it, and the report says elsewhere that he never practised a recovery on the Hunter (p.31, p.37). The type exemption (ATRE) was renewed on 28 June 2012 and again on 27 August 2014 on the basis of "holds a valid licence", with no training or check requirement attached (p.32).

② 12 September 2014, the company's chief pilot, acting as display authorisation examiner (DAE), renewed his DA. The assessment used two Jet Provosts in formation and he flew No. 2 throughout (p.32). The assessment words were about formation quality: "smooth accurate… crisp, safe and correct". The report then notes that, following a leader, he had no opportunity to show the safety gate for solo manoeuvring, the speeds, the handling or the minimum height, and no opportunity to show his positioning against the display line (p.33).

③ 24 June 2015, the DA was renewed again, this time in the RV-8, the assessment reading "leading and wingman in a two-ship RV-8 display" (p.33). Under the rules then in force, renewal in one category renewed them all, and the Hunter and Jet Provost authorities came along with it (p.34).

④ Before the 2015 show the organizer obtained a permission issued by the CAA under Article 162 of the Air Navigation Order, with a display area map issued by the CAA. The area was drawn beyond the airfield, and a 1 km radius covered the A27 junction (p.100, p.211 item 6). The FDD's risk assessment listed "aircraft impacting outside the airfield boundary" as hazard 8, severity catastrophic, probability "extremely unlikely", the judgement "unacceptable"; the mitigation read: the pilot complies with the Rules of the Air, the FDD and the FCC monitor by radio and stop the display if needed (p.102). The matrix wants the risk reduced before an "unacceptable" rating can stand, and that column was filled with those three measures. The report adds that the FDD considered the whole assessment compliant with CAP 403 (p.102, p.214 item 36).

**What was visible then**: the night before, he read his own ground notes, which covered type handling and the limits to be observed (p.23). He had firm numbers for the Jet Provost safety gate: 220 kt minimum entry for a low-level loop, 2,200 ft at the apex, plus the height the manoeuvre needs to finish (p.29). CAP 403, 13th edition, February 2015, paragraph 5.58: all aerobatic manoeuvres, inverted passes and manoeuvres pulled past the vertical included, must be flown above the approved display height; the DA currency requirement was three displays or practices in 90 days, one of them on the display type (p.36, p.37). His own DA form gave a Hunter minimum pass height of 100 ft, aerobatic category S, minimum aerobatic height 500 ft (p.34). His own written manoeuvre parameters: entry not below 350 KIAS, no entry below that; apex gate 4,000 ft, made up of 3,000 ft for the second half of the loop, plus 500 ft for the authorised minimum height, plus 500 ft of margin (p.24). The organizer's briefing document for participants, section 4: fly only manoeuvres known and practised, and keep the aircraft positioned at all times so that, if the engine or the structure fails, the impact point is outside the crowd area (p.100). The ten-hazard list of the risk assessment; the Article 162 permission and its map (pp. 100–102). Weather: Stansted 150° at 14 kt, 28°C, dew point 16°C, QNH 1014; Shoreham at 12:20, 120° at 12 kt, 24°C, QNH 1013 (pp. 24–25). Weight: full fuel plus a 180 lb pilot, 18,814 lb before take-off against a maximum of 25,000 lb, centre of gravity inside limits (p.50). Technical state: a test flight after maintenance on 20 March, on which he reported a problem with the right altimeter verbally (p.51); the test flight record of 26 June 2011 shows engine speed at 8,250 rpm three times and 8,350 rpm once, above the 8,100±50 limit, with no technical log entry, no inspection, and the endorsement "airworthy and serviceable" (p.50). Known only afterwards: the fuel regulator diaphragm was aged past the point of airworthiness (p.200, p.212); the left altimeter read about 100 ft low in test and lagged, and at a 2,700 ft apex its pointer partly covered the thousands counter (p.180, p.212); at the day's weight and density altitude the second half of the loop needed at least 2,600 to 2,950 ft (p.212); the bent loop rolled about 60° and the exit track pointed straight at the A27 (p.26, p.112).

**What happened.** 11 people on the ground died and 13 were injured (the pilot seriously, 1 seriously, 11 slightly), and the aircraft was destroyed (p.27). Impact speed was about 225 kt (p.213). The pilot survived; canopy jettison and the ejection seat were triggered by the impact, and he did not initiate ejection himself (p.216 items 57 and 59). Daily traffic on the A27 runs at about 58,500 vehicles (p.215). The report made 11 safety recommendations, on top of 21 from three earlier special bulletins (p.21).

Back to the three questions. The commonest answer to the first is "roll out of the loop if the apex is short". In this case that action cost four seconds: flight test showed the recovery could still be made within four seconds of passing the apex, he had never practised it on the Hunter, and he had said he was unsure whether he could roll at 105 KIAS (p.37, p.181). The item most often missed in answer to the second is the four loops flown in the Jet Provost the weekend before: apexes at 2,750, 3,150, 2,800 and 2,900 ft, speeds of 105 to 130 KIAS, almost coincident with the 2,700 ft and 105 KIAS of the accident day (p.67, p.213). On the Jet Provost those are normal figures. On the Hunter they are zero margin. The third question lands on the renewal before the season began: assess by type, and write the entry speed and the apex gate into the sequence list handed to the FDD. The report adds one sentence of its own: of the 13 fatal accidents at UK airshows between 1996 and 2015, this was the only one that killed a member of the public (p.123 table 8 note 38), and a history with no third-party deaths does not establish that the activity is safe (p.200).

**Options ruled out**: ejection, since the cockpit sat outside the ejection envelope for most of the segment after the apex, and the seat cartridge's two-year installed life was four and a half years over and its six-year total life a year over (p.198, p.216); a stop called by the FCC, since at the apex the aircraft was plainly below the gate, but the FCC did not know what the gate was and had no means of measuring height and speed accurately, and the 2014 video of the aircraft overflying the Lancing residential area had been neither noticed nor acted on at the time (p.190, p.215); sending the manoeuvre sequence into the approval process, since nothing then required the organizer or the FDD to hold a per-display sequence list, on the grounds that it was unnecessary (p.106, p.214), and the same report cites the Canadian practice of submitting distance, entry and exit points, speeds and minimum heights for each manoeuvre 10 days before the show (p.106); renewal by type, since AAIB only recommended renewal by category or type in a 2016 special bulletin and the CAA reply put the study out to April 2018 (pp. 188–189); separating aerobatic airspace from people on the ground, since AAIB recommended designated aerobatic airspace with no non-essential personnel, vehicles or vessels beneath it (2016-039) and the CAA did not accept it (p.194), after which the report records another line of thinking, that deviation from the Rules of the Air be allowed only where participants control the area and a sufficient risk assessment has been done (p.195); flying the 2014 sequence again as it stood, since that year the apex margin was sufficient and the exit track ran along the display line (p.118), a precedent that made "fly it again" look like something needing no fresh validation.

**Early signals**: ① the Shoreham display of 30 August 2014, aerobatics over a residential area, on video, noticed and acted on by nobody at the time (p.190, p.194); the report later found such overflights were "not confined to one pilot, one aircraft or one venue" (p.192); it was pressed down because nothing had gone wrong. ② the renewal of 12 September 2014, flown as No. 2 in formation, the assessment text about formation quality only and the instruction column carrying a single line, "discussed the importance of instruction and continued supervision" (pp. 32–33); it was pressed down because formation flying is part of display flying too. ③ two renewals in 20 months, neither of them on the Hunter, Jet Provost formation in September 2014 and RV-8 in June 2015 (pp. 32–34); they were pressed down because the rules let one category cover all of them. ④ the test flight record of 26 June 2011, three readings at 8,250 and one at 8,350 rpm over the limit, no entry, no inspection, signed "airworthy" (p.50); it was pressed down because the flight itself was normal and the overspeed only momentary. ⑤ the previous weekend's four Jet Provost loops, apexes of 2,750 to 3,150 ft at 105 to 130 KIAS, close to the accident day (p.67), a point the report wrote into the causal analysis on its own (p.189); it was pressed down because that was another aircraft, and nobody had put the two sets of numbers side by side.

**The point of escalation**: the RV-8 renewal of 24 June 2015 locked the whole season's Hunter authority into place. From that day to 22 August no Hunter assessment stood behind it (pp. 33–34), and the six Hunter displays inside 90 days satisfied the currency requirement exactly, so the continuity of the authority showed no gap (p.37). On the day he had only the one public display (p.211 item 2), the tanks were full, and on paper the permission ran to 10 March 2016 (p.38, p.50). On the signing side, the 2014 signature came from the operator's own chief pilot acting as DAE (p.32); the 2015 renewal needed assessment in one category only, and the assessor is not named (p.34). After that, full fuel on 21 August, the tailwind take-off on 22 August at 112 KIAS instead of 120, 24°C and an airfield density altitude of 1,310 ft each shaved the energy budget thinner (p.24, p.50, p.317). After the 20 March test flight he had reported the right altimeter verbally; the instrument went for repair, and the repairer said there was no component history card and its history could not be established (p.51, p.53). By 12:22, pulling at 310 KIAS and 185 ft, the only switch left was a rolling recovery within four seconds of the apex (p.178, p.181). The escalation took the form of continuity of authority and continuity of energy stacked on one timeline.

**Failure chain**: technical layer: entry at 310 KIAS, 40 below his own gate; engine speed falling from 7,530 to between 6,500 and 6,800 in the climb, 7,010 at the apex, all below maximum thrust (p.119); about 60° of roll, and tests show a loop with 90° of track change in the climb reaching an apex 300 to 400 ft lower than a straight loop (p.211); apex 2,700 ft against a requirement of 2,600 to 2,950 ft (p.212); 105 KIAS at the apex, with a rolling recovery never practised on the type (p.37); the sorties that produced the gate data had apex density altitudes between 2,400 and 3,400 ft against 4,000 ft on the day, a difference of 1,500 ft (p.321).

Organizational layer: DA renewal in one category could cover them all (p.34); neither renewal was flown on the Hunter (p.213 item 30); the manoeuvre sequence did not have to go to the FDD (p.214 item 38); the risk assessment used the 500 ft rule in the Rules of the Air as its mitigation while the Article 162 permission waived that rule within 1 km of the crowd, and the FDD did not know about the waiver (p.214 items 40 and 41); the CAA inspected 7% of the displays it permitted in 2015 (p.215 item 55) and had never set an acceptable level of safety for display flying (p.216 item 56).

Incentive layer: the Hunter is expensive to run, its engine life is finite and spares are scarce, so training was arranged by riding along with other flying tasks (p.186); the cost of writing the boundary down and re-validating by type fell on the operator and the regulator, while a complete display season and an available venue paid out on the spot.

The break sits between the organizational layer and the incentive layer. The only two documents that could have stopped this extrapolation before the event, the per-type renewal record and the manoeuvre sequence list, did not exist at the time, and they did not exist because neither carried any current return for any signatory.

**Minimum counterfactual**: turn the renewal of 24 June 2015 into a solo assessment on the Hunter, one sortie containing one bent loop and one rolling recovery past the apex, marked by the examiner against 350 KIAS and 4,000 ft. The action falls two months before the accident, the aircraft was at North Weald, the DAE was the company's own chief pilot, and no luck is required (pp. 32–33, p.186). The cost is calculable: his solo final handling test in 2011 was one sortie of 1 hour 05 minutes (p.31). No new regulation and no separate CAA approval are needed, because the rules then allowed renewal by category and did not forbid doing it by type (p.34, p.188). Had that assessment been marked against the 4,000 ft gate, the "sufficient margin" success of 2014 would have stopped being a reason to skip the next check (p.118). The step changes no part of the display sequence and no clause of the framework. It puts one assessment on the aircraft being assessed. The report itself records that both renewals "complied with the rules of the time" (p.188, p.213 item 30), and inside those rules this step was available.

**Outcome and sources**: 11 dead, 13 injured; 11 recommendations, plus 21 from three special bulletins; the report also asked the Department for Transport to commission an independent review of UK airshow governance (2017-011), the CAA's own review having produced 29 actions (p.210); the answer to 2016-041, renewal by type, was a review of category structures and a study of behaviour transfer between types, to be completed by April 2018 (p.189). The CAA has since withdrawn the general minimum-height exemption for airshows, permissions now state the display area boundary, and a report is required within seven days of the display (p.193, p.195); 2016-039, clearing the ground beneath aerobatic airspace, was not accepted (p.194). Case 1 leaves the word "framework" with a permanent problem. A safety framework is designed around the energy characteristics of the known fleet: display line distance and exclusion zone size are scaled to the speeds and turn radii of the common types. For every new type, the framework is therefore performing an implicit extrapolation, on the assumption that the new type's energy characteristics fall inside the existing envelope. The declarant in that implicit form is the framework's maintainer, and what the report pushed for after this case was, in substance, the writing of "a new type must submit an energy declaration on entry" as an explicit step. A reader can run the same check on his own framework: which class of object are your isolation distances, authority thresholds and approval levels calibrated to, and whether a step exists where a new object declares its energy, scale or risk characteristics. The generality of a framework is an assumption nobody has validated. Making it explicit costs one table.

### Case 2 (Drugs and Devices): A Reprocessing Protocol That Covered a Model It Should Not Have

ph-007. An Ethicon XCEL trocar (5 mm), circulated after reprocessing by Stryker Sustainability Solutions. FDA device recall record Z-3183-2026, the API record checked for this book: the reprocessed device may crack, and 2,838 units were recalled. Reprocessing and reuse of single-use devices has mature protocols and a regulatory framework behind it. The framework's applicability to this model's structure, meaning the integrity of the bladeless trocar's seal and hub connections after reprocessing cycles, was covered over by the protocol's generality. The protocol had validated that devices of this class can be processed this way. It had never validated this one.

Stop at the decision point: the review moment when the reprocessing protocol added the new model to its list and the batch was released. On the table: the protocol's prior safety record, the structural difference between the new model and its predecessors, and the release schedule. **Outcome.** Recall, batch withdrawn. Primary source: FDA, Enforcement Report Z-3183-2026, 2026, link at Appendix B ph-007.

Case 2 has an institutional counterpart in devices. The FDA regulates reprocessed single-use devices by device class, and the seam between a class-level generic protocol and a model-level structural difference is exactly where this case sits. The management answer is to align the granularity of the two lists: the validity statement goes by model, which one was validated, and the release decision goes by class, any of which may pass. Alignment costs documentation work, and it buys a release step that can answer "was this one validated" every time it is asked. Any reader can run the same alignment check: ask whether the validity statement behind your generic protocol, template or SOP is written at the granularity of a class or of an individual. One step off in granularity puts the boundary one step off, and a boundary one step off has already produced one accident in Case 2 and another in Case 3.

#### Case 6 (Chemical): A Scale-Up with No Reactivity Data (condensed)

ch-006. A reactor ran away into a decomposition explosion in November 2024, in the United States. CSB report page (2026, page checked for this book): 2 dead and 2 seriously injured, with vessel fragments thrown beyond the site boundary; the investigation records three findings, no reactivity test data, an emergency relief system inadequately designed, and no process safety management system. The chapter's criteria light on the first finding. Process scale-up from bench to production is the classic extrapolation, and reactivity data is the first line of a scale-up declaration. Without data, the relief system can only be sized against an extrapolated heat release rate, and the error in that estimate gets amplified, in a runaway, into vessel fragments. Minimum counterfactual: complete reactivity testing (adiabatic calorimetry, for instance) before scale-up, and size the relief system on measured data. Primary source: CSB, 2026, link at Appendix B ch-006.

#### Case 7 (Aviation): An Old Manoeuvre After a Safety Notice (condensed)

av-005. An R44 helicopter in October 2021, in Germany. BFU report (2022, checked for this book): during a low-g manoeuvre the rotor hub struck and cut through the mast, the aircraft broke up in flight, and 3 people died; the manufacturer's safety notice had stated the low-g risk long before. Transplanting display manoeuvre conventions between types is the aviation form of condition two: a manoeuvre that holds on one type gets carried to another as part of the common display vocabulary. The safety notice wrote the boundary down, and the boundary statement never entered the operating convention. Minimum counterfactual: check the display fleet's manoeuvre list against manufacturer safety notices, type by type, and mark any manoeuvre a notice covers as barred. Primary source: BFU, 2022, link at Appendix B av-005.

#### Case 8 (Aviation): A Tug Take-Off in Crosswind (condensed)

av-025. A glider tug in December 2025, in the United Kingdom. AAIB report (2026, page checked for this book): after take-off in strong crosswind and turbulence the aircraft lost control and hit the ground; the tug pilot was seriously injured and nobody in the glider was hurt. The tug's operating envelope had been established in everyday conditions, and strong crosswind plus turbulence stacked two variables from outside it at the same moment. The decision to continue the take-off rested on a success baseline from everyday conditions, which is the weather version of criterion ②, "we validated it at A". Minimum counterfactual: write the combination limit on wind speed and turbulence into the operating standard, and stop when it is exceeded. Primary source: AAIB, 2026, link at Appendix B av-025.

### Case 3 (Healthcare): The Boundary of a Syringe Scale

me-002. Medical wards of an English general hospital. HSIB report (Administering high-strength insulin from a pen device in hospital, 2022, archived snapshot checked for this book): U-500 high-strength insulin, at 500 units per millilitre. The dedicated safety needle for the pen device disappeared from the supply catalogue after a contract change, and nurses switched to drawing the dose with a U-100 syringe: a syringe graduated at 100 units per millilitre used on fluid at 500 units per millilitre, every graduation on the barrel five times the intended dose, and the patient had two hypoglycaemic events. The U-100 syringe's validated boundary was the world of U-100 concentration. The instrument did not change. The world changed five-fold, and a qualified tool inside the boundary became an overdose generator outside it.

Stop at the decision point: the safety needle disappearing from the catalogue, and the nurse choosing a substitute instrument before drawing the dose. On the table: the syringe at hand, the concentration on the label, and the seam along which the catalogue change notice never reached the ward. **Outcome.** Two hypoglycaemic events; the report made recommendations along the chain from supply to bedside. Primary source: HSIB, 2022, link at Appendix B me-002 (archived link, grade A−).

The five-fold scale in Case 3 is worth one piece of risk arithmetic. The instrument's qualification did not change, since a U-100 syringe is still the right tool for U-100 fluid. What changed was the fluid. The matching relation between tool and fluid was implicit, held by catalogue pairing and by habit, and it had never been written as an explicit check item. When a distant action such as a contract change alters the matching relation, the clinical end knows nothing, because the change's blast radius on the supply chain (Chapter 21, Device 2) has no mechanism for reaching the end user. HSIB's recommendations along that chain repair the notification line. The cross-industry generalisation is plain: wherever a tool and a material sit in an implicit matching relation (instrument and fluid, blade and material, code and dependency version), write the relation as an explicit check item, and give changes to the relation a notification line that reaches the point of use. Making the relation explicit is one-off documentation work. Its return arrives at every supplier change and every catalogue adjustment.

### Case 4 (Agriculture): Raw Milk's Safety Assumption and 171 Cases

ag-001. Commercially distributed raw milk in California, from September 2023. CDC MMWR report (Vol. 74, page checked for this book): 171 confirmed cases in a Salmonella Typhimurium outbreak, 22 hospitalised, of whom 120 (70%) were under 18; the timeline runs from first illness on 21 September, to the county health department's report on 18 October, to detection in samples on 23 October, to the production halt and recall on 24 October, to resumption of production on 31 October. Raw milk is unpasteurised, and its saleability rests on assumptions about process and testing. The outbreak showed the boundary of those assumptions, batches, microbial flora, sampling rate, consuming population, failing at commercial scale.

Stop at the decision point: the approval of resumption on 31 October, with the testing regime carried over unchanged. On the table: the post-recall remediation list, the sampling scheme, and the epidemiological fact of the share of children in the consuming population. **Outcome.** Further cases appeared inside the resumption period (MMWR coverage runs to March 2024). Primary source: CDC, MMWR Vol. 74, 2025, link at Appendix B ag-001.

The resumption approval deserves taking apart. The remediation list focused on testing, on sampling rate and batch management, and the population difference the outbreak had exposed, seven cases in ten being minors, was not among the conditions to be re-checked before production restarted. The testing question is whether this batch may leave the plant. The population question is who this batch is more dangerous to. The point where the two meet, risk weighted by population, was absent from the resumption decision, and the extrapolation ran on from the plant gate to the shelf. Further cases inside the coverage period turn that absence into an outcome. A general checklist for resumption decisions therefore gains a line: review the structure of the consuming population, who eats the product and who is most vulnerable, with demographic difference entering the declaration form at the specification of its first line.

The wording of a declaration form has one more job to do, which is anti-narrative. Keep narrative words such as "natural", "pure" and "traditional process" out of validation documents. A narrative word need not be false. Raw milk is natural. What makes the word useful is that it underwrites the boundary: a reader who sees "natural" fills in "safe", and the basis of the fill-in is the feel of the word rather than data. The language discipline for validation documents, Device 3's boundary in the conclusion sentence, therefore gains a banned-word list: conclusions and boundaries may use data words only, concentration, dose, detection rate, temperature; narrative words belong in marketing documents, and the two document types are signed off by different owners carrying different liability. The list's value is not censorship, since nobody can police language. It is a reminder at the moment of writing. The moment you put a narrative word on the page, you should know that the sentence has left validated ground.

#### Case 5 (Manufacturing): An Exhaust Validated on Dry Ground (full card)

mn-011.

**When and where**: a recall in January 2013, after several years on sale; the United States. CPSC recall notice, page checked for this book.

**What and who**: about 25,000 commercial all-terrain vehicles of one model; the defect was that in wet conditions leaf debris accumulated and the exhaust ignited it.

**What they were doing**: performance validation was completed on dry ground. Exhaust temperature, insulation distance and clearance to combustibles were all designed and passed against dry-ground operation. Real use contained a variable dry-ground validation had not covered, which was vegetation debris from working wet ground. Wet debris clings to the exhaust surface, bakes on, accumulates, carbonises and ignites at exhaust temperature; on dry ground the debris either does not cling or gets blown away, so validation had no reason to doubt the surface temperature. After 18 fire reports and one hand burn, the manufacturer recalled about 25,000 units with a repair kit. The validation box (dry ground, typical vegetation, normal work rhythm) and the use box (wet ground, heavy debris, continuous low-speed work) shared one machine and not one set of physics.

**The decision point**: the review of the product definition and the validation plan, which is to say the approval of the validation condition list. On the table: the description of the target market and work scenarios, the cost and schedule constraints on validation, the validation conventions of comparable products, and one question that was not on the list, which is the environment we never simulated in which the target user will drive this machine.

**What was visible then**: the validation condition list, the scope-of-use statement in the product manual, the climate and work types of the target market, all of them discoverable. Known only afterwards: the form of the debris accumulation and the ignition path, which appeared only in the post-accident engineering analysis.

**Stop point.** You are sitting in this seat with the material above in front of you.

> **Stop here. Decide before you turn the page.**
>
> 1. What would you do? Write the action, not "pay more attention".
> 2. Which item here are you most likely to miss, and why does it look unimportant?
> 3. If you may set one checkpoint, when is it and what does it check?

**What happened.** 18 fire reports and one hand burn, with about 25,000 units recalled for a repair kit. Back to the three questions. The commonest answer to the first is "add a heat shield", and a heat shield is a retrospective patch, while the prospective move is putting wet-ground operation into the validation matrix, even as a single extreme case. The item most often missed in answer to the second is the validation condition list itself, which reads as a technical document and is in fact the product's boundary declaration: the conditions absent from it are the world the company has not promised. The third question lands on the validation matrix review, which must answer with the list of extreme work scenarios in the target market; the gap between that list and the matrix is the difference list for the declaration form. Primary source: CPSC, 2013, link at Appendix B mn-011.

**Options ruled out**: putting wet-ground conditions into the validation matrix, squeezed out by cost and schedule; restricting wet use in the manual, commercially unworkable and unenforceable in practice; a dedicated post-launch complaint category for debris, since complaints went through the routine quality process and were never aggregated by scenario.

**Early signals**: ① wet-ground work appears in the marketing material and not in the validation matrix, pressed down because "marketing speaks in scenarios and validation works to standards"; ② occasional "the exhaust runs hot" feedback through the repair channel, pressed down as "normal"; ③ competitors' validation conventions also excluded wet ground, pressed down because "that is how the industry does it".

**The point of escalation**: every year of sales without a fire vouched for the dry-ground validation, and the sales share in wet regions was not tracked, so exposure had no ledger. The escalation took the form of exposure made invisible.

**Failure chain**: technical layer, wet debris accumulating, carbonising and igniting at exhaust temperature. Organizational layer, no declaration mechanism for the gap between validation conditions and real use. Incentive layer, validation cost immediate, fire risk delayed and probabilistic. All three broke at once. The break sits where marketing and validation use two scenario languages, marketing working by scenario and validation working by standard, and the fork in language is why the difference was never declared.

**Minimum counterfactual**: at the validation matrix review, force the marketing scenario list and the validation condition list side by side and declare each difference item. One action, in product definition, costing half a day of review.

**Outcome and sources**: 18 fires and one burn, with about 25,000 units recalled. Primary source: CPSC, 2013, link at Appendix B mn-011.

## 4 What Shows Earliest

Six weak signals, each with the reason usually used to press it down.

1. The rollout plan has no section on what changed against the pilot. Reason: "the pilot already proved it."
2. A difference is acknowledged out loud ("the users here are different") and never appears in the decision record. Reason: "everyone knows."
3. The conclusion sentence of a validation report carries no scope of validity. Reason: "who reads that closely."
4. Reuse across models, populations or regions triggers no re-validation. Reason: "same principle."
5. A difference someone raised disappears from the minutes. Reason: "meeting time was short."
6. A boundary event, some minor anomaly, is explained as normal fluctuation outside the pilot. Reason: "the sample is still small."

Put the six signals back against the eight cases. Signal 1, no difference section in the rollout plan, matches Case 5, where the validation matrix and the marketing scenarios were never placed side by side. Signal 2, difference acknowledged verbally, matches Case 3, where the concentration difference lived in the nurse's common sense (the graduations differ) and not in a process check item. Signal 3, no scope in the conclusion sentence, matches Case 2, where the reprocessing protocol stated its validity by class. Signal 4, reuse without re-validation, matches Case 7, where a manoeuvre vocabulary crossed between types. Signal 5, the raised difference vanishing from the minutes, matches Case 8, where the wind limit judgement sat in the crew's conversation and not in the operating standard. Signal 6, the boundary event explained as fluctuation, matches Case 4, where early signals before the outbreak were never aggregated by scenario.

Re-sort the six by what it costs to find them. Signal 3 is cheapest, a matter of reading conclusion sentences. Signals 1 and 2 come next, reading documents and minutes. Signals 4 and 5 need cross-department comparison. Signal 6 is the most expensive, because it needs anomaly data aggregated by scenario. The starting advice is the usual one: do the cheap thing first. run every conclusion sentence in your organization's validation documents through a single pass, once, and rewrite the ones without a scope of validity to Device 3's specification. That rewrite makes every downstream citation carry the boundary automatically. The document-layer work is one-off, and its return is set by the number of times the document is cited.

## 5 How to Defend

Device 1: the extrapolation declaration form. Any act that carries a conclusion outside its validation environment files one form: the original validation's boundary (sample, conditions, duration), the target environment's difference list, and an item-by-item statement of why each difference does not affect the conclusion. The items whose effect cannot be stated are exactly the items that need supplementary verification, so the form's value is that it exposes what cannot be explained. The form is archived. It is the raw voucher of "what was known at the time" for any later postmortem.

Device 2: independent supplementation of the difference list. The user drafts the list and the pilot team supplements it, the two sides listing separately and not permitted to merge their discussion before writing. Listed separately the differences come out complete, since what the pilot team knows is often in the user's blind spot and the reverse also holds. The union of the two lists is the real difference surface.

Device 3: the boundary written into the conclusion sentence. Every validation report's conclusion sentence carries its scope of validity ("holds under conditions X"). Downstream citations that strip the boundary count as citation errors and enter the postmortem ledger. What this changes is a writing habit: once the boundary is promoted from footnote to body text, its probability of being cited stops being zero.

Device 4: graded release. Extrapolations are graded by the item count of the difference list. Zero differences, released directly. With differences, verification intensity and monitoring density rise with the count. Where a difference touches a safety boundary, direct extrapolation is barred. The three criteria for exploratory failure in Chapter 40 connect here: controlled extrapolation needs a declaration, a budget ceiling and a stop-loss point. Grading turns extrapolation from "do it if you can" into "do it by the price list".

Run the four devices against this chapter's cases. Device 1 on Case 5: declare the validation condition list and the target market scenario list side by side, with the wet-ground row filled into the difference list. Device 2 on Case 3: the clinical side and the supply side each write a list of what this catalogue change altered, and "the safety needle is gone" is a row the clinical list carries and the supply list does not. Device 3 on Case 2: rewrite the reprocessing protocol's validity statement to run by model ("this protocol is validated for model X over N cycles"). Device 4 on Case 6: grade the scale-up project by its count of difference items, and bar direct release where reactivity data is missing.

The four devices have four failure modes. The declaration form dies when declaration becomes a formality: the difference list reads "no significant difference" and no reviewer asks a follow-up. The patch is that a "no significant difference" statement must name the dimensions checked, five checked and each written as no difference, and a declaration that cannot name its dimensions is treated as undeclared. Independent supplementation dies when the pilot side is absent: the two-column table gets drafted by the user, and the pilot column stays blank year after year as the team disperses and people move. The patch is that the pilot's boundary knowledge is written into the closing report when the pilot ends, with the boundary clauses a mandatory deliverable of closure. Boundary-in-the-conclusion dies of format compliance: a scope of validity is present and empty ("wide applicability"). The patch is that a scope must carry specific dimension values, conditions, sample size, duration. Graded release dies of standard drift: the definition of a safety boundary gets loosened a step at a time, "touches a safety boundary" becoming "touches a major safety boundary". The patch is that the grading definitions go into the rulebook, with revision requiring a governance signature. All four deaths share one health question: did the last declaration form change a decision. A form that has never changed a decision is the new paperwork.

The relation between the declaration form and insurance or regulatory filings is worth a sentence. An archived form has a second use as an insurance submission, the difference list and the supplementary verification records going in with the application, and its value at claim time is that it is the raw voucher of what was known. A decision with a declaration record and a decision without one are treated very differently in claims and in litigation. The document exists to make the decision-maker's state of knowledge reconstructable, not to absolve him, and Chapter 10's evidence grading extends here to the decision document itself.

## 6 Boundaries with Adjacent Mechanisms

Against where intuitive expertise breaks down (Chapter 14): extrapolation is the transport of a conclusion, on the data layer; the failure zone is the transport of the judge, on the human layer. Where one scene occupies both, check the person first, his premises from experience, and the conclusion second, its validation boundary. Repairing the person is cheap and repairing the conclusion is expensive.

Against narrative before evidence (Chapter 13): narrative-first means the conclusion has run ahead of the evidence, while boundary extrapolation means the evidence has run past its range of validity. The first lacks evidence and the second lacks a boundary declaration. Sizewell's "replication" narrative (Chapter 13, Case 2) and this chapter's difference list are two faces of one coin.

Against crossing a scale threshold (Chapter 24): a change of scale is the commonest difference item and the most underrated, since a pilot of ten becoming a rollout of a hundred thousand changes the distributions of users, load and anomalies all at once. A change of scale belongs on the first line of the difference list.

## 7 Five Self-Checks

1. Your organization's most recent pilot-to-rollout: does a difference list exist? Who drafted it? Has the pilot side read it?
2. The validation conclusions you have cited: does the conclusion sentence carry a scope of validity? If not, what boundary did you supply yourself?
3. The most recent reuse across models, populations or regions in your field: who was asked to re-validate? Nobody, or nobody?
4. Which steps of your product or process are done "the way we have always done them", with the original validation conditions no longer findable?
5. Take an old validation report of yours and rewrite its conclusion sentence with the boundary attached. Once rewritten, does it still support the way the thing is being used now?
