# Chapter 15 Practical Drift (Snook)

> What this chapter covers: how formal procedure and actual practice can diverge for years without producing a casualty; Scott Snook's drift model, its three activating criteria, four settings in the field, and the two-end alignment defence.
> When to turn back: when what training teaches no longer matches what the site does; before you write a new rule that "must be followed"; after an audit finds that practice and manual disagree.
> Estimated reading time: 50 minutes.

The hardest thing about spotting practical drift is that it produces no victims while it runs. Every crew works by a method it has worked out for itself, and the work gets done well. Every checklist is ticked as the procedure requires, and the ticks are all there. Studying the 1994 shootdown of a US Army Black Hawk by a friendly fighter, Scott Snook found the frightening shape of the structure: every link in that accident had been running in exactly the same way, every day, before it. He named the phenomenon practical drift. Organizations do not slide into chaos. They drift, each unit toward what is convenient for that unit.

## 15.1 What it is

Definition: practical drift is the gradual departure of actual practice from formal procedure, where the departure persists over a long period, nobody corrects it, and members of the organization come to regard the fork as normal. The departure is no sudden impulse toward risk. It is a local adaptation to real working conditions. The workload changed, the staffing changed, the tools changed, the manual did not, and reality grew a second method.

Three activating criteria. The mechanism has started if any one of them appears:

1. Formal procedure and actual practice have diverged for a long time, and the divergence has a substantial history;
2. The divergence is read as expertise: departing from the manual is treated as proof of professionalism;
3. Nobody believes he is violating anything, because the rule is written in a form no one could follow.

The third criterion is the dividing line between drift and ordinary violation, and the reason drift's consequences run heavier. A violator knows what he is risking. A drifter does not, because the procedure he bypassed is missing from his risk model altogether.

Snook's book deserves two paragraphs of background, since they explain why a model drawn from military research is not confined to the military. In April 1994, in the northern Iraq no-fly zone, two US Air Force F-15 fighters shot down two US Army Black Hawk helicopters, and the United Nations personnel aboard were all killed. Snook's finding was that every unit taking part in that sortie, the fighter wing, the helicopter detachment, the air traffic control agency and the joint operations centre, was running on its own actual version of the mission; each version departed from the joint task regulation, and each departure had been happening daily for months without consequence. He pictured the organization as two ends of a drift: at one end the baseline practice written in the regulation, at the other the actual practice each unit executed. During a consequence-free stretch, nobody looks at the distance between the two ends.

That background also supplies a key property. Drift happens unit by unit. Each crew, each post, each station drifts on its own, at a speed and in a direction set by local working conditions; the organization holds no single drift quantity that can be read off, because drift keeps no central record. What an accident needs is only to string already-drifted units onto one event chain, and on the day they are strung together every unit is doing what it does on any other day. Snook called this the latent structure of drift: every site on the eve of a disaster is as calm as routine.

Three words need separating under this chapter's usage: deviation, violation, drift. A deviation is a one-off departure, archivable afterwards, the raw material of Chapter 11. A violation is a deliberate bypass of a rule the actor knows exists. Drift is the process by which the fork becomes the default, and the actor gradually loses any sense that the rule exists. The relation is progressive. A deviation is tolerated repeatedly and hardens into a convention; the convention settles into the site's normal way of working; the gap between the normal way and the rule is forgotten, and drift is complete. The best position for a defence line is between the first level and the second, which is where the three-state register for deviation ledgers does its work (Chapter 11, Device 1). The worst position is to start checking for violations after the third level, because by then you face a complete parallel rulebook, and punishing anyone you pick is a misjudgement.

The two-end alignment mentioned at the head of the chapter can now be stated properly. Snook's remedy is not a return to the regulation, since most regulations genuinely lag reality. His remedy is to align baseline and actual at both ends on a regular schedule: either amend the regulation until it can be executed in reality, or resource reality until it can execute the regulation as written. The operative word is regular. Alignment is a calibration act; drift is a continuous process; a single alignment does not stop the next drift. Alignment has to be mounted on the organizational calendar, twice a year and on any change, and become a standing operation the way instrument calibration is. Device 1 in section 15.5, the manual-reality comparison, is that alignment in institutional form.

## 15.2 How it grows

Four generating conditions, in structure, incentive, time and information.

The structural condition: rules are written by people who do not work the line, and they are written from the design state rather than the operating state. The incentive condition: following the book carries an immediate cost, while the cost of departing is delayed and probabilistic. The time condition: drift needs time, and every "this trip is special, simplify it once" leaves no record behind. The information condition: no comparison mechanism exists, so nobody measures the difference between manual and reality.

Then there is drift's arithmetic. A single departure is usually harmless, which is Chapter 9's point that one broken layer is a fault. The disaster form of drift is several fork points stacking in the same direction on one event chain.

Three features of the dynamics deserve adding.

Drift has a directional bias. It always drifts toward lower immediate cost. Pausing, cross-checking, waiting, reading back: each of these actions costs time now and pays off somewhere else, and the first cut of drift always lands here. To observe an organization's drift direction is to observe how it prices time.

Drift is contagious. A new member meets two demonstrations, the manual version and the site version, and the trainer will teach the site version, because that is the one that gets the job done. Drift therefore copies itself automatically through staff turnover, and every handover is an inoculation. Chapter 30's handover rupture connects to this chapter at exactly that point.

Drift self-organizes a shared understanding of when drifting is allowed. Crews form default terms among themselves: which steps may be dropped when it is busy, which may never be dropped. The understanding is never written down and is executed with fair consistency, and its existence is itself proof that drift is no error; it is the organization revising its rules through action. This is also where prevention gets hard. What you face is not a lapse of memory. It is a parallel rule system, running in fact.

Push the arithmetic one step further and the disaster form appears. Each fork point strung on an event chain passed every safety review on its own, because the object of review is the regulation, not the practice. A drifted organization therefore shows a particular compliance face: the paper system is complete, and the paper system has nothing to do with reality. Three of this chapter's four cases carry that face.

The relation between drift and audit needs its own warning. Routine audit is drift's incubator. The benchmark an audit compares against is a document, and the carrier it inspects is a document as well: records, signatures, ledgers. Drift grows precisely on the layer outside documents. A fully drifted practice can generate a complete set of passing audit evidence, because the drifted organization learned long ago to complete the paperwork. Case 9's working papers and Case 3's surgical checklist are both conforming output from that production line. Two conclusions follow. An organization whose audit finds zero differences is the one whose drift level most deserves suspicion. And audit method has to include site observation and shadow comparison, described in section 15.4; an audit that reads only documents is structurally blind to drift.

## 15.3 The pattern in four domains

This section held four cases when it was first drafted (aviation, maritime, healthcare, construction) and holds nine after expansion; Cases 5 to 9 (aviation, maritime, nuclear, finance) were added. Nine cases cover the hosts drift commonly lives in: the seam between training and design, the seam between scheduling and the body, the seam between tagging and handover, the seam between checklist and pause, the seam between template and object.

#### Case 1 (Aviation): The Logbook in Flight

**Case ID**: av-020.

**When and where**: 18:57 local time, 9 March 2023, mountainous terrain near the town of Franklin, Macon County, North Carolina, United States. The helicopter departed Murphy (5NC4) at 18:44 bound for Asheville (NC95), 13 minutes en route (NTSB ERA23LA147 p. 1, p. 4). The flight was at night, in Class G airspace, on a company VFR flight plan, with no air traffic control clearance (p. 4).

**What and who**: an EC135 P2+ helicopter, registration N558MT, manufactured in 2012, operated by Med-Trans Corporation (MED-TRANS CORPORATION, trading as LifeForce) under Part 135 on-demand air taxi, air medical, holding a Part 135 operating certificate (p. 3). Four people were aboard: one pilot and three passengers, one of whom the report mentions was a flight nurse. The pilot was 51, held a commercial certificate with airplane and helicopter ratings, 4,723 total flight hours, 1,867 on type; his last proficiency check was on 1 March 2023, and his second-class medical carried no limitations (p. 3). He flew single-pilot from the right seat (p. 3). Airframe total time was 6,067.7 hours at the AAIP inspection of 23 February 2023; the emergency locator transmitter was installed and did not activate (pp. 3, 4).

**What they were doing**: Before departure the pilot worked out the highest obstacle on the route himself, 6,100 ft, with several 5,000 to 5,500 ft peaks along it; he also calculated that the flight would run into the end of evening civil twilight (p. 1). Both calculations were his, and the report contains no second route analysis.

After takeoff he set the autopilot at 5,500 ft and levelled off (p. 1). He then reached for his night vision goggles and found them on the co-pilot's seat, lying under an aircraft logbook. He moved the logbook to the pilot's door pocket, "where it normally was kept" in the report's words, and inside that same movement decided to check the logbook's flight hours against the maintenance hours due (p. 1).

Once the check was under way he noticed the cloud base descending, reset the autopilot to 5,000 ft, and then "put his head back down" into the logbook (p. 1). The flight nurse in the rear asked for an updated arrival time. He looked up and saw the helicopter heading for a tree-covered peak. He pulled aft on the cyclic to climb, the tail boom struck several trees, the vertical stabiliser separated from the tail boom, and he put the helicopter down on a road (p. 1).

Thirteen minutes passed between takeoff and ground contact. In those thirteen minutes the cockpit held one person, and no second pair of eyes was watching the relative position of the altitude, the cloud base and that peak.

A word on the nature of this material. The report runs five pages, its factual section cites no recorded data from the aircraft, and the entire sequence comes from the pilot's statement and the site examination (pp. 1, 5). Whether the helicopter carried recording equipment, and of which kinds, is not stated in the report. Every moment on this card, the level-off, the moving of the logbook, the altitude change, the look up, has a single source. That is not thin reporting. It is the standing condition of this class of investigation when there is no recorder and no second crew member: the sequence can only be retold by the person who lived it, and what he retells is exactly how his attention was allocated at the time.

**The decision point**: three moments, and not one of them left a document.

Before 18:44 on 9 March, in cockpit preparation, the cruise altitude was set at 5,500 ft. The grounds were his own route data: highest obstacle 6,100 ft, several peaks between 5,000 and 5,500 ft (p. 1). That single choice fixed every margin that followed, because 5,500 ft is level with the highest of those peaks, which leaves zero tolerance. The choice had no paper carrier of the kind a weight and balance sheet provides; the report holds only the pilot's statement.

After takeoff, in the stowage that preceded donning the goggles, the logbook was on the co-pilot's seat with the goggles on top of it, instead of in the pilot's door pocket (p. 1). He moved it to its usual place and then decided to check it there and then. No meeting, no approval, no second person.

After he saw the cloud base descending, he took the autopilot from 5,500 to 5,000 ft and went back head-down into the logbook (p. 1). This is the last reversible moment in the sequence. Changing altitude took a few actions; stopping the check would have taken a few actions. He took the first.

What all three share is the absence of any paper carrier. This is not the author's inference. The investigation was graded Class 4, the NTSB did not travel to the site (p. 5), and the report cites no company operations manual, no training record, no duty regulation. Most cards in this book can name a meeting date and a signatory at the decision point. This one cannot, because on that day the cockpit held one person.

There is a fourth moment of a different kind: the aft cyclic input after he looked up and saw the peak. It lasted on the order of a second, and the report gives it one sentence, that he pulled aft to climb and the tail boom struck several trees (p. 1). It does not belong to drift. It is the compensating action after drift. When a person finds himself at the wrong altitude and on the wrong heading, the action space left is a few seconds wide.

**What was visible then**: Before the flight on 9 March, what he had in hand was his own computed obstacle data, highest 6,100 ft with several peaks at 5,000 to 5,500 ft, and his own conclusion that the flight would meet the end of evening civil twilight (p. 1).

In flight, what was actually in the cockpit: the autopilot setting at 5,500 ft and later 5,000 ft; the night vision goggles on the co-pilot's seat; the logbook under the goggles; the pilot's door pocket, the logbook's usual place (p. 1). Outside the window, the cloud base, which he himself described as descending (p. 1). On the intercom, the rear medical crew, and the flight nurse's request for an arrival time, the only external input that pulled him out of the head-down state (p. 1).

What was learned afterwards: the distance between the helicopter and the tree-covered peak at the moment he looked up; the separation of the vertical stabiliser from the tail boom after the tail struck trees; the extent of airframe damage from the landing on the road (p. 1, pilot statement and site examination). The K1A5 weather station, 4 nautical miles from the accident site, recorded at 19:15, eighteen minutes after the accident, a cloud base of 5,000 ft, visibility 10 miles, temperature 12 Celsius, dew point 0 Celsius, no precipitation and no obscuration, at a station elevation of 2,020 ft (p. 4). That observation was not on his desk, and the report does not say he consulted that station before departure. It establishes something else: visibility that night was not the obstacle. The obstacle was terrain and a lowered head, present at the same time.

**What happened**: the tail boom struck trees and the vertical stabiliser separated, the helicopter landed on a road, three aboard were slightly injured and one was unhurt, and the airframe was substantially damaged (pp. 1, 4). The NTSB's probable cause, in the report's words, was the pilot's "improper decision to review the aircraft logbook in flight, which resulted in a controlled flight into terrain" (p. 2). The report carries five findings: aircraft control (pilot), mountainous terrain (decision related to conditions), task allocation (pilot), decision-making and judgement (pilot), and task monitoring and vigilance (pilot) (p. 2).

The three questions, looking back. The commonest answer to the first is "check the logbook after landing", and that action costs nothing: the logbook has its place in the door pocket and can be checked on the ground just as well. What the case spent instead was the whole margin of a 13-minute leg. The item most often missed under the second question is the number 5,000 ft. When the cloud base fell he changed altitude, and the route carried peaks of 5,000 to 5,500 ft; that information was in the pre-flight calculation, written by him. The third question lands in pre-flight preparation: check which tasks on this trip must be done in the air, and if you cannot name one, do not carry the object for it into the cockpit.

**Options ruled out**: the report records neither the alternatives the pilot considered nor any company rule on when the logbook is to be checked. The four items below are built from what was on his desk and from conditions in the report that make them unavailable.

Check the logbook after landing. Zero cost, since the pocket is where it normally lived (p. 1). The report records no rule either forbidding or requiring an in-flight check: at the level where documents exist, the act was neither prohibited nor required, only habitually scheduled.

Have a second person read the figures. This was a Part 135 single-pilot operation, the right seat was empty, and the goggles were lying on it (pp. 1, 3). The option did not exist on this leg.

Hold 5,500 ft and leave the altitude alone. The action the pilot described was a 500 ft descent (p. 1). The report records no consideration of routing around, climbing, or turning back; when the cloud base fell, he did not treat changing altitude and stopping the current task as one act.

Switch to instrument flight. The leg was on a company VFR plan with no clearance in Class G airspace (p. 4). IFR was not part of that day's mode of operation, and changing mode is itself an abort of the mission.

**Early signals**: no meeting record exists to cite, so the four signals below can only be described by how they were handled, not by what reason dismissed them. This is drift landing on a single-pilot post: there was no second person who needed persuading.

Signal one: the cruise altitude of 5,500 ft, level with peaks of 5,000 to 5,500 ft (p. 1). The signal sat on the pre-flight calculation sheet, and the handling was to fly it as computed.

Signal two: before flight he had already calculated that evening civil twilight would end during the flight (p. 1). Daylight disappearing inside the leg was his own result, and cabin paperwork was then scheduled into the same leg.

Signal three: the pre-flight stowage order had already changed. The logbook had left the door pocket and was lying on the co-pilot's seat, with the goggles on top (p. 1). The signal was an object in the wrong place, and the handling was to move the logbook back and start checking it on the spot.

Signal four: the descending cloud base was seen, and the handling was 500 ft down and the head down again (p. 1). The signal was processed into an altitude number. It was never processed into an action stop.

**The point of escalation**: in cockpit preparation before 18:44 on 9 March, the logbook was set down on the co-pilot's seat with the night vision goggles on top of it, and was later fetched for checking in flight (p. 1). The report does not record the day this practice began, nor how many times it had been executed before. What can be checked is that one day: it happened, no second person was present, and no one signed.

**Failure chain**: Technical layer. The autopilot held 5,000 ft; the route carried peaks of 5,000 to 5,500 ft; there was no visual reference while his head was down; at look-up the helicopter was close to a tree-covered peak; aft cyclic, tail boom striking trees, vertical stabiliser separating; a landing on a road and substantial damage (p. 1). Organizational layer. Part 135 single-pilot operation with no second pair of eyes in the cockpit; a company VFR plan with no clearance, so no outside opportunity to warn (pp. 3, 4); no company document in the report governing when the logbook is checked (pp. 1, 5). Incentive layer. En-route time was treated as time available for other work, a point the report does not support with any document and which can only be read from the sequence: he carried the paperwork aboard, and after seeing the cloud base fall he went back to it (p. 1). All three layers broke at once. The junction: the falling cloud base was a technical-layer signal, and the one channel that could translate it into "stop looking down" was another person in the cockpit, a channel that single-pilot operation removes by design.

**Minimum counterfactual**: put the logbook back in the pilot's door pocket before departure and check it after landing. One action, taken in cockpit preparation before 18:44, with no luck required: the pocket is where the logbook normally lived, and the report says so in those words (p. 1).

**Outcome and sources**: controlled flight into terrain at 18:57 on 9 March 2023; three people slightly injured, one unhurt, airframe substantially damaged; investigation graded Class 4, the NTSB did not attend the site, and the report was first issued on 6 June 2023 (pp. 1, 5). Primary source: NTSB, Aviation Investigation Final Report, ERA23LA147, 2023; link in Appendix B, av-020.

One layer in this case is worth naming. The act of a crew member going head-down into a logbook has a respectable name in aviation: task management, the business of putting en-route time to use. The material of drift is often exactly this kind of respectable practice wisdom. It makes sense, colleagues vouch for it, it has a record of efficiency, and its only problem is that it was never checked against another rule. Preventing drift does not mean abolishing practice wisdom. It means giving practice wisdom an interface for that check: every clever local practice deserves one question, which accident was the rule you bypassed written for. Answer it, and the practice at least knows what it is betting. Fail to answer it, and the practice is betting against a history nobody has read.

#### Case 2 (Maritime): The Watch Alarm the Music Covered

**Case ID**: ma-002.

**When and where**: about 02:38 on 14 October 2025, off Lochinver in Sutherland, Scotland, on the rocky shore at Rubha Rodha. The trawler left Stornoway at 21:30 on 13 October for the fishing grounds (MAIB Report 17/2026 p. 1, p. 2). At the time of the accident it was dark, with slight seas and moderate to good visibility (p. 8).

**What and who**: the prawn trawler Silver Cloud II (fishing number WK 80), wooden, built in 1972, registered length 15.58 m (overall length 16.55 m), gross tonnage 42, bought in 2024 by Silver Cloud Shellfish Ltd and normally based at Ullapool and Gairloch (pp. 2, 8). Three people were aboard: the skipper, the mate and one deckhand. The skipper and the mate each had about 25 years in fishing, and the deckhand had been aboard about 12 months; all held the certificates Seafish requires, and safety drills were held on board regularly (p. 2). The accident was investigated as a very serious marine casualty (p. 8).

**What they were doing**: On 10 October the vessel finished a repair period. The skipper helped on site and went home to rest around midnight (p. 2).

At 11:00 on 11 October the vessel sailed from Gairloch to start fishing. The skipper already had influenza-like symptoms at sailing, headache, sore throat, blocked nose and muscle aches, and was taking paracetamol; he judged himself fit to sail (p. 2). Fishing ran until the evening, the vessel entered Ullapool at 23:00, and he went to bed at midnight.

He rose at 03:30 on 12 October and sailed immediately. The mate took the first watch until 05:30, the skipper took over until shooting the gear at 06:30; hauling at 11:30, then fishing through the day; that evening the vessel steamed to an old submarine mooring buoy in Loch Ewe, arriving at 20:30, and he went to bed at 23:00.

At 04:15 on 13 October the vessel left the buoy for the grounds and shot at 06:45; hauling at 11:00 found the net badly damaged, and the decision was taken to go to Stornoway to land the catch and repair, berthing at 15:00. The skipper went to sleep while the mate and deckhand repaired the net, landed the catch and ate. He rose at 21:00, the crew decided to add a night tow to make up for the shot lost that day, and the vessel left port at 21:30 (p. 2).

The mate stood the first two hours in the wheelhouse, and at 23:30 the skipper took over and was left alone. He set a waypoint near the grounds, expected to arrive at 01:15, and engaged the autopilot (p. 2). Between 23:30 and 01:15 he fell asleep in the wheelhouse. The vessel missed the waypoint, held 092 degrees and continued toward the Scottish mainland, and at 02:38 it struck the rocks at Rubha Rodha (p. 2).

**The decision point**: three moments, none of them signed.

At 11:00 on 11 October, at the Gairloch quay before sailing, he sailed with symptoms. The report's words are that the skipper had influenza-like symptoms at sailing but judged himself fit to sail (p. 2). That judgement involved no medical examination, no review and no second signature. It locked the next three days of rota into a body already running at a discount.

At 21:00 on 13 October, aboard at Stornoway, the crew added a night tow. The background was the badly damaged net found at the 11:00 haul and a shot lost that day (p. 2). The crew made the decision jointly; the report's phrase is "the crew decided". The immediate consequence is written in Table 1: on 13 October, 16 hours 30 minutes of work, a longest unbroken work period of 15 hours, and a longest unbroken rest of 4 hours (p. 4).

At 23:30 on 13 October, in the wheelhouse, the mate handed over and the skipper kept the watch alone. At that point 19 hours 15 minutes had passed since he rose at 04:15 that day (p. 2; the interval follows from the report's narrative). Three people were aboard, the mate had just finished two hours on watch, and the deckhand had been aboard 12 months (p. 2). The decision at that moment was not to call anyone.

None of the three moments has a paper carrier, and the report is blunt about why. MSN 1884 (F) amendment 1 requires work and rest arrangements to be planned and recorded (p. 5), and "a complete record for the four days prior to the vessel's departure could not be obtained" (p. 6). No record means nothing to sign, and without anything to sign, all three decisions are absent from the paper.

**What was visible then**: Before sailing on 11 October: his own symptoms, four of them, being held down with paracetamol (p. 2); the schedule, a cycle of 10 days at sea and 4 to 5 ashore (p. 4).

Aboard on 13 October: three people available, of whom the mate had about 25 years and the deckhand about 12 months (p. 2); the fact that the net had been badly damaged that day (p. 2); the fact that a shot had been lost (p. 2); the vessel's equipment fit, a watch alarm mounted in the wheelhouse that armed when the autopilot was engaged and needed a button pressed every 4 minutes, with the volume rising if no one intervened for 1 minute after the first alarm, and no visual alarm or audible repeater in the crew accommodation (p. 4).

In the wheelhouse at 23:30 on 13 October: the waypoint and the expected arrival time, the autopilot, and the music that was playing. Also present was a division of labour already customary on that vessel: during trawling the skipper or mate would usually keep the wheelhouse for long stretches, while watches on passage were taken by the mate and the deckhand (p. 4). That night the passage watch fell to the skipper himself. The report records that music was often played on this vessel when hauling and often in the wheelhouse, and that music was playing in the wheelhouse on the night of the accident (p. 4).

What was learned afterwards: the skipper's total rest across three days (6, 6.5 and 7.5 hours) and his longest unbroken rest (6, 3.5 and 4 hours), in Table 1, which the MAIB reconstructed from interviews (p. 4); the missing records for the four days before departure (p. 6); and the judgement that the alarm was highly likely to have been masked by music in the wheelhouse, the report's phrase being highly likely (p. 6). One more item: the period when human alertness falls naturally runs from about 02:00 to 06:00 (p. 5), and the grounding at 02:38 falls inside that window (p. 6).

**What happened**: the vessel grounded at 02:38. The impact and the movement that followed woke the skipper and the crew; an inspection found the fish room flooding to about 1 m and still rising, and the skipper decided to abandon. The three donned life jackets, the deckhand stepped straight onto the rocks, and the mate entered the water on the starboard side and was pulled ashore by the deckhand. The skipper put the engine in neutral, activated the emergency position indicating radio beacon, sent a distress alert by digital selective calling, took the VHF radio, and entered the water on a line to be pulled ashore. At about 03:00 the RNLI lifeboat arrived and recovered all three (pp. 2, 3). There were no injuries; the hull was breached, flooded and partly submerged, then broke up on the rocks and was declared a constructive total loss (pp. 1, 8).

The MAIB made no new recommendations and issued no safety flyer, on the grounds that findings of this kind had recurred repeatedly in earlier investigations: Sustain (16 November 2023, off the entrance to Loch Broom, skipper asleep on watch) and Crystal Stream (6 June 2025) (pp. 5, 7).

The three questions, looking back. The commonest answer to the first is "fit a watch alarm". This vessel had one, and it was not a legal requirement (p. 6, original: although not a requirement on board Silver Cloud II, a watch alarm was fitted). It still did nothing, and the cost of that answer is turning a four-minute button press into an action that may or may not be taken. The item most often missed under the second question is the absence of a repeater in the crew accommodation. It reads like an equipment detail. In fact it decides that this alarm can only wake someone already in the wheelhouse, which makes it structurally useless against the watchkeeper himself falling asleep. The third question lands before 21:00 on 13 October: count the seven-day rest total, and cancel the night tow if it falls short.

**Options ruled out**: the report records no discussion of alternative arrangements among the crew. The five items below are built from conditions aboard at the time and from facts the report can verify.

Two-person watches. Three people were aboard, and the mate had already stood the first two hours before 23:30 (p. 2), so he was capable of taking over. Single-person watches are not prohibited at this vessel size, though the arrangement runs against MGN 313 (F) guidance on the risk of lone lookout (p. 6). The real cost of this option is the mate's rest, and the mate's rest that day was fragmented as well (p. 4).

Cancel the night tow. The direct cost is one shot of gear, which is exactly what the 21:00 decision set out to recover (p. 2). The report records no one proposing cancellation.

Turn the wheelhouse music off or down. The vessel had no ban on music; it was played routinely when hauling and in the wheelhouse, part of the work (p. 4). MGN 313 (F) lists distractions in the wheelhouse, music systems among them, as a contributory factor in collisions and groundings (p. 5). The guidance was written and the vessel's practice had not followed it: a verifiable fork between text and practice.

Have the deckhand keep him company or take the watch on passage. The third person was in his bunk at the time; the report records that the deckhand had been aboard about 12 months, was building experience, and did not stand trawl watches independently (pp. 2, 4). Passage watches were normally shared by the mate and the deckhand (p. 4), and that night they were not arranged that way.

Fit a repeater for the alarm in the accommodation. That is an equipment modification requiring the owner to spend money (p. 4 records that the vessel had no repeater). The report records no discussion of it.

**Early signals**: five, each with the account available at the time.

Signal one: sailing with symptoms at 11:00 on 11 October (p. 2). The account available: the symptoms were influenza-like, held down with paracetamol, and he judged himself fit to sail.

Signal two: three days of rest at 6, 6.5 and 7.5 hours, with longest unbroken rest of 6, 3.5 and 4 hours (p. 4, Table 1). The rules require no less than 10 hours in any 24 and no less than 77 hours in any 7 days, with daily rest split into no more than two periods, one of them at least 6 hours (p. 5). Rest on 12 and 13 October was split into three periods each, with longest periods of 3.5 and 4 hours, so both conditions failed at once. The account available: MSN 1884 carries its own exception, allowing an authorised departure for operational reasons provided safety is not harmed (p. 5). That exception had become the default channel.

Signal three: 16.5 hours of work on 13 October, with a longest unbroken stretch of 15 hours (p. 4). The account available: the net broke during the day, a shot was lost, and it had to be made up.

Signal four: the alarm sounded only in the wheelhouse, with no repeater in the accommodation (p. 4). The account available: the equipment was not a legal requirement, and having it at all was a bonus (p. 6).

Signal five: music and alarm sharing one space (p. 4). The account available: music was the normal background of the work, played when hauling and in the wheelhouse.

**The point of escalation**: two consecutive actions between 21:00 and 23:30 on 13 October. The crew decided on a night tow and the vessel left at 21:30; two hours later the mate handed over and the skipper stayed alone (p. 2). Once the vessel had left, it had no physical means of relieving the watchkeeper before the 01:15 waypoint, and two of the three remaining people were in their bunks. Nothing was signed at this step and nothing was recorded: the work and rest arrangements for that day were never written down (p. 6).

One time gap deserves recording separately. The vessel missed the waypoint at 01:15 and grounded at 02:38, 83 minutes later (p. 2). On the alarm's setting, with the autopilot engaged, a button press was required every 4 minutes, the alarm sounding if it was not pressed and rising in volume after 1 minute without intervention (p. 4). Eighty-three minutes is room for more than twenty alarms. The report does not record whether the alarm sounded that night. It records a conclusion: the crew did not know the skipper had lost the ability to keep watch until the vessel struck (p. 6).

**Failure chain**: Technical layer. Grounding at 02:38, hull breach, fish room flooding to about 1 m, subsequent break-up (pp. 1, 2). Organizational layer. A single watchkeeper and no structured rota (p. 4, original: an informal watchkeeping arrangement and had no structured rota); no work and rest records, with the four days before departure missing (p. 6); an alarm with no repeater in the accommodation, designed on the assumption that someone in the wheelhouse could hear it (pp. 4, 6). Incentive layer. Catch and trip rhythm: a shot was lost to net damage on 13 October and a tow was added that night to recover it (p. 2); a cycle of 10 days at sea and 4 to 5 ashore, landing catch every two days (p. 4). All three layers broke at once. The junction: the one channel that could carry the fact of the skipper asleep to the accommodation was the alarm, and the alarm was designed to sound only in the wheelhouse, where it was highly likely to be masked by music that night (p. 6). The channel broke at the level of equipment fit and volume, and that level sits on nobody's checklist: the alarm was not broken and the music was not a violation.

**Minimum counterfactual**: before deciding on the night tow at 21:00 on 13 October, check the rest total against 77 hours in 7 days, and cancel the tow if it falls short. One action, taken two hours before departure, with no luck required: the three days' figures in Table 1 were already in his own routine, and the floor set by the rules is written in MSN 1884 (p. 5).

**Outcome and sources**: all three rescued, unhurt; the hull breached, flooded, broken up and declared a constructive total loss; the MAIB made no recommendations and issued no safety flyer (pp. 1, 7, 8). Primary source: MAIB, Report 17/2026, Silver Cloud II, August 2026; link in Appendix B, ma-002.

There is a quieter layer in Case 2: the fact that the alarm really was fitted to that vessel. The equipment was real, the function was real, the defence line existed, even though no requirement demanded it (MAIB Report 17/2026 p. 6). What drift does is not dismantle defences. It makes them mismatched. The alarm was kept as its design scenario specifies, a quiet wheelhouse, while the wheelhouse's real scenario, music, had long since stopped being the design scenario. That sentence generalizes into a test question for drift: is this defence line's design scenario still the same scenario as the site today. The difference between the two is the part drift has eaten, and that part appears on no defect list, because the alarm was not broken and the music was not a violation.

#### Case 3 (Healthcare): The Checklist That Drifted (condensed)

me-001. An NHS orthopaedic treatment centre in the United Kingdom. HSIB investigation report (Administering a wrong site nerve block, 2018; the archived snapshot was read and verified for this book): the nerve block for a 69-year-old patient's right ankle operation was injected into the left leg, and the patient was not harmed. The Stop Before You Block checklist was in use and was being filled in, but the way it was executed had evolved from pausing at each item and confirming aloud in both directions to ticking items off as you went. National figure: about 192 wrong-site operations a year. The decision point sits at the moment the checklist was simplified from an item-by-item pause and check to a running tick. Minimum counterfactual: execute the checklist as designed, with a pause and a cross-check at every item. Primary source: HSIB, 2018; link in Appendix B, me-001 (archive link, grade A−).

This condensed case holds the sharpest phenomenon in the chapter. After drift, the checklist still produces complete compliance records. The ticks are real, since every form was signed. The check did not happen, and that is real too. A paper record proves that the form was filled in; it cannot prove that the check took place. The difference between the two is the measure of the pause that got eaten. Device 2, which registers pause-type actions on a separate list, is aimed at exactly that difference: a checklist protects the pauses between the ticks, not the ticks on the paper.

#### Case 4 (Construction): The Safety Space Filled with Cable (condensed)

ce-003. Ickenham station, London Underground. RAIB report (Report 02/2026; the pages were read and verified for this book): a passenger fell onto the track and was struck and killed by a train. The recess beneath the platform edge is the provision that gives a person who falls a space to get into; the investigation found the recess filled with communications cable, so the refuge was too small. Each cable installation permit was one small occupation, and no occupation was ever approved under the heading of safety space. The decision point sits at one of the sign-offs that approved cable being run under the platform. Minimum counterfactual: keep a register of protected space, and route every occupation through a separate approval. Primary source: RAIB, Report 02/2026, 2026; link in Appendix B, ce-003.

One sentence in Case 4 deserves taking apart: no occupation was ever approved under the heading of safety space. Every occupation went through approval, with engineering permits and works permits complete. What was missing was any approver being required to answer how much refuge space would remain after this occupation. The absence of that heading made each occupation compliant at every level of approval, while the accumulated result was the loss of the safety function in full. Device 3's register turns space into a quantity with a number, the remaining clear width, updated at every occupation, so that the heading finally has an account behind it. This is a general rule for drift defences: drift happens in domains that have no dimension, and the first step of the defence is to give that domain a quantity.

#### Case 5 (Aviation): The Full Rudder the Training Taught

**Case ID**: av-033.

**When and where**: 09:16:15 Eastern Standard Time on 12 November 2001, over the residential area of Belle Harbor, Queens, New York, United States. American Airlines Flight 587 began its takeoff roll at 09:13:51 and became airborne at 09:14:29, departing runway 31L at Kennedy airport (NTSB AAR-04/04 p. 1, p. 3).

**What and who**: American Airlines Flight 587, an Airbus A300-605R (N14053), operating under 14 CFR Part 121 on instruments, the first leg of the crew's one-day round trip (p. 1). Aboard were 2 flight crew, 7 cabin crew and 251 passengers, 5 of them under the age of two on laps (p. 1). On the training side the subject is the American Airlines Flight Training Academy in Fort Worth, Texas, and its Advanced Aircraft Maneuvering Program (AAMP), the upset recovery module within the company's selected event training, taught in initial, recurrent, transition and upgrade training (pp. 79, 80). Under the company's General Flight Training Manual, edition of 1 September 2001, maneuver training was delivered by professional simulator instructors who did not hold airline pilot qualifications (p. 79).

**What they were doing**: AAMP was developed from 1996 onward, after a review of worldwide large multi-engine transport accidents between 1987 and 1996 identified loss of control as the leading cause (p. 80). The course took shape in 1997: 6.5 hours of ground school and six simulator items, one of which was an uncommanded roll to at least 90 degrees of bank (pp. 81, 82).

How that item was run is set out in the report. The instructor pressed a touchscreen button and the simulator first rolled about 10 degrees one way, then rolled past 90 degrees the other way; before that, the simulator suppressed aileron, spoiler and rudder response, and kept suppressing them until bank reached 50 degrees or a full 10 seconds had passed; after 50 degrees, yaw and roll control were restored progressively within 1.3 seconds (p. 83). During the item the instructor told the pilots that a heavy aircraft had just passed ahead, some instructors naming a Boeing 747, and issued a wake turbulence warning (p. 82). Trainees were told to recover the way ground school had taught them.

The first officer's training record: AAMP ground school in March 1997, his first AAMP simulator session in November 1997, then recurrent sessions in November 1998, November 1999 and December 2000, with the next scheduled for late November 2001 (pp. 81, 82). The captain attended ground school in May 1997 and his first simulator session in December 1997 (pp. 81, 82). The course had run in full on the first officer for four years, and he passed every cycle.

**The decision point**: three irreversible moments. The first two carry signatures. The third is only an action.

On 27 July 1995 the airline's managing director of flight training and standards wrote to the Principal Operations Inspector (POI) requesting approval of a training manual revision, on the grounds of "recent NTSB concerns and recommendations", noting that the company was "proactively" running this training in simulator periods, and listing maneuvers that included upset recovery from more than 90 degrees of bank and pitch attitudes beyond 35 degrees (p. 80). On 1 August 1995 the POI approved the manual revision (p. 80). This is the only moment in the case signed by both the company and the regulator, and it is where the drift begins: the training scope was expanded voluntarily by the company, and the expanded part carried no statement of its structural boundary.

On 6 October 1997 the company's chief pilot and vice president of flight replied to a joint letter from the Boeing Commercial Airplane Group, Boeing Douglas Products Division, Airbus and the FAA (p. 88). That joint letter had gone out on 20 August 1997, observing that "overemphasis on rudder's superior roll authority relative to aileron and spoiler at high angle of attack is a concern", and recommending full aileron first and rudder only after no response (p. 87). The reply refused that order, on the grounds that the company did "not advocate rudder first or rudder only", and stating that if pitch rate after unload was insufficient, company pilots "would not hesitate to roll the lift vector off the vertical axis" (p. 88). Airbus later told the NTSB that it did not receive the reply until 20 January 1998 (p. 88, note 143). The signatory was the chief pilot and vice president of flight.

The third moment is the seven seconds from 09:15:51 on 12 November 2001. The first wake encounter came at 09:15:36, and the first officer answered it with wheel inputs of 37 degrees right and 34 degrees left, while rudder pedal travel stayed on the order of 0.1 inch, inside the authority of the yaw damper (pp. 3, 4). The second encounter came at 09:15:51, with the wheel at 64 degrees right and the pedal at 1.7 inches right; between then and 09:15:58.5 the pedal alternated through 1.7 inches right, 1.7 left, 1.7 right, 2.0 right, 2.4 left and 1.3 right (pp. 4, 5). At 09:15:58.4 the right rear main attachment fitting of the vertical stabiliser failed and the whole fin came off, at an airspeed of about 251 knots (p. 4).

The third moment carries no signature and needed none. The first officer was executing a sequence he had practised and passed for four years.

**What was visible then**: On the course side, what was in hand, in date order.

The AAMP training workbook, edition of 1 January 1997, one copy per pilot. It covered wake turbulence response, and one line read "High AOA maneuvering = RUDDER"; another passage read that the rudder's effectiveness as a roll control increases with angle of attack, and that at high angle of attack the rudder becomes the most effective roll control (pp. 84, 85). The workbook was revised later, and the edition in use at the time of the accident was dated 1 May 2000 (p. 84).

The company's own A300 operating manual, Flight Controls page 8, edition of 8 September 1999: two independent rudder travel limiting systems, reducing maximum rudder deflection from plus or minus 30 degrees below 165 knots, in steps, to plus or minus 3.5 degrees above 310 knots (p. 93). Two years before the accident, that manual already said this rudder had very little travel left at high speed.

Also on the desk was a body of external and internal feedback, every item dated. On 22 May 1997 the company's A300 technical pilot wrote to the Airbus chief test pilot, noting that the handout described the rudder as the primary roll control at high angle of attack, and that instructors were already teaching pilots to use rudder to control roll in wake encounters (p. 87); on 23 May the Airbus chief test pilot faxed back agreeing to a telephone conference (p. 87). On 27 May an internal memorandum from the general manager of flight operations technical to the chief pilot and vice president of flight recorded "serious concern" about "certain flawed aerodynamic theory and flying techniques" in AAMP, called the teaching of rudder as the primary means of roll control in high angle of attack recovery "extremely dangerous", and asked for "immediate corrective action" (pp. 88, 89). On 13 June an internal note from the Airbus chief test pilot observed that the simulator was "particularly inaccurate at large sideslip angles", so pilots might draw the wrong conclusions from it (p. 87). On 20 August the four-party joint letter went out (p. 87).

Public statistics were available at the time. The NTSB searched the ASRS database afterwards, and among reports from 1988 to 1999 in which large transport aircraft encountered wake turbulence as the trailing aircraft, the maximum bank pilots estimated was usually within 30 degrees and never above 60 degrees (p. 83). The data sat in the database throughout; the search was done after the fact.

What was learned afterwards: the load at the moment the vertical stabiliser failed was about twice the certified limit load and above the certified ultimate load (p. 159); on the real aircraft at 240 knots pedal travel should have been limited to 7.9 degrees, while full rudder input in the simulator measured 10.3 to 18.9 degrees, and some trainees said they "could not feel the rudder stop" (p. 90); and the first officer's two wake responses on the 727 in 1997, which come from interviews with pilots who flew with him (pp. 137, 138).

**What happened**: at 09:15:58.4 the right rear main attachment fitting of the vertical stabiliser failed and the whole fin separated; both engines then separated from the airframe, and the aircraft fell into the Belle Harbor residential area, killing all 260 people aboard and 5 on the ground (pp. 1, 4). The NTSB's probable cause was the in-flight separation of the vertical stabiliser, the loads on it having exceeded ultimate design values, caused by the first officer's "unnecessary and excessive" rudder pedal inputs, with those inputs contributed to by characteristics of the A300-600 rudder system design and by elements of the American Airlines Advanced Aircraft Maneuvering Program (p. 160). Two board members dissented from changes made to the ordering of the contributing factors; their dissent is printed separately after the main text, and one line of it reads that the investigators could find no instance of abnormal rudder use by this first officer before his AAMP training, and that when a captain asked him about an earlier event, he "insisted that AAMP instructed him to use the rudder pedals that way" (p. 165).

The three questions, looking back. The commonest answer to the first is "revise the course", and revision presumes that someone knows there is a gap between what the course does and what the structure can take. That knowledge was scattered, before the accident, across four dated letters and memoranda, and not one of them entered a course approval form. The item most often missed under the second question is page 8 of the company operating manual, Flight Controls: the line that runs from plus or minus 30 degrees down to plus or minus 3.5 degrees. It reads like a system description. It is in fact the entire travel of that rudder at high speed, and the pilots who had been trained did not know the line existed (p. 159, finding 10). The third question lands at the manual approval of 1 August 1995: had the approval form carried one line asking what structural boundary corresponds to the full rudder input this course teaches, two departments would have had to meet.

**Options ruled out**: four, each of them on the desk at the time, and each with an outcome the report can verify.

Change the order of actions as the joint letter recommended, full aileron first and rudder only after no response. The reply of 6 October 1997 refused (p. 88). The refusal argued the point item by item and changed wording in the text; it did not change the simulator item.

Cancel the 90-degree bank item and replace it with a low-fidelity demonstration. The NTSB wrote the feasibility of this path into the report: without a motion platform or with a simple screen display, the exercise might be better suited to awareness training and less likely to introduce wrong information (p. 160, finding 18). It was not done at the time. After the accident the company changed the item: freeze the simulator first, brief, then hand control back, and stop describing the setup as following a heavy aircraft (p. 83).

Model the real aircraft's pedal travel in the simulator. The simulator's elastic cable stiffness was calculated mathematically rather than built from real cable, and that is where the difference comes from (p. 90, note 146); static measurements on the real aircraft were not made until 31 January to 2 February 2002, in Tulsa (p. 91). It was not done at the time, and nobody proposed doing it.

Have the structures and airworthiness side co-sign the training course. On the certification side there was one POI approval (p. 80), and the training instructors did not have to be airline pilots (p. 79). This path had no institutional entry point at the time.

**Early signals**: five, each with a date, and each answered with a document.

On 22 May 1997 the company's own A300 technical pilot wrote to the Airbus chief test pilot that the handout made rudder the primary roll control at high angle of attack and that instructors were already teaching it that way (p. 87). The answer was the fax of 23 May agreeing to a meeting.

On 27 May 1997 the internal memorandum from the general manager of flight operations technical called part of the aerodynamic theory "flawed", called teaching pilots to use rudder as the primary roll control "extremely dangerous", and demanded immediate correction (pp. 88, 89). The report records no course change following that memorandum; the next verifiable textual change is the workbook rewording of 1 September 1997 (p. 84, note 138).

On 20 August 1997 the joint letter from Boeing, Boeing Douglas, Airbus and the FAA went straight at the "overemphasis on the superiority of rudder" (p. 87). The answer was the reply of 6 October, which rejected it point by point (p. 88).

On a 727 flight in 1997 a captain watched this first officer, levelling off between 1,000 and 1,500 ft, answer a momentary wake encounter with repeated full-rudder alternating inputs; the captain said he had never seen any pilot react that way (p. 137). He spoke to the first officer afterwards, and the first officer said AAMP had taught him to use the rudder that way; the captain advised him to use less of it, and did not report it as a course problem (p. 138 and note 181). On the next flight together, the first officer still went to the rudder quickly when he met wake (p. 138).

Signal five: Airbus says it did not receive the reply of 6 October 1997 until 20 January 1998 (p. 88, note 143). The last of the four external opinions arrived three and a half months after it was sent.

**The point of escalation**: on 1 September 1997 the AAMP workbook was revised, deleting the word "maneuver" and adding "coordinated" before "rudder" (p. 84, note 138). The report records that the change answered the manufacturer's concern about the company's "emphasis on the use of rudder". The change happened at the level of wording only: the simulator's 90-degree item stayed as it was, the instructor's "following a heavy aircraft" setup stayed as it was, the 10-second suppression and 1.3-second restoration stayed as they were (p. 83). Once the text was fixed, the objections lost their target: the company's reply could say it did "not advocate rudder first or rudder only" (p. 88), because the workbook no longer said so. Nobody signed this step; the report records only the revision date and the content of the change.

**Failure chain**: Technical layer. After the second wake encounter the pedals swung through six large alternating inputs in seven seconds (p. 5); at 09:15:58.4 the right rear main attachment fitting failed under a load about twice the certified limit load and above the certified ultimate load (p. 159); the fin separated and the aircraft went out of control. Organizational layer. Ground school taught pilots to use rudder to help roll (p. 159, finding 8); the 90-degree item produced bank angles beyond anything in the real wake statistics, so trainees linked wake encounters to the need for a violent recovery (p. 83; p. 159, finding 9); pilots were not adequately trained before the accident on the effects of pedal input at high speed or on how the travel limiter works (p. 159, finding 10); the simulator's pedal travel did not match the aircraft (p. 90). Incentive layer. The course was the company's safety calling card, certification rested on a single POI approval (p. 80), training instructors did not have to be airline pilots (p. 79), and the training system and the airworthiness system each ran on its own line. All three layers broke at once. The junction broke in the cell marked text changed, action unchanged: the rewording of 1 September 1997 brought the text into line with the external objections, while the simulator item and the instructor's spoken setup did not move; four years later the first officer executed an action, not a text.

**Minimum counterfactual**: in the second half of 1997, calibrate the simulator's pedal travel against one static measurement on the real aircraft, with the aircraft parked and actual pedal travel recorded across airspeed bands, then re-run the course. One action, taken four years before the accident, with no luck required: the NTSB performed that measurement in January 2002, at American's Tulsa maintenance base, on an A300-600 (p. 91).

**Outcome and sources**: 265 people killed, 260 aboard and 5 on the ground; the NTSB issued eight new recommendations, A-04-56 through A-04-63, in addition to A-02-01, issued earlier on this accident (pp. 161, 162). Primary source: NTSB, Aircraft Accident Report AAR-04/04, In-Flight Separation of Vertical Stabilizer, American Airlines Flight 587, 2004; link in Appendix B, av-033.

#### Case 6 (Maritime): A Watch Rota with No Ledger

**Case ID**: ma-003.

**When and where**: about 00:06 on 6 June 2025, at Barmore Island in Loch Fyne, Argyll and Bute, Scotland. The trawler left Tarbert at 03:30 on 5 June, the fourth and last fishing day of that week (MAIB Report 11/2026 p. 1, p. 2).

**What and who**: the prawn trawler Crystal Stream (fishing number LH 147), wooden, registered length 16.43 m, overall length 17.78 m, gross tonnage 112.34, under the UK flag, owned by M & I MacKinnon Ltd, bought in 2023, based at Tarbert, fishing for prawns in Loch Fyne and the Clyde with the catch going to the owner's transport business (pp. 4, 12). Four people were aboard: the skipper, the mate and two deckhands, one of whom was clearly unwell that day (p. 2). The skipper held a near-coastal fishing certificate issued in 1998, and all hands had completed Seafish's one-day course in sea survival, firefighting, first aid and safety awareness (p. 5).

**What they were doing**: The vessel left at 03:30 on 5 June. The skipper steered south, handed the watch to the mate at 03:50 and went below to sleep (p. 2).

The vessel's routine ran Monday to Friday, leaving in the early hours and returning each night or every other night depending on the catch; weekends were nominally for rest, though the skipper and crew sometimes used them for maintenance (p. 5). Before this departure the vessel had fished prawns three days running (p. 5).

At 05:00 a drop in engine revolutions woke him, the vessel being on the grounds. The first tow went out at 05:30 and was hauled at 10:30, with the second shot immediately after. He stayed in the wheelhouse through this stretch with the windows open, drinking coffee often (p. 2). At 11:45 a coaster passed ahead of the vessel, he took avoiding action, and then went back to trawling. At 18:30 the fourth and last tow of the day began, and he set a north-west course for Tarbert (p. 2).

At 19:30 the whole crew ate a heavy dinner near the Inchmarnock channel. At 22:00, with the vessel passing Inchmarnock Island, the first deckhand offered to take the watch, and he refused (p. 2). At 23:15 the last tow was finished and the gear recovered. The crew sorted catch on deck while he stayed in the wheelhouse and brought the speed up to about 7 knots (p. 2). From that moment until the vessel reached the water off Tarbert, he was asleep at the helm (p. 2).

At 23:45 the first deckhand saw the vessel passing Tarbert and took it that the skipper had decided to put in another tow in Loch Fyne before returning to port. He carried on sorting catch (p. 2). Shortly after midnight the vessel struck a submerged obstruction, the impact threw the first deckhand across the sorting table, the others lost their footing, and the skipper was woken by the blow (p. 2).

**The decision point**: three moments. The first two have documents. The third has none.

On 12 September 2023 a Maritime and Coastguard Agency (MCA) surveyor boarded the vessel after its change of owner, and the survey report set out three requirements: that the safety folder and risk assessments be reviewed and updated; that evidence be provided that the crew had completed the required safety training and held the appropriate qualifications and medical certificates; and that new crew induction records and rest time records be established (pp. 4, 5). The owner and skipper submitted partial close-out evidence by email and submitted nothing specific on the crew's medical certificates. On 25 September 2023 the vessel was issued a UK Fishing Vessel Certificate (UKFVC) (p. 5). The signatory was the MCA.

The renewal survey of 14 May 2024 listed seven deficiencies, one of them that two crew members held no medical certificates; the report records that "no documentation was available to confirm that the crew held valid medical certificates to close the deficiency" (p. 5). On 21 June 2024 a new UKFVC was issued, valid until 25 June 2029 (p. 5). The signatory was the MCA. Thirty-eight days separate the two documents, and the report records no supplementary check in those thirty-eight days.

At 22:00 on 5 June 2025, in the wheelhouse, the deckhand offered to take over and the skipper refused (p. 2). This was the last moment at which the watch could have been handed over, and the only decision in the case he made on the spot, with no second person reviewing it. It has no document; the report gives one sentence of narrative and records no reason.

Put the three moments in a row and the shape of the gap appears. The rules require rest time to be planned and recorded (p. 7). The survey of September 2023 already stated that rest records had to be established. By June 2025 the vessel still showed "no evidence of any system for managing crew rest time" (p. 7). Between the two sits a renewal survey and a certificate valid to 2029.

**What was visible then**: Before departure: three consecutive days of prawn fishing that week; his own sense of fatigue (p. 5); a catch that had begun to worsen two days before the accident, and operating difficulties on board that were putting him under stress (p. 5). The rota held only himself: the report's wording is that the skipper "was managing his own rest" (p. 9), and no second person on board was counting the hours.

On that day he handed over at 03:50, was woken by the change in engine revolutions at 05:00, and between then and 23:15 his sleep amounted to that one period of 1 hour 10 minutes (p. 2).

Through the daylight of 5 June: one deckhand was clearly unwell (p. 2); and his own condition, handled by keeping the windows open, drinking coffee often and getting up to move about, all three of which were his own response to his state. The report notes that the open windows and the moving about match the advice in MGN 313 (F) on maintaining alertness, and that coffee is not on that list; it then records that these measures "were not sufficient to offset the effects of accumulated fatigue" (p. 9).

At 22:00 on 5 June: the person available, since the first deckhand offered (p. 2); the custom of the vessel, the report recording that he "was satisfied with the crew's ability to keep a navigational watch, but preferred to steer himself during fishing operations and when entering or leaving port" (p. 5); the instruments, an autopilot that followed a magnetic compass heading, had no link to the plotter and could not follow a preset track (p. 7), and a plotter he "generally did not use" (p. 7); and the watch alarm in the wheelhouse, which was fitted but had no interface with the autopilot and was not working at the time (p. 4).

What was learned afterwards: rest within 24 hours of "slightly more than 4 hours", taken in two periods; rest across six days "just over 24 hours" (p. 9, Figure 3 reconstructed by the MAIB from interviews); a regulatory floor of 10 hours in any 24 and 77 hours in any 7 days (p. 7); and the refusal of the deckhand's offer, which the MAIB wrote up directly as "a missed opportunity to reduce sleep debt" (p. 9).

**What happened**: the hull struck the submerged obstruction and breached, and the fish room flooded fast. The crew rigged the vessel's emergency pump and tried to keep up with the water; at 00:09 the skipper reported the impact to the coastguard on VHF; he then deliberately put the vessel ashore on rocks on the south-west side of Barmore Island. The liferaft inflated upside down and took time to right; the second deckhand was too weak to evacuate and was helped into the raft; the crew did not know the raft's equipment well and could not find the knife for the painter, so the skipper went back aboard to cast it off. At 00:49 the four reached the shore. At 01:16 the RNLI inshore lifeboat from Tighnabruaich arrived and took them off (pp. 2, 3). Injuries were minor and pollution was slight; the vessel took a list, partly submerged, was declared a constructive total loss and was later broken up and removed (pp. 1, 12).

The MAIB made one recommendation to the owner (2026/136): establish a safety management system under the Fishing Safety Management Code, establish watchkeeping procedures under MGN 313 (F) and the Fishermen's Safety Guide, and establish a fleet-wide fatigue management system (p. 11). The owner had watch alarms fitted across its vessels (p. 11), and the MAIB issued a safety flyer to the fishing industry on this case (p. 11).

The three questions, looking back. The commonest answer to the first is "fit a watch alarm". This vessel had one, with no interface to the autopilot, and it was not working (p. 4); what the owner fitted fleet-wide after the accident was the same thing, the same generation of equipment. The item most often missed under the second question is one line in the survey report of 12 September 2023, the requirement to establish rest time records. It reads like a paperwork requirement. It is in fact the only action in that system that ever counts hours; without it, rest is nothing but the person's own sense of it. The third question lands at the certificate issued on 21 June 2024: the survey listed seven deficiencies, one of them had no close-out evidence, and the certificate was issued anyway, valid for five years.

**Options ruled out**: four.

Accept the deckhand's offer at 22:00. The first deckhand raised it himself (p. 2), and he was capable of keeping watch, the report recording that the skipper "was satisfied with the crew's ability to keep a navigational watch" (p. 5). The cost is the deckhand's own rest that day, and one pair of hands fewer on the sorting table.

Cut the day's work short or stop early. The catch had been worsening for two days before the accident (p. 5), and the fourth day was the last fishing day of the week; stopping early costs that week's income directly.

Assess the risk of lone lookout in the wheelhouse and adjust on the basis of the assessment. MGN 137 (M+F) strongly recommends that a vessel in darkness not rely on the navigational watchkeeper as the sole lookout (p. 8); no such assessment had been done on board, and the report states plainly that "the risks of single-handed watchkeeping were not identified in the vessel's risk assessment" (p. 1).

Connect the watch alarm to the autopilot. The alarm had no interface with it (p. 4); the modification is equipment work, and the owner fitted alarms fleet-wide only after the accident (p. 11).

**Early signals**: five.

Signal one: the survey report of 12 September 2023, requiring the risk assessment to be reviewed and updated, evidence of training and medical certificates, and induction and rest time records to be established (pp. 4, 5). Handling: partial evidence by email, and the certificate issued on 25 September as before.

Signal two: the seven deficiencies at the renewal survey of 14 May 2024, including two crew without medical certificates (p. 5). Handling: no close-out evidence, a new certificate issued on 21 June 2024, valid until 2029.

Signal three: the risk assessments in the safety folder. The online copy held one, covering boarding and disembarking; the one filed at survey covered a person overboard and had last been reviewed on 30 March 2022 (p. 7). Handling: at the 2024 survey the surveyor reminded the owner to review risk assessments regularly, and nothing changed after the reminder (p. 10).

Signal four: six-day rest of "just over 24 hours", and "slightly more than 4 hours" in 24 (p. 9). The account available is written in the report: the vessel had "no system for managing rest time", and rest was arranged by the skipper himself (pp. 7, 9).

Signal five: on 5 June one deckhand was clearly unwell and later unable to evacuate unaided (p. 2). Handling: sailed as normal, worked the fourth day as normal.

**The point of escalation**: two consecutive actions between 22:00 and 23:15 on 5 June 2025, the refusal of the offer and then bringing the speed up to about 7 knots alone (p. 2). After 23:15 three people were on deck sorting catch and he was alone in the wheelhouse; the alarm had no connection to the autopilot (p. 4); the autopilot would hold only a magnetic heading and would not follow a track (p. 7); and he did not use the plotter (p. 7). Nothing was signed at this step and nothing was recorded: the work and rest arrangements for that day were never written down (p. 7).

**Failure chain**: Technical layer. At 00:06 the hull struck a submerged obstruction, the fish room flooded, and the vessel listed and partly submerged after going aground (pp. 1, 2). Organizational layer. A single watchkeeper with no risk assessment (pp. 1, 9); no written safety management system, with one safety folder aboard and one maintained online by an external provider, the online copy holding a single risk assessment, on boarding and disembarking (p. 7); no rest time management system and no records (p. 7); a watch alarm with no interface to the autopilot, not working at the time (p. 4); crew without valid medical certificates (p. 5); no induction procedure and no regular drills, with the liferaft handled inexpertly (pp. 7, 10). Incentive layer. A worsening catch that week, a fourth and final fishing day, and a catch delivered straight into the owner's transport business (pp. 4, 5). All three layers broke at once. The junction: fatigue was known to the man himself (p. 9 states that the risk "was not identified by those responsible for implementing the requirements", with operational demand overriding the rest requirement), and two entry points could have turned it into "not on watch tonight". Handover was one, and the deckhand offered it at 22:00 and was refused. Recording was the other, and that entry point had never been built.

**Minimum counterfactual**: accept the deckhand's offer to take the watch at 22:00 on 5 June 2025. One action, taken two hours before the accident, with no luck required: the man was already in the wheelhouse, and the words only had to be said (p. 2).

**Outcome and sources**: all four survived, with minor injuries and slight pollution; the vessel was a constructive total loss and was broken up and removed; the MAIB made one recommendation to the owner, who had watch alarms fitted across the fleet (pp. 1, 11, 12). Primary source: MAIB, Report 11/2026, Crystal Stream, May 2026; link in Appendix B, ma-003.

Cases 5 and 6 sit side by side at the end of this section to complete the two far ends of drift. Case 5 shows drift growing in the seam between training and design, the drift furthest from the line. Case 6 shows drift growing in the seam between scheduling and the body, the drift furthest from paperwork. Put those two ends together with the intermediate forms in Cases 1 and 2, and the distribution map is complete: drift lives in no department. It lives in the seam between each department and the rule.

#### Case 7 (Nuclear): Two Tagging Events at One Plant in a Fortnight (condensed)

nu-001. Unit 3 at the Gravelines nuclear power station, France. ASNR event notification (October 2026; the notification was read and verified for this book): during an outage an electrical tagging error left a standby emergency diesel generator de-energised for longer than the 24-hour ceiling allowed by the operating rules, and the event was rated INES 1; within two weeks a second tagging-type event occurred at the same plant (nu-002, equipment unavailable for 7 hours 45 minutes). An outage is the period when procedure density at a nuclear plant is at its highest, and tagging and state restoration change hands hundreds of times: every handover is an action carried by convention, the 24-hour ceiling is written in the operating rules, and the fork in execution is written in habit. Two weeks separated the events, and the second was executed no differently from the first. Minimum counterfactual: build an item-by-item handover sheet for outage electrical tagging and state restoration, with mandatory double verification. Primary source: ASNR, 2026; link in Appendix B, nu-001.

#### Case 8 (Maritime): A Deck with No Separation (condensed)

ma-011. The fishing vessel Weston Bay (GY123). MAIB Report 9/2026 (pages read and verified for this book): two men went overboard while crab pots were being deployed, and one died. The investigation found no physical separation between people and gear, no man-overboard risk assessment, no man-overboard drill, and rescue equipment aboard that was unsuitable for recovering an unconscious person. Work on a fishing deck is carried by generations of experience: how the pots are stacked, where a man stands, how the ropes are flaked, all of it governed by conventions that work, except that the possibility of a person being taken overboard by pots and ropes had never entered any assessment as a risk. Minimum counterfactual: run a man-overboard risk assessment before the operation, and configure drills and rescue equipment to what it finds. Primary source: MAIB, Report 9/2026, 2026; link in Appendix B, ma-011.

#### Case 9 (Finance): After Changing Audit Firm (condensed)

fi-009. Zhong Shen Ya Tai Accounting Firm. Administrative penalty decision of the China Securities Regulatory Commission (No. 54 of 2026; the original text was read and verified for this book): the firm failed to exercise due diligence in auditing the annual report of the same entity, with confiscation and fine each of 1,226,415.10 yuan, and the two signing accountants fined 200,000 yuan each; the finding concerned revenue inflated through fictitious powertrain sales. This case forms a pair with another firm in Chapter 14's materials library (fi-008): two firms in succession failed to intercept the same fraud, which indicates that the failure point is not the personal quality of one group of people but the executed form of the industry's standard audit procedures. The procedure is written into the working-paper template, execution follows the template, and a filled template means the procedure is complete. The template and the fraud technique never had a mandatory meeting point. Minimum counterfactual: upgrade confirmation and site-visit procedures for revenue accounts as fraud techniques evolve, instead of reusing the working-paper template. Primary source: China Securities Regulatory Commission, No. 54 of 2026, 2026; link in Appendix B, fi-009.

Set the nine cases side by side and three regularities of drift are complete. The host of drift is the high-frequency action: a logbook check in flight (every trip), music in the wheelhouse (every night), a tag handover (hundreds in one outage), a tick on a checklist (every operation), a filled working paper (every engagement). Drift never parasitizes a low-frequency decision, because a low-frequency action never gets the chance to become a convention. Drift's camouflage is efficiency and professionalism: every site version is faster, smoother and more experienced-looking than the manual version, so drift gets celebrated as improvement until the day it is reclassified as an accident. Drift's reconciliation cost runs three orders of magnitude below the accident cost, and the organization pays only the latter, because the former arrives without an invoice. Those three together give the budget logic of prevention: every penny allocated to a reconciliation interface, Devices 1 through 4, saves a thousand times its value at the tail.

## 15.4 The earliest you can see

Eight weak signals, each with the reason it is usually waved through.

1. What training teaches differs from what the site does, and both sides know it. The reason: "training covers principles, the site covers what actually happens."
2. New people are brought up to speed by older staff, not by the manual. The reason: "learning the job from someone experienced is the most effective way."
3. A step in the manual has never once been executed in practice and still appears on every compliance checklist. The reason: "better to have it written than not."
4. The site's verdict on the manual is that it was written for the auditors. The reason: "everyone understands that."
5. When workload rises, the first thing simplified is the pause in the process: the cross-check, the read-back, the wait. The reason: "efficiency."
6. An audit finds that practice differs from the manual, and the outcome is a change to the site rather than the manual, or a change to the manual without asking the site. The reason: "one end has to move."
7. A key action is demonstrated in two versions, the factory version and the site version, and new people are told to watch the site version. The reason: "the manual is out of date, don't learn it wrong."
8. The person who signed the procedure cannot say which accident or risk the procedure was written against. The reason: "compliance requires it."

Put those signals back against the cases. Signal one, training and site differ and both sides know it, matches Case 1: handling paperwork in flight was a default the site taught. Signal two, new people taught by colleagues rather than by the manual, matches Case 8: every practice on that deck came from being shown. Signal three, a manual step never executed but still on the compliance form, matches Case 9: the procedure lines in the audit template were filled in as usual, with the real intensity of confirmations and site visits accounted for elsewhere. Signal five, the pause simplified first, matches Cases 1 and 6: a head down in a logbook and a watch stood alone both traded a pause for output. Signal six, the audit finds the fork and only one end moves, matches Case 2: the alarm was fitted, which is the manual end, and nobody touched the music in the wheelhouse, which is the site end. Signal eight, the signatory cannot say what accident the procedure addresses, is the hidden thread of the chapter: in Case 5's training actions and Case 8's deck conventions, neither the signatories nor the trainers knew which stretch of history they had routed around.

The method for finding drift deserves a section of its own, because its difficulty differs from that of normalization. Normalization is audited against ledgers, which have archives. Drift is audited against practice, which has none. The workable method is shadow comparison: take one key process, send one group through a full walkthrough following only the manual and another group through following site habit, and have the two write up a difference list item by item. Every entry on that list then gets classified. Site practice better: amend the manual. Site lacking resources: supply the resources. Site taking the easy road: put it on a ledger. Shadow comparison costs one crew's time, and it measures the one thing in the organization that nothing else measures: the distance between text and practice.

A cheaper high-frequency probe sits alongside shadow comparison. Register the difference between "the manual says" and "we actually do" at the crew meeting, ten minutes at a fixed slot each time, on one question only: which steps did we do differently from the manual this week. Nothing registered needs handling straight away; let it accumulate. A quarter of registrations is that crew's drift map. Most crews are startled the first time they draw it: the entries do not number two or three, they number dozens, and more than half of them are differences nobody can remember starting. The value of the map is not the correction, which runs through Chapter 11's three-state register. The value is making the fork visible: an invisible fork is climate, and a visible fork is an agenda item.

## 15.5 How to prevent it

Device 1: manual-reality comparison. Twice a year, write the manual's steps for a key process alongside actual practice and produce a difference list. Each difference is either an amendment to the manual, conceding that reality is better, or a change to the process, resourcing reality to follow the manual. Only one thing is forbidden: failing to compare. Signatories: the process owner and one representative from the line.

Device 2: list protection for pause actions. Every pause-type action in a process, the cross-check, the read-back, the visual confirmation, goes on a separate register, and any request to simplify one has to be heard item by item, with the default answer being no. Pause actions are the first thing drift eats.

Device 3: a spatial dimension for occupation approval. Wherever a physical space in a facility carries a safety function, keep a register of protected space, route every occupation through a separate approval, and separate the approver from the user.

Device 4: a resource clause in every rule you write. Every mandatory procedure carries a statement of its execution cost, the time, the people and the tools required, and whenever the procedure changes, that cost is checked against the real workload on the site. Writing a rule nobody can execute is prepaying for one drift.

Each of the four devices has one direct use on this chapter's cases. Device 1 on Case 1: write the site practice of using en-route time alongside the manual's see-and-avoid clause, and classify the difference as a site lacking resources, since only a document mount or a two-person crew would let the practice stand. Device 2 on Case 6: register the handover confirmation as a pause-type action and hear any request to simplify the watch rota item by item, because simplifying the rota is exactly the entry point for watchkeeping drift. Device 3 on Case 4: re-examine the cable occupation of the platform recess against the protected-space register, and either approve each existing occupation retroactively or remove it. Device 4 on Case 9: attach execution cost and conditions of applicability to each line of the audit programme template, and require any engagement outside those conditions to escalate to a different procedure rather than filling in the template.

Four failure modes, one per device. Manual-reality comparison dies when the comparison becomes a formality: the difference list is written and filed, and the revision schedule is always next quarter. Pause-action protection dies when the register goes stale: a new process goes live without the register being updated, and the shield protects an action that no longer exists. The space register dies when its scope is too narrow: only physical space is registered, and nobody watches the occupation of process space, meaning authority, approval slots and handover slots. The resource clause dies when the cost statement is wrong: the department writing the rule underestimates what execution costs, and the clause drifts the day it goes live. All four share one patch. Each device's output, the difference list, the register of pause actions, the space ledger, the cost statement, carries a mandatory annual review date, and the review is done by someone other than the device's owner.

One organizational precondition is worth naming. The reconciliation interface needs a third party both sides accept. The owner of the manual believes the site does not follow the rules; the site believes the manual has nothing to do with the ground; without a third party the reconciliation talks break down every time. The line representative's signature in Device 1 and the cost statement in Device 4 are both ways of institutionalizing that third party. An organization without the role can only convene the conversation between manual and reality in the form of an accident.

The reconciliation meeting has one output that is easy to overlook: drift contains improvement proposals. When site practice departs from the manual, sometimes the site is wrong, sometimes the manual is wrong because the rule has not kept up with changes in equipment, process or users, and most of the time each side holds part of the truth. The meeting's decisions therefore have only three legitimate exits: amend the manual, conceding that reality is better; supply resources so reality returns to the manual; or put the difference on a ledger and run with the risk. An organization that presumes site practice is guilty by definition will miss the first exit, and the first exit is often the most valuable of the three, since the site wisdom absorbed into a manual revision is exactly what lets the next edition still be executed. An organization that bans every fork does not buy compliance. It buys better-kept false accounts.

The last design detail is who attends. The manual side, the people who write the rule; the site side, the people who execute it; the safety side, the people accountable for the consequences. All three in the room is the minimum for the meeting to be valid, and with any one missing the alignment degrades into a one-sided decision. The chair is best held by the safety side, since safety is the one function among the three whose performance is measured by finding forks. Frequency follows risk: twice a year for high-risk processes, once a year for low-risk ones, plus ad hoc meetings triggered by any change. An organization that writes this meeting into its quality system has fitted drift with a standing brake.

How well the brake works depends on where the minutes go. The direct readers of the reconciliation minutes should be the manual's owner and the training department, as input to revision, with the safety committee copied for the risk view. Minutes that end up in any other drawer turn the brake into one more document ritual. A fixed three-column format is worth adopting: difference described, cause classified as site error, manual error or missing resource, and decision with a deadline. Only minutes with all three columns can be checked against at the next meeting six months later, and the completion rate of the previous round's decisions is the only proof that the mechanism is alive.

## 15.6 Borders with neighbouring mechanisms

Against normalization of deviance (Chapter 11): drift describes the existence of the fork, that how the work is done has changed; normalization describes the fork being refiled, that how it is classified has changed. Drift can occur without normalization, since a fork held on a ledger stays in the state of correction by a deadline. Normalization normally uses drift as its raw material but needs that second step, explanation and filing. The order of treatment: align first, which is this chapter, then defend against refiling, which is Chapter 11.

Against compliant and unsafe (Chapter 32): compliant and unsafe means every inspection passes and the whole is still dangerous, because the inspection system and the real risk are misaligned. Practical drift is text and practice forking, because documents and behaviour are misaligned. The Ickenham recess sits on both: the occupation went through engineering approval, which is compliant, and the safety function was gone, which is unsafe. When filing a case, classify by the mechanism that set it off and attach the residual mechanisms as secondary.

Against automation and skill decay (Chapter 26): drift is a person's practice departing from the text; decay is a person's ability to execute an existing action diminishing. Rising automation accelerates both at once, since the text updates more slowly than the system, which is drift, and the person stops practising as the system takes over, which is decay. Drift in an automated environment has one further form: the system's own configuration forks from the text, and no old hand is left who would notice.

## 15.7 The five self-checks

1. Write down three differences between what the manual says and what is actually done in a process you own. If you cannot, is that because you do not know, or because there are none?
2. Which item on your organization's checklists do you know has never actually been executed? Is it still on every form?
3. At the last process simplification, what kind of action was cut: output-producing or pause-type? When a pause action is cut, is there a ledger entry?
4. Which rules on your site are written in a form nobody could follow? Name three, and ask yourself whether you dare propose a revision.
5. Does protected space, physical or procedural, have a register in your organization? When was it last occupied?
