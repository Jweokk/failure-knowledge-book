# Chapter 14 Where Intuitive Expertise Breaks Down (Kahneman & Klein)

> What this chapter covers: the conditions under which expert intuition is reliable and the conditions under which it fails; the three criteria; what the failure zone looks like on the ground in four domains; how to test "experience" when it is offered as an argument, and how to make intuition explicit.
> When to turn back: when a veteran is put into a new environment; when someone answers data with "I have done this for twenty years"; when your own sense that a new situation "feels right" arrives.
> Estimated reading time: 50 minutes.

Kahneman, an experimental psychologist who spent a career doubting intuition, and Klein, a naturalistic decision researcher who spent a career defending it, once wrote a paper together whose title is the answer: a failure to disagree. What they found is that under particular conditions the two schools agree completely on when intuition can be trusted. There are two conditions: the environment is regular enough, and the feedback is fast enough. This chapter is that agreement turned into engineering, moving "can this be done on intuition" out of folklore and into three criteria a practitioner can check against a situation.

## 1 What It Is

Definition: intuition is a recognition capacity trained jointly by the regularity of an environment and the feedback of practice. Kahneman and Klein give the two preconditions for it to hold. The environment must be regular enough for the regularities to be learned, and practice must carry timely, high-quality feedback, so that errors are seen and corrected (Kahneman & Klein, "Conditions for Intuitive Expertise: A Failure to Disagree", American Psychologist 64(6), 515–526, 2009 (Crossref record checked, DOI 10.1037/a0016755)). Take away either precondition and intuition enters its failure zone. It still answers quickly, its confidence does not move, and its accuracy drops to the level of a beginner.

Three activating criteria. Any one of them appearing puts intuition out of service as a basis for decision:

1. The environment is unfamiliar, or the rules have just changed (new industry, new equipment, new population, new regulatory framework).
2. Feedback is delayed by more than a few months, or there is no feedback at all (the consequences of the decision cannot be seen and cannot be measured).
3. There is no opportunity for repeated practice (a once-in-a-lifetime situation).

What matters is admitting where the real danger of the failure zone sits. Inside it, intuition does not weaken; it decouples from its own confidence. The certainty the judge feels and the actual hit rate of the judgment part company, and the judge can feel only the first. That is why the most dangerous person in a failure zone is the old expert whose environment changed and whose confidence did not. He is not bluffing. He genuinely feels certain, and that certainty was once calibrated, in the old environment.

Klein's half of the school deserves a paragraph of its own, because it says what "a regular environment" concretely means. Klein studied fire commanders, nurses and military commanders, experts who grew up inside real environments, and the model he produced is the recognition-primed decision: a veteran reads a scene by pattern matching, the matched pattern arrives with a workable course of action attached, and the whole process runs fast enough that the person feels only "intuition". The joint between that model and the criteria is the phrase pattern library. The environment feeds the library, so the environment's regularity sets the library's valid range. Regularity can be measured: the same class of situation recurs, and the same cues point to the same outcome. Change the environment and the library is still there. Matching still happens, options still come out, and every match is wrong. The recognition-primed decision model never says intuition fails. It says precisely where intuition fails: on the first day after the library is swapped.

Calibration needs writing out in full, because every test in this chapter rests on it. Calibration is the correspondence between confidence and hit rate: say you are nine tenths certain, and you are right nine times in ten. There is one route to it, a large volume of feedback. Weather forecasters are the old specimen, because the forecast is made daily, the outcome is checkable every time, and the check cannot be avoided. The same person, well calibrated in a feedback-dense domain, returns to ordinary performance the moment he moves into a feedback-sparse one. The corollary is hard: calibration is a property of the domain, and an expert who believes he carries it with him is making a mistake. So the question an organization should ask about intuition is never "how accurate are you". It is "what is the feedback density of your domain". The first question measures temperament. The second measures structure.

One organizational consequence of the confidence-accuracy split is worth writing down. A judgment in the failure zone cannot be repaired by thinking it through again. The second pass draws on the same pattern library and returns the same answer, and confidence may rise, because familiarity gets misread as confirmation. That is why all four defences in this chapter are external: an admission form, a checklist, a retraining trigger, an instrument. What they share is that the checking action is placed outside the judge's pattern library. An organization that expects its judges to doubt themselves ends up with confidence squared.

## 2 How It Grows

There are three entry paths into the failure zone.

Path one: cross-domain transplant. Intuition accumulated in domain A gets carried into domain B (an old CEO dropped into a new industry, a senior mechanical engineer taking over a software system, a successful clinician moved into management). Environmental regularity changed; the recognition template did not. Transplanting happens often because promotion and secondment reward exactly the past record of intuition: the more successful the person, the more likely he is to be moved.

Path two: one success treated as a regularity. A single survival is distilled into reusable "experience". A rule with a sample size of one is unusually confident inside the failure zone, because it never meets a counterexample. A counterexample needs repetition, and this rule has one instance.

Path three: rule change erodes the old regularity. The environment did not change, the rules did (new regulation, new aircraft type, new version, new procedure), and the old intuition is already off target at the level of detail while the subject perceives no change. Not perceiving the rule change is the quietest entrance to the failure zone. Paths one and two at least come with a signal that the environment moved. Path three comes with no signal at all.

The amplifier is hierarchy. The more senior the judgment, the fewer people there are to check it. A front-line error is found the same day (a cross-threaded bolt will not go back in), while an executive's strategic judgment shows itself three to five years later, and the showing can be re-explained by narrative (Chapter 13). The feedback loop is weakest at the top of an organization, which is where the failure zone stays exposed longest.

Beyond the three paths and the one amplifier, add an observation about time. The failure zone has an age structure. A young person's intuition library is thin, so the failure zone is small: there is not much intuition to fail. In mid-career the library is at its thickest and the environment at its most stable, and the hit rate is at its lifetime peak. Late in a career the library is still at its thickest, while the environment has turned over several times, and the library holds layered memory left by several generations of it, without the person necessarily knowing which layer he is drawing on. This explains a portrait that recurs in failure-zone cases: the judgment that goes wrong was made by the most senior person in the room, and that judgment was accurate earlier in that person's career. Organizational ageing therefore has a dimension that is usually left out of the count. Equipment and procedure age (Chapter 29), and judgment assets age as well: the thickest library corresponds to the oldest version of the environment.

The three paths are not exclusive. In real cases they feed each other. Someone who has crossed domains is especially likely to distil a single success into a rule (path two), because his old intuition occasionally lands in the new environment at a rate above a newcomer's chance level, and those few hits get read as proof that the old intuition holds in the new setting. Rule erosion (path three) gives the other two their best cover: when failure arrives, attribution goes anywhere except to the rule change, because nobody saw the rule change. In an audit, check the three paths separately. Path one checks the CV, the history of matching between person and environment. Path two checks the sample, the number of cases the basis rests on. Path three checks versions, the distance between the version of the rules and the version of the judging habit.

## 3 The Shape in Four Domains

#### Case 1 (Aviation): The Narrow-Runway Illusion

**Case ID**: av-011.

**When and where**: 19 July 2025, 10:53 local time, Lansing, Michigan, Capital Region International Airport (KLAN), runway 28L. The earlier leg that day had departed Coldwater, MI (KOEB) at 10:24, 29 minutes en route, under visual flight rules (VFR) throughout, with no flight plan filed and both departure and arrival inside Class C airspace (CEN25LA260 pp. 1, 4).

**What and who**: a Cessna A185F, registration N888DK, built in 1982, single piston engine (Continental IO-550-D027B, 300 hp), tailwheel landing gear, four seats; the annual inspection was current at the time of the accident (14 April 2025), and airframe total time was 1,652.4 hours. The operation was Part 91 private flying with no certificate holder. One pilot in the left seat, three passengers, no second pilot, four-point harnesses (pp. 3, 4).

**What they were doing**: the pilot held a private certificate with single-engine land and single-engine sea ratings, no instrument rating and no instructor rating, and a third-class medical certificate with limitations. Total flight time 295 hours, 100 hours on type, 173 hours as pilot in command, 57 hours in the previous 90 days, 16 in the previous 30, 7 in the previous 24 hours; the last flight review recorded in the report was 16 August 2022, 35 months before the accident (p. 3). The recurring flight review period required by the FAA is 24 months (14 CFR 61.56(a)). The flight was a short cross-country. The destination field's 10:53 observation read few clouds at 3,000 feet, overcast at 4,400 feet, visibility 10 miles, wind 200° at 8 knots, temperature 24°C, dew point 19°C, altimeter 30.02 inches of mercury, no turbulence; 28L was dry asphalt, 8,506 feet long and 150 feet wide (pp. 4, 5). With the wind from 200° and the runway's magnetic bearing at 280°, the angle between them is 80°, the crosswind component about 7.9 knots and the headwind component about 1.4 knots (converted from the wind data at p. 1 and the runway data at p. 5).

**The decision point**: three moments, each with a carrier that can be checked.

Moment one, before the 10:24 departure. Runway width is printed in the pre-flight material: both the airport diagram and the Airport/Facility Directory (A/FD) list runway dimensions as a separate item. The file holds no record of any pre-flight preparation. Report p. 6 states that the NTSB did not travel to the scene, that the investigation was Class 4, and that the report rests on the pilot's statement and material supplied by the FAA; the type and this operation were not required to carry a cockpit voice recorder or a flight data recorder, so what was said on approach and where the pilot looked left no trace in the file.

Moment two, the flare shortly before 10:53. The report records that the pilot stated he flared too high and suspected the runway width illusion had affected him (p. 1). Three things were in use in the cockpit at once: the visual angle the runway edge lines subtended, the altimeter reading, and the flare rhythm built up over 100 hours on type. The illusion acts on the first of them. Runway width changes the angle the edge lines subtend, that angle gets read as the remaining height, and the reading has a different source from the altimeter.

Moment three, after the touchdown bounce. The report's order is: bounced on contact with the runway, the nose began to weathervane into the crosswind, the pilot made control inputs to stop the ground loop, did not succeed, the right wing struck the runway (p. 1). The probable cause has two clauses: improper flare, and failure to maintain directional control while landing in crosswind conditions (p. 1). The findings run to three lines: aircraft control (pilot); landing flare (unable to attain or maintain); crosswind (response, compensation) (p. 2).

The direction of the width illusion has a standard formulation in flight training material, and both directions are given there. A runway narrower than the one a pilot is used to makes the aircraft seem higher than it is, the approach goes low and the touchdown comes early. A runway wider than the familiar one makes the aircraft seem lower, the approach goes high, and the touchdown is hard or runs off the end (FAA, Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge, Ch.17 "Runway Width Illusion"). Runway 28L is 150 feet wide. The report records neither the width of the pilot's usual runway nor the direction of the illusion, only his account of flaring too high (p. 1). This chapter keeps "narrow-runway illusion" as shorthand for the class.

**What was visible then**: what could be had in the cockpit, ordered by how checkable it is.

Pre-flight material. The runway is 8,506 by 150 feet, asphalt, dry, printed on the airport diagram and in the A/FD (p. 5); the same documents carry the field elevation of 860 feet and the approach type column for 28L, which reads None (p. 5). These numbers were in the flight bag before departure and needed no equipment to obtain.

The 10:53 local observation. Few clouds at 3,000 feet, overcast at 4,400 feet, visibility 10 miles, wind 200° at 8 knots, temperature 24°C, dew point 19°C, altimeter 30.02 inches of mercury, turbulence column None/None (p. 4). Field pressure altitude 860 feet with a temperature of 24°C is roughly 11°C above the standard atmosphere at that altitude, so density altitude sat above pressure altitude (converted from the data at pp. 4 and 5); this could be worked in the cockpit, and the report does not record whether it was.

Cockpit instruments. The report records no reading from the altimeter, the airspeed indicator or the vertical speed indicator. Aircraft of this class are not required to carry a flight data recorder, so the readings existed only in the pilot's memory, and memory appears in the report in a single phrase: flared too high (p. 1).

The runway itself. A 150-foot asphalt strip, and the angle its edge lines subtended was the only real-time height reference on this approach. It is more immediate than the altimeter and easier to mislead, since the angle changes with viewpoint, cockpit height and seating posture, while the altimeter does not (p. 5 gives the width; the mechanism is at FAA handbook Ch.17).

The pilot's own records. Licence, medical, and five lines of hours in the logbook (p. 3), plus his judgment of the aircraft's condition, which the report records as his statement that there had been no mechanical malfunction or failure beforehand (p. 1). That sentence removed a whole class of hypotheses at the time.

What was learned only afterwards: damage to the right wing and aileron was classified as substantial (p. 1); the defining event was coded as loss of control in flight (p. 1); the findings put the landing flare on its own line as unable to attain or maintain (p. 2); and the NTSB never went to the scene at all (p. 6). All four are in the report. None of them was in the cockpit.

**What happened**: all four people aboard were uninjured, the right wing and aileron were substantially damaged, and there was no fire and no explosion (pp. 1, 5). The NTSB published its final report on 5 September 2025 without travelling to the scene; the investigator in charge was Bradley Miller, and the FAA's Grand Rapids office participated (p. 6). Back to the three questions. The most common answer to the first is "go around". The sequence in this case put the cancellable moment before touchdown: by the bounce the aircraft was on the runway, the speed was decaying, the 7.9 knots of crosswind were still there, and the energy and attitude window a go-around needs had already closed, while the action's cost had been 30 seconds with a table before departure. The item most often missed under the second question is the five lines of experience figures on p. 3. All of them were valid and none of them was about width; no line records how many different runway widths he had landed on. The checkpoint for the third question belongs in the approach briefing before departure: one item only, how far this runway's width is from the usual one, and whether the flare reference is the edge lines or the touchdown markings.

**Options ruled out**: a go-around. The report does not record it being considered. The structural fact that can be checked is that the cancellable window lay before touchdown, and that at the bounce the aircraft was already on the runway with the speed decaying and about 7.9 knots of crosswind still present (pp. 1, 4). Diverting to another runway or another airport. KLAN was the destination, the flight plan field reads None, and no alternate is recorded (p. 4); with 10 miles visibility the weather offered no obstacle, and the obstacle was that a diversion needs to be decided in advance, a decision that is not in any document. Taking an instructor or a second pilot along. The report records Second Pilot Present: No (p. 3); Part 91 private flying requires neither a second pilot nor an instructor aboard, and the cost of this option is one seat and one scheduling action. Moving the flare reference from the runway edge lines to the touchdown markings. The report records no approach briefing at all, and the type and operation were not required to carry a voice or data recorder, so what was looked at and said on approach left no trace (p. 6). A fifth option was flying the trip under instrument flight rules (IFR). The pilot held no instrument rating (p. 3), which put this out of reach at the time; it never entered the option list.

**Early signals**: ① the flight review stopped at 16 August 2022, 35 months before the accident, past the 24-month cycle (p. 3; 14 CFR 61.56(a)); the reason it was waved through: licence valid, medical current, 57 hours in 90 days, three items all in order, and none of them triggers an interception. ② no instrument rating (p. 3), which means no training in the cross-check that treats instruments as primary and vision as secondary; the reason it was waved through: under visual flight rules vision as primary is the prescribed action anyway. ③ 100 hours on type and 295 in total (p. 3), ample for private flying but covering one type and one class of runway; the reason it was waved through: hours get used as a substitute for scenario coverage. ④ the destination runway is 150 feet wide (p. 5), with the usual runway width unrecorded; the reason it was waved through: 8,506 feet of length is enough, and width is on no checklist. ⑤ a crosswind component of about 7.9 knots (converted, pp. 1 and 5); the reason it was waved through: 8 knots is a routine value in private flying, and the report records the turbulence as None. ⑥ four seats, three passengers, a single pilot in the left seat (pp. 3, 4); the reason it was waved through: carrying passengers is not a regulatory problem, and Part 91 adds no review for it.

**The point of escalation**: the bounce. Before it, a high flare was a height error that could be covered with back pressure at the moment of touchdown. After it, the aircraft had been thrown back into the air, the speed was decaying, the nose was swinging into the crosswind, and the correction available had shrunk to rudder and aileron, with ground contact already having happened once. A tailwheel aircraft yaws quickly at that moment: once the mainwheels touch, the nose turns into the crosswind, the fuselage axis separates from the direction of travel, the angle of attack becomes asymmetric across the wings, and one wing drops. The report's order is flare too high, bounce, weathervane, control inputs, unsuccessful, right wing strikes the runway (p. 1); it records neither the height of the bounce nor the time from bounce to wingtip contact. No signature, no approval, no second person present. In Part 91 private flying this step has no documentary carrier at all, which is exactly why it cannot be audited afterwards. The pilot's retelling is all there is to check.

**Failure chain**: technical layer: runway width changed the visual height reference and the flare went high; touchdown bounce; the nose weathervaned in the crosswind; correction insufficient; right wing and aileron struck the ground, damage substantial (p. 1). Organizational layer: a Part 91 private operation has no operator safety management system, no dispatch and no second pilot (pp. 3, 4); regulation sets the flight review at 24 months and this case ran 35 (p. 3); the training and checking columns record review dates and not scenario subjects such as runway width. Incentive layer: empty in this case, and the emptiness is itself a fact. There was no scheduling pressure, no hourly target, no carrier rule, and on a 29-minute leg nothing became more expensive because of one more table lookup on runway width. All three layers broke at once. The interface sits between the organizational and technical layers: a perceptual error needs an external check to be caught, and nothing in the Part 91 structure holds a post responsible for supplying that check.

**Minimum counterfactual**: before the approach, read the runway dimensions line in the airport material (28L: 150 feet), put the width difference into the briefing, and move the flare reference from the edge lines to the touchdown markings. One action, taken before the 10:24 departure, needing no luck: the data is in a public document and the report already carries it at p. 5, and the cost is 30 seconds and one sentence. The action changes no flying skill. It changes one thing, moving the "how much height is left" judgment off the visual angle and onto a ground marking that does not vary with runway width. Nor does it require the pilot to know the word illusion: look up the width, compare it, change the reference point, three steps that can be executed without understanding the perceptual mechanism behind them.

**Outcome and sources**: four people aboard uninjured; right wing and aileron substantially damaged; no fire, no explosion (pp. 1, 5). The NTSB concluded improper flare followed by failure to maintain directional control in a crosswind landing (p. 1). The investigation was Class 4 with no travel to the scene, and the report rests on the pilot's statement and FAA material (p. 6), so half of what this card can say depends on what the pilot said himself. He said he flared too high, and he said the runway width illusion may have affected him: the two most informative sentences in the case and the two that cannot be re-checked. Primary source: NTSB, Aviation Investigation Final Report, CEN25LA260, Cessna A185F, N888DK, Lansing, Michigan, July 19, 2025, adopted 2025-09-05 (PDF 6 pages), link at Appendix B av-011; both directions of the runway width illusion at FAA, Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge, Ch.17 "Runway Width Illusion".

#### Case 2 (Chemicals): A Newcomer on a Screen with Three Valves

**Case ID**: ch-004.

**When and where**: 4 June 2025 at 14:14:28, Monaca, Pennsylvania, furnace 5 of the ethane cracker (ECU) at Shell Polymers Monaca; at 14:20:45 the gas in the firebox was ignited by the pilot lights and the furnace exploded (CSB 2025-05-I-PA p. 61, Appendix A timeline). The site sits about 30 miles northwest of Pittsburgh, covers 386 acres, and started production in November 2022 (p. 10).

**What and who**: Shell Polymers Monaca, an ethane cracker of Shell Chemical Appalachia under Shell plc, with seven cracking furnaces in parallel and a site output of 3.3 billion pounds of polyethylene pellets a year. On the day of the incident the site held 351 employees, 529 contractors and 2 visitors (p. 10). Two people were in the posts at issue: one process control engineer (a PACO engineer in Shell's internal usage, meaning process, automation, controls and optimization) and one ethylene control room operator. The responsible parties are written as posts: the operations production specialist (P-Spec) who assigned the valve-opening task, the PACO engineer who executed it, and the operator who acknowledged the alarm (pp. 26, 28).

**What they were doing**: a planned return to service. The coke catchers on all seven furnaces had never been cleaned since production began in November 2022; in late March 2025 furnace 1 was opened for inspection and the coke had reached the manway (p. 24). In early April the unit shut down for maintenance, the catchers on furnaces 1, 2, 4 and 7 were cleaned for the first time, and furnace 5 was next in the queue (p. 24). Furnace 5 shut down on 30 May, and on the night shift of 31 May the energy isolation was completed: two 36-inch motor-operated valves (MOVs), one on the furnace side and one on the column side, were closed together, steam at a pressure above process pressure was introduced into the cavity between them, and the vent between them was opened (pp. 24, 16). At that point furnaces 1, 2, 3 and 4 were cracking; furnace 6 shut down on 1 June; furnace 7 moved to hot steam standby (HSSB) on 4 June (pp. 24, 59–60).

Catcher cleaning requires no entry into the furnace, so the work was not isolated as a confined-space entry. The furnace-side valve was closed, the decoking valve open, and the pilot lights left burning, the reason being to keep the refractory from dropping below the dew point and condensing; on the night shift of 18 October 2021 water from a hydrostatic test had damaged the refractory brick at the bottom of a furnace (p. 25 and note c). That decision was inconsistent with the site's Furnace Isolation Plan, which requires isolating the furnace and cutting the gas to the pilot lights, and the change to lighting them did not go through the deviation review of the Safe Isolation Policy (p. 38). While a furnace sits in pilot-light-only mode, three alarm groups, furnace pressure high, carbon monoxide high and methane high, are suppressed by design to prevent alarm flooding and nuisance alarms, and the suppression appears on screen as a grey bell icon (p. 25 and note d). At 09:20 on 3 June, because the fan motor control firmware was updated during the cleaning, the operator on shift switched the induced draft fan from pressure control to speed control, at about 163 rpm (pp. 30, 60). At 13:40 that day the two MOVs were re-energized (pp. 26, 60). The 08:00 meeting on 4 June was to move furnace 5 out of pilot-light-only and into HSSB; the furnace start-up procedure does not cover the step of leaving pilot-light-only, so the operator had no procedure to follow (p. 26).

**The decision point**: four irreversible moments.

Moment one, the 2023 ECU process hazard analysis (PHA) revalidation. The document is on file, and the revalidation record identifies the case of "the furnace-side MOV not closed while the column-side MOV is open, causing cross-flow or backflow" as a scenario that could kill several people (p. 42, Figure 20). The same revalidation judged two safeguards, the operator's response to the methane high alarm and the start-up procedure, to be sufficient, and wrote that adding an engineering control was grossly disproportional to the risk reduction (p. 43). The same document carries a separate recommendation, to remove a backflow path created by the routing of the differential pressure transmitter tubing, and management concluded that the risk was tolerable and did not adopt it (p. 43). The carriers are the PHA revalidation record and the human factors checklist; the report names no signatories.

Moment two, the 2016 and 2017 HMI design specifications. Shell commissioned a process automation contractor to write the HMI Detailed Design Specification, with a first edition in 2016 and a second in 2017 (p. 46). The specification required control logic in the same area or unit to be merged onto a single SIS logic drawing wherever possible, and limited annotation text on those drawings to 40 characters (pp. 47, 49). The three MOVs therefore share one screen, ordered top to bottom as furnace side 511, decoking 512 and column side 513, switched by scrolling, with labels that differ only in the last digit (pp. 27, 28).

Moment three, the 08:00 meeting on 4 June 2025. The operations production specialist assigned the task of opening the column-side valve to the PACO engineer, and the minutes are on file (p. 26, Figure 13). The engineer had never performed that task before (pp. 7, 26, 40). The meeting did not discuss whether an SIS bypass permit was needed (p. 26). The path itself was not new: close both MOVs to isolate, have the PACO engineer open the column-side valve from the engineering station, then use the furnace-side valve to bring the furnace back to cracking. It had been executed successfully 19 times (pp. 20, 41).

Moment four, 14:14:28. The SIS lock was released at 14:04 (p. 61). At 14:13:47 the engineer entered configuration (debug) mode, the screen refreshed and jumped back to the top, and his gaze moved from the column-side valve logic in the lower third of the screen to the furnace-side valve logic in the upper third (p. 28). At 14:14:28 the open command went to 511 rather than 513 (p. 61).

**What was visible then**: the screen in front of the PACO engineer held three logic blocks laid out identically and numbered 511, 512 and 513, with nothing else describing furnace side, decoking or column side (pp. 27, 28, 50); the scrollbar returned to the top on refresh (p. 28); there was one written job aid, in which the two steps "select SIS mode" and "issue the open command" are in the wrong order (pp. 36, 40); and there was an indication that the SIS was in read-only mode, which he did not recognize at first and which a process control engineer called over to help spotted (p. 27). On the control room side there was an alarm reading "MOV in unexpected position", its description text differing from the column-side valve alarm only in the last digit, and a grey bell showing the three safety alarm groups still suppressed (pp. 35, 25). On the process side, the induced draft fan was in speed control at about 163 rpm, and the furnace's structural pressure limit was 0.01 psig (p. 30).

About the engineer's experience the report supports only three sentences: he had never performed this task before (pp. 7, 26, 40); he was assigned at the daily production meeting and had to follow the written job aid (p. 40); his process knowledge was limited, and he could not see the process consequence of opening the column-side valve while the furnace-side valve was open (p. 40). The report records neither his years in the trade nor how many SIS bypasses he had worked before, only that this particular task was his first. Another person was in the same room that day. The process control engineer called in to help had opened valves before, saw at the door that the SIS was in read-only mode, and said it had to be switched to debug before it would accept commands (p. 27). One screen, and one man saw the mode first while the other saw the valve first. The difference is not intelligence; it is whether the pattern library has that cell in it.

What was learned only afterwards: two MOVs open at once made a path from the high-pressure header to the low-pressure firebox; within 6 minutes about 641 pounds of cracked gas accumulated in the firebox; and the explosion tore the furnace wall open (pp. 29, 30).

**What happened**: the explosion came at 14:20:45 and fire followed, with the fire fought until 16:00 (pp. 31, 61). Nobody was injured. Fifteen employees were evacuated from the ethane cracking area, and one contractor trapped in the unit elevator beside furnace 5 was rescued (p. 32). Furnaces 4 and 6 shut down for inspection and returned to service on 18 June and 20 June respectively (p. 32). Shell estimated property damage at $95 million, and the release was about 5,100 pounds (515 pounds of unburned cracked gas and about 4,600 pounds of combustion products) (pp. 7, 31). Furnace 5 was rebuilt over six months and restarted on 22 January 2026 (p. 32). OSHA issued three citations on 1 December 2025 with proposed penalties of $26,480, and the case closed on 2 January 2026 (p. 32). The CSB listed two major safety issues, reliance on administrative controls and the human-machine interface, and issued two recommendations to Shell (p. 9). Back to the three questions. The most common answer to the first is "colour-code the three valves". This screen already had numbers, colours and zones, and what it lacked was task analysis and a confirmation step; colour would only have added another layer for a person to look at. The item most often missed under the second question is the number 19: it converted an unreviewed work path into site routine. The checkpoint for the third question belongs on the signature page of the PHA revalidation: one item only, for any scenario judged capable of killing several people, is at least one safeguard independent of human recognition.

**Options ruled out**: an engineering control. Linde's original design provided two, a key interlock used in sequence and a SIL 2 interlock that closes the furnace-side valve automatically when low backflow pressure is detected (p. 44). Both live on the field manual panel, and the panel was never programmed, at commissioning, to support removing the double isolation, so both interlocks were equivalent to absent whenever the furnace sat in double isolation (p. 44). Opening the valve in the field. Design and policy both require a field operator to work the panel and visually confirm valve position (p. 39), but the column-side valve can only be opened by the PACO engineer from the engineering station (p. 19). An SIS bypass permit. Shell policy requires a bypass permit naming the equipment tag to be monitored; staff understood the requirement to apply only when operations opened a valve, and the CSB could not find that exemption in the policy text (pp. 37, 41). The emergency stop (ESD). It was not pressed, and Shell judged afterwards that pressing it would not have changed the result, since an ESD takes longer to close the column-side valve than a close command issued inside the SIS (p. 37, note 2). Deferring the isolation removal to a period with someone watching. The report does not record the option; the site does not count moving in and out of maintenance status as start-up, shutdown or abnormal operation, so no isolation zone was set (p. 32 note a, p. 37).

**Early signals**: ① the 2023 PHA revalidation stated that the scenario could kill several people, and both safeguards were administrative controls (pp. 42, 43); the reason it was waved through: engineering controls were judged grossly disproportional to the risk reduction. ② the human factors checklist in the same revalidation did not record opening and closing MOVs from the engineering station as a human factors problem, although it had been done a dozen or more times (p. 48); the reason it was waved through: this was routine work, not abnormal. ③ 19 successful restarts (pp. 20, 41); the reason it was waved through: nothing went wrong on any of them, which counts as having been validated, and the CCPS names this phenomenon outright in bypass work practice as normalization of deviance (p. 41). ④ the job aid has "select mode" and "issue command" in the wrong order (pp. 36, 40); the reason it was waved through: the aid was written during commissioning and has always been used this way. ⑤ three safety alarm groups suppressed by design in pilot-light-only mode, among them a carbon monoxide alarm that could have acted about 90 seconds before ignition (pp. 25, 35); the reason it was waved through: suppression exists to hold down alarm flooding and nuisance alarms. ⑥ the induced draft fan in speed control at 163 rpm rather than the normal pressure control (pp. 30, 60); the reason it was waved through: raising speed by hand while the furnace is being placed in service is site routine (p. 30).

**The point of escalation**: 14:16:46. The column-side valve had not moved for two minutes, and the engineer concluded that the furnace-side valve was already open and the command had done nothing, so he withdrew the 511 open command from the SIS logic (pp. 28, 61). Withdrawing it deleted the only record showing that 511 had just been opened; what he saw with his own eyes was a valve that had been open all along. At 14:17:50 he scrolled back to the bottom of the screen and sent the open command to 513 (p. 61). From that moment both valves were open, the path existed, and a valve needs about 4 minutes to travel from closed to fully open (p. 30). The backflow clock had started at 14:14:28 and had already run 3 minutes 22 seconds.

There were two further chances in the six minutes between 14:14:28 and 14:20:45. At about 14:15:45, roughly five minutes before the explosion, the "MOV in unexpected position" alarm reached the ethylene control room; the operator on shift acknowledged it, read it as the column-side valve alarm because the column-side valve was the one under discussion that day, and took no corrective action (pp. 35, 39). Had he handled it the approved way, a field operator would have been sent to check the furnace-side valve, and the CSB adds a note: such a field person might have been standing near the explosion point, and the result could have been serious injury or death (p. 28, note a). At 14:20 the same operator found the furnace-side valve open and the column-side valve also open, walked to the engineering station and told the engineer to close the column-side valve; by then it was 78% open and still opening (p. 28). The close command was issued at 14:20:39, and the explosion came 6 seconds later (p. 61). Neither chance corresponds to an approval action, and neither had a second reviewer. The SIS lock had been released, configuration mode had been entered, and both the withdrawal and the reissue sit in the logic log (p. 61 A13.6, A13.7), yet no action required anyone's approval.

**Failure chain**: technical layer: two MOVs in series open at once, creating a path from the header to the furnace 5 firebox; a MOV's full stroke takes about 4 minutes and the backflow lasted about 2.5 minutes; the induced draft fan's capacity was below the backflow rate; the firebox first contained the gas within its 0.01 psig structural limit, and the furnace was not designed to withstand an internal explosion; about 641 pounds of cracked gas accumulated in 6 minutes and met the pilot lights (pp. 29, 30). Organizational layer: all 11 safeguards were policy, procedure or human response to an alarm, delivered as paper and speech (pp. 35–37, 8); the PHA knew the scenario could kill several people and still chose purely administrative controls (p. 43); the start-up procedure does not cover leaving pilot-light-only (p. 26); the job aid's steps are out of order (p. 40); no SIS bypass permit was raised (p. 41); the HMI specification merged three valves onto one screen, capped labels at 40 characters and required no task analysis (pp. 47–49); the human factors checklist did not cover the task (p. 48). Incentive layer: the work plan for 4 June was to move furnace 5 from pilot-light-only to HSSB (p. 26), and the restart schedule competed with other site modification projects for the same money; reprogramming the manual panel was listed as a future project while other projects took priority and were approved (p. 44); OSHA's proposed penalty of $26,480 is not on the same scale as $95 million in damage (p. 32). All three layers broke at once. The interface: the organizational layer handed the step of identifying the right valve to the operator's pattern library, and the incentive layer put the modification that could have replaced that library, the reprogramming of the panel, at the back of the queue.

**Minimum counterfactual**: after the 2023 PHA revalidation and before the next removal of the double isolation, program the field manual panel so that the double isolation can be removed, which puts Linde's two interlocks, the key sequence and the SIL 2 low-pressure auto-close, into service in the double-isolated state, and retire the path of the PACO engineer opening the column-side valve from the SIS. One action, in the modification window after commissioning, needing no luck: the hardware is already on the panel and what is missing is programming, and report p. 44 records that this path sat on the future projects list, so what was missing was taking it off that list. The cost is one programming job and one training session; the return comes in two layers. The key interlock makes opening the wrong valve physically unreachable, and the SIL 2 interlock corrects two-valves-open automatically the instant low pressure appears. It also requires nobody to admit he can be wrong, because an interlock does not ask an operator for his years of service.

**Outcome and sources**: no injuries or deaths, 15 people evacuated, one contractor rescued from the elevator; property damage $95 million; furnace 5 out of service for six months (pp. 7, 31, 32). The CSB concluded that the cause was the simultaneous, inadvertent opening of two MOVs used for isolation, with contributing factors including the assignment of a process control engineer with limited process knowledge to operate the valves and reliance on ineffective administrative controls to prevent backflow (p. 8). The same report carries a harder finding: all 11 controls Shell identified failed, because employees had not been adequately trained beforehand, did not understand the controls, or were not capable of following them (p. 37). That sentence rounds out the definition of an administrative control. Its effectiveness is not a property of a document; it is a property of people. Primary source: U.S. Chemical Safety and Hazard Investigation Board, Furnace Explosion and Fire at Shell Polymers, Monaca, Pennsylvania, Incident Date June 4, 2025, Report No. 2025-05-I-PA, September 2026 (PDF 70 pages), link at Appendix B ch-004. Page numbers follow the PDF's physical page order.

#### Case 3 (Maritime): The Chain of Reaching In to Rescue (Condensed)

ma-010. The emergency response rescue vessel Viking Islay, September 2007, off the East Yorkshire coast, England. The MAIB report (Report 12/2008, pages read for this book) records that corrosion of steel structure inside the anchor chain locker consumed oxygen and left an oxygen-deficient atmosphere. The first crew member entered and collapsed. His shipmates went in after him one after another without gas detection equipment, and 3 seamen died. The report's own words confirm it: "three seamen on board the ERRV Viking Islay lost their lives as a consequence of entering an enclosed space". The rescue instinct is a virtue in open air; in an oxygen-deficient compartment it was the second and third cause of death. The decision point is the moment the second crew member stood at the hatch. Minimum counterfactual: after the first man collapses, the second does not enter, and calls for support first, carrying a gas detector. #### Case 5 (Healthcare): Pain That Never Entered the Triage Differential

**Case ID**: me-015.

**When and where**: the reference event took place in the emergency department of a non-specialist acute hospital in England, anonymised in the report as AH1, and the report records no specific date. HSIB opened its investigation on 20 July 2017 and published the final report on 23 January 2020. The clinical timeline runs on the clock: onset at 18:00, death at 03:15 the following day (HSIB 2020 §2.1–2.67, Appendix 1).

**What and who**: a national investigation by the Healthcare Safety Investigation Branch (HSIB) into the recognition pathway for acute aortic dissection (AD). The bodies involved were the emergency department at AH1 and the regional cardiothoracic centre (RCC). The posts, in the order the patient met them: a triage nurse (EDS, 15 years in that emergency department), a nurse in the low-dependency area (EDSN, more than 7 years of nursing experience), an advanced care practitioner (EDACP, a registered nurse, 22 years in practice, less than 1 year in emergency care), a foundation year 2 doctor in emergency medicine (FY2, in the second year since qualifying, less than 1 week in the department), an emergency medicine consultant (EMC, 18 years in the department), and a medical specialty registrar (MSpR, in the sixth year since qualifying, in the first year of specialty training in medicine, 8 months at the hospital) (§2.14, 2.18, 2.21, 2.27, 2.30, 2.35).

**What they were doing**: the most ordinary class of work an emergency department does, sorting non-specific chest pain, investigating it, and deciding who stays. The report supplies the denominators. England sees about 2,500 acute dissections a year, and about 2,000 of those patients reach hospital alive; on that basis an average emergency department meets about 15 cases a year, which is about 1 in 400 of the patients who present with chest pain (§4.6.1.2). Chest pain accounts for about 5% of emergency attendances, and about 45% of those are eventually diagnosed as cardiac (§4.9.3.1). Mean time from arrival to diagnosis runs between 4 and 5 hours (§4.8.7.4), and diagnostic delay occurs in 16% to 40% of cases (§5.2.4). The mortality denominators are harder still: about 20% die before reaching any hospital, and about 50% die before reaching a specialist centre (§5.2.2).

The patient's path ran alongside those numbers. A 54-year-old man, ordinarily fit; at 18:00, while weightlifting, sudden retrosternal pain with nausea and a sense of impending death, the pain moving to between the shoulder blades; at 18:40 he rang NHS 111, the call handler followed the script and asked about the character of the pain (tearing, severe), he answered that it was severe and lasted no more than 15 to 20 seconds, then agreed to the description crushing, aching, band-like, and took 300 mg of aspirin (§2.2–2.8). At 19:04 an ambulance arrived, crewed principally by a first-year student paramedic (PM2); blood pressure in the two arms measured 127/61 and 114/84 mmHg, 19 minutes apart, with "no signs of abdominal aortic aneurysm" recorded; the ECG showed occasional ectopics and an enlarged cardiac shadow (§2.10–2.11). The crew agreed that everything pointed to muscle pain and conveyed him anyway, to rule out a myocardial infarction (§2.12). He reached AH1 at 20:04, was booked in at 20:12, and sat in the waiting area (§2.13).

**The decision point**: four irreversible moments.

Moment one, 20:46 to 20:49, triage. EDS entered "chest pain" into the electronic Manchester Triage System (MTS) with observations of pulse 128, blood pressure 108/47 and respiratory rate 17; the system assigned category 2 on a five-level scale, where category 2 requires assessment within 10 minutes, and she signed at 20:49 (§2.17, Appendix 1). After signing he was sent back to the waiting area and entered a cubicle only at about 21:00 (Appendix 1). The MTS chest pain flowchart contains no "aortic pain" discriminator, which is precisely what HSIB's first recommendation set out to add (recommendation R/2020/066). The carrier is the electronic triage record, and the signatory is EDS.

Moment two, 21:21, the first assessment entry. EDACP picked the case up from the electronic board, and triage gave no verbal handover (§2.22). After examining the patient he wrote "looks well", with a differential of musculoskeletal chest pain, chest infection and cardiac chest pain; a Wells score of 0, not suggestive of pulmonary embolism; neither palpation nor movement reproduced the pain, and he was unhappy with his own diagnosis, because the pain would not reproduce and the patient said he felt unwell (§2.23–2.26). The carrier is the 21:21 electronic record. He then handed the patient to FY2, verbally, and ended his shift.

Moment three, 22:48, FY2's entry. The chest X-ray was read as no obvious abnormality, troponin T was 30 ng/l against a normal range of 0–14, and D-dimer was above 3000 ng/ml against a normal range of 0–230; the record notes only negative findings for venous thromboembolism, and the abnormal values were written down without being taken anywhere (§2.28–2.29). At 22:58 an addendum records discomfort, back pain and vomiting (§2.34). Radiology reported the chest X-ray the following day as an enlarged cardiac shadow with a widened mediastinum (§2.28).

Moment four, 23:27, the imaging request. Once MSpR took over she said she had seen a similar case before, suspected dissection, asked for blood pressure in both arms (123 and 100 mmHg, a difference of 23) and requested an emergency CTA at 23:27 (§2.35–2.38); the scan ran at 00:01 and was formally reported at 01:06 as Stanford type A, with the tear extending to the right common iliac artery and the right brachiocephalic artery (§2.42–2.44).

**What was visible then**: there is no cockpit here, only consulting rooms, and what could be seen was just as limited. The list follows the order of contact.

At the triage desk, 20:46 to 20:49, EDS had the MTS electronic flowchart in front of her: select the presenting complaint "chest pain", click through the items, and the system assigns a category by algorithm. The evidence she could enter was four observations, pulse 128, blood pressure 108/47, respiratory rate 17 and a pain score of 1/10, together with the patient's colour and posture as he sat in the waiting area. She remembered afterwards that she had looked once and thought he was in a bad way (§2.15). The system returned category 2, to be seen within 10 minutes (§2.17). The flowchart has no aortic pain line, and no line anywhere asks for a comparison of the two arms.

At the assessment desk, 21:20 to 21:26, EDACP had no handover, only the triage note and the electronic board (§2.22). What he could see was a complete history (weightlifting, sudden onset, 10 to 15 seconds, left arm and back, a sense of impending death), one examination (neither palpation nor movement reproduced the pain), a Wells score table calculated at 0, and the patient's appearance: looks well, no pain face, no sign of circulatory failure (§2.23–2.24). Of those four, the last was the most persuasive and the least reliable, because it fitted muscle strain after exercise exactly.

At the handover desk, 22:48, FY2 had three new items, the chest X-ray she read as no obvious abnormality, troponin T of 30 and a D-dimer above 3000 (§2.28). She could also see how the patient had changed over three hours: walking about, pale, sweating, vomiting, back pain (§2.33–2.34). Her seniority was second year since qualifying and less than 1 week in the department (§2.27). Of the three new items, two were abnormal and one had been judged normal, and the report records that her entry carried only the negative findings that excluded venous thromboembolism (§2.29).

At the consultant's end, around 22:50, EMC was the most senior person in the department with 18 years behind him. His information on this case amounted to one telephone call: he went to the bedside, the patient was in the toilet, and the examination did not happen (§2.30). His advice to FY2 was that abnormal blood results were worth worrying about, that she should speak to the medical registrar, and that admission might be needed (§2.31).

At the diagnostic end, 23:00 to 23:27, MSpR held the most unusual information of the five: one precedent, a similar case seen before, plus systolic pressures of 123 and 100, a difference of 23 mmHg (§2.35–2.37). In nurse training that figure is taught as significant once it passes 20, and it did not reach the record until 23:21.

What was learned only afterwards: the chest X-ray had in fact shown an enlarged cardiac shadow and a widened mediastinum (§2.28); the CTA showed the dissection extending along most of the aorta to the right common iliac artery and the right brachiocephalic artery (§2.44); scored retrospectively on the ADD-RS he would have had 1 point (§4.9.5.3); and his four hours in the department were shorter than the national mean of 4 to 5 hours (§4.8.7.4).

**What happened**: at 01:57 the transfer vehicle left. About 12 minutes later breathing stopped and the heart stopped soon after; the vehicle diverted to another hospital to collect blood, and on arrival received a message from the regional centre to come immediately, so it turned around without the patient ever leaving the vehicle. The regional centre said that after 32 minutes of CPR nothing more could be done, and death was declared at 03:15 (§2.62–2.67). HSIB made two recommendations: that the Manchester Triage international reference group add "aortic pain" to MTS as a chest pain discriminator, and that the Royal College of Emergency Medicine and the Royal College of Radiologists jointly develop, deploy and evaluate a national recognition and management pathway (R/2020/066, R/2020/067). There were also three safety observations and one safety action, under which NHS Pathways amended the telephone triage algorithm in version 18 (§5.2). Back to the three questions. The most common answer to the first is "more training for emergency doctors". Six people were involved in this case and three of them had more than ten years of experience, while the one who recognized the dissection was in his sixth year since qualifying with a single precedent for this disease to draw on. Training cannot change an exposure of 15 cases a year per person. The item most often missed under the second question is the denominator of 1 in 400 chest pain patients: it says that practice does not make perfect with this disease, and every judgment that the patient looked well was made on that premise. The checkpoint for the third question belongs within 10 minutes of triage: calculate the ADD-RS once, and arrange imaging at 1 point or above.

**Options ruled out**: adding "aortic pain" to the triage table. The MTS chest pain table had no such item at the time; HSIB made the recommendation in January 2020, and the Manchester Triage international reference group replied on 17 April 2020 that it had been added to four flowcharts, chest pain, back pain, abdominal pain and collapse, with a definition attached ("sudden onset, presenting with severe abdominal or chest pain, which may be sharp, stabbing or tearing..."). A national diagnostic pathway. None existed at the time; the report recommended that the two colleges develop one, and the reply said the working group planned to publish guidance in 2021. Routine CTA for all chest and back pain. The report discusses the cost of that route, which requires balancing detection rate against CTA capacity, and it therefore argues for screening first with a score and D-dimer (§4.9.5.2, 4.9.6.3). The ADD-RS itself. The tool was published and available as an online calculator at the time, and the report's retrospective calculation for this case comes to 1 point (§4.9.5.3); it was never outside the options, it simply went unused. Controlling blood pressure and heart rate before transfer. The report records that this was considered and given up, to save time and to avoid needing a doctor in the vehicle (§5.1.13), while the ICU consultant argued against delay on the grounds that the disease carries a 10% hourly risk of death (§2.50).

**Early signals**: ① about 2,500 cases a year nationally, with about half dying before reaching a specialist centre (§5.2.2); the reason it was waved through: the disease is intrinsically lethal, and the deaths were booked to the disease. ② neither palpation nor movement reproduced the pain (§2.24); the reason it was waved through: musculoskeletal pain stayed in the differential, and the report describes a tendency not to abandon that diagnosis while exploring more serious cardiopulmonary problems (§5.1.5). ③ troponin of 30 and a D-dimer above 3000 (§2.28); the reason it was waved through: the record said only that venous thromboembolism was excluded, and the abnormal values were never converted into a next action (§2.29). ④ the chest X-ray judged as no obvious abnormality, then read the next day as an enlarged cardiac shadow and a widened mediastinum (§2.28); the reason it was waved through: the on-shift read is taken as the conclusion, and a chest X-ray is a poor test for dissection in any case (§5.1.8). ⑤ the patient walking about the department, saying he felt unwell, vomiting, with back pain (§2.33–2.34); the reason it was waved through: observations and oxygen saturation were normal and he looked well (§2.23, 2.29). ⑥ triaged as category 2 yet placed in the low-dependency area (§5.1.4); the reason it was waved through: category 2 sets a 10-minute assessment time and says nothing about the level of monitoring.

**The point of escalation**: 22:48. Three lines lay in the record at the same moment, pain that would not reproduce, a D-dimer above 3000 and a troponin of 30, and what FY2 wrote was a direction, chase the bloods and possibly home, resting on a chest X-ray reported as no obvious abnormality (§2.27–2.28). Before that moment, anyone taking the case over who laid the three side by side would have moved toward imaging; after it, the record carried a direction written as exclusion, and later people followed the exclusion.

From that moment to the CTA request at 23:27 lie 39 minutes, and the report records what filled them: EMC advised referral to medicine, FY2 called the medical registrar, went back to the patient and found him vomiting, and the patient's partner also agreed that something was wrong (§2.31–2.32). The force that overturned the direction came from the eye at the bedside, not from the record. The carrier is the 22:48 electronic entry; the step needed nobody's approval and got none. EDACP had gone off shift, and EMC was consulted by telephone only after this point (§2.26, 2.31).

**Failure chain**: technical layer: the symptoms are non-specific, since 85% of patients have sudden severe chest or back pain that can be described as tearing or as crushing (§1.9); diagnosis rests on imaging alone (§5.2.6); a chest X-ray is unsuited to detecting dissection (§5.1.8); and a dissection can extend from the ascending aorta to the iliac arteries within hours (§2.44). Organizational layer: the triage table lacks an aortic pain item, and the existence of recommendation R/2020/066 is the evidence; triage to assessment had no verbal handover (§2.22); for three hours the patient was carried by EDACP and FY2 while the consultant never examined him (§5.1.6); between diagnosis and transfer there is a further wait of more than an hour for the formal report (§5.1.11); and nationally there is no usable incidence and outcome data, which the report makes a safety observation in its own right (O/2020/053). Incentive layer: chest pain is about 5% of emergency attendances and about 45% of it is cardiac, so throughput and priority sit on that side; the report records that junior doctors may be reluctant to request a CTA out of concern about its availability (§4.9.4.5); and transfer time pressure overrode treatment before transfer (§2.50, §5.1.13). All three layers broke at once. The interface: the epidemiological data is in the report and the checklist line is not, and no post existed to translate data into a line. The two recommendations were meant to create that post, and at the time it had no name.

**Minimum counterfactual**: before 22:48, calculate the ADD-RS for this patient. The score covers three classes of high-risk feature, predisposing conditions, pain features and examination features, on a 0 to 3 scale; here it comes to 1 point retrospectively, which in the published literature means considering a CTA, even without a D-dimer result at the time (§4.9.5.3). One action, taken in any minute after triage, needing no luck: the score is published with an online calculator, and it measures three facts that can be asked for, a family history of Marfan syndrome or aortic disease, whether the pain was sudden and severe, and an absent pulse or an inter-arm blood pressure difference. Nor does it require the person calculating to trust his own intuition, because the score moves "should I suspect this" from feeling to counting.

**Outcome and sources**: the patient died at 03:15 the following day, and HSIB issued 2 recommendations, 3 safety observations and 1 safety action (§5.2). One of the report's central findings is blunt: during his time in the department the patient never saw a consultant, and was seen by an advanced care practitioner and an FY2 (§5.1.6). Another is equally blunt: a four-hour delay from arrival to diagnosis is not unusual with this disease (§5.1.10). The inability to produce national incidence and outcome data is a gap the investigation admits to itself (O/2020/053), and the gap is the physical evidence for criterion 2, since a system that cannot count its own numerator is in no position to give its judges feedback. Primary source: HSIB, Delayed recognition of acute aortic dissection, 23 January 2020, link at Appendix B me-015 (archived link, grade A−); this card cites by paragraph number (§) because the report is web-only and sets no page numbers.

The ending of case one is worth a second look, specifically the pilot's self-report that he suspected the runway width illusion had affected him. That report is rare twice over. Survivors of an illusion usually attribute the incident to the wind, the light or how they were feeling that day, and blaming an illusion amounts to admitting that perception itself was wrong, an admission that needs the help of evidence afterwards. Rarer still is blaming the illusion rather than the state of the day, because it shows that the man completed the upgrade from "I made an error" to "human perception errs on narrow runways". After that upgrade the experience remains usable, carrying a note on its range of validity from then on. That is what Device 2 is meant to produce in quantity: experts who know where their confidence comes from, with the confidence intact and the annotation attached.

#### Case 6 (Healthcare): Judgment Alone on Non-Specific Symptoms (Condensed)

me-013. Diagnosis of pulmonary embolism (PE) in an English emergency department. The HSIB investigation report (March 2022, archive snapshot read for this book) drew on 14 serious incident reports, concluded that individual judgment under non-specific symptoms lacked decision support, and made 3 recommendations. The symptom spectrum of PE (breathlessness, chest pain, a fast heart rate) overlaps several common diagnoses, and clinical intuition carried far too much of the sorting load: the judge's experience holds few PE samples (criterion 3), the symptom environment is irregular (criterion 1), and the outcome feedback is broken (criterion 2). The decision point is the moment of individual judgment with no decision support tool present. Minimum counterfactual: embed a standardised scoring tool in the emergency pathway and make its use mandatory. Primary source: HSIB, Clinical decision making: diagnosis of pulmonary embolism in emergency departments, 2022, link at Appendix B me-013 (grade A−).

A number in case two deserves a paragraph of its own: 11 administrative controls. The figure looks generous, and taken apart item by item it is all the same material, procedures, training and prompts, every one of them resting on people having stable attention and stable recognition. Dense stacking of administrative controls has a name in this chapter's frame: transferring the safety load of the system onto people, and then treating human reliability as an input assumption. When the CSB lists reliance on administrative controls as one of its two major safety issues, an investigating body is confirming that the transfer failed, and confirming it at its own level of authority. A conversion rule for the reader: for each administrative control you meet, ask once what guarantees its effectiveness. If the answer comes back to human attention, the control's real weight in that system is close to zero, unless the system has also provided a backup for attention, in the form of interlocks, fault tolerance or a forced wait.

#### Case 7 (Aviation): Three Minutes in Cloud for a VFR Pilot (Condensed)

av-014. A Cessna FR 182 on a private ferry flight, February 2021, Germany. The BFU investigation report (2022, read for this book): the flight entered cloud under visual flight rules, spatial disorientation led to loss of control, the aircraft struck the ground, and the pilot was killed. The environmental premise of visual flight is continuous visual reference to the ground, and cloud removes that premise entirely; with no ground reference the pilot's felt sense of attitude returns systematically wrong answers. This is criterion 1 in its most complete form, since the environment changes wholesale the instant the aircraft enters cloud. The decision point is the decision to keep flying visually in front of a gap in the cloud. Minimum counterfactual: check the route forecast during pre-flight planning, and set a personal line of "no going visual". Primary source: BFU, 2022, link at Appendix B av-014.

Cases five to seven push each of the three criteria to an extreme. Case five is the textbook for criteria 2 and 3, broken feedback plus a low-frequency scenario. Case six is a triple failure zone, with all three criteria satisfied at once. Case seven is the instantaneous, full version of criterion 1, the environment replaced wholesale inside the second it takes to enter cloud. The value of the criteria lies in how they combine. One criterion on its own can be negotiated, through training, experience or remediation; when two or more hold together, any confidence in intuition should be counted as zero until an instrument or a checklist is in place.

Combining them has a second use, which is profiling posts. Run the three criteria over each key judgment post in your organization and most will land in a stable combination. Scheduling and triage posts, with high judgment frequency and fast feedback, usually lack only the environmental upkeep of criterion 1. Purchasing and investment posts, with slow feedback, lack criterion 2. Emergency and safety posts, with once-in-a-lifetime events, lack criterion 3. The combination type decides the combination of defences: a post short of criterion 1 gets a change trigger, short of criterion 2 gets records and review, short of criterion 3 gets instruments and checklists. Draw that map across the whole organization and the budget for intuition defences has an ordering for the first time, with the posts carrying the heaviest combination going first.

The chain rescue in case three deserves a second look as a domain general rule. Serial deaths during rescue from an oxygen-deficient confined space are a recurring category in marine investigation: the first man goes down to the environment, and the second and third go down to the rescue instinct. The mechanism is at its purest in this category. Saving people is one of the strongest social instincts humans have, it is almost always right in open air, and it is fed by dense feedback in a stable environment; a confined space turns the environment's single critical parameter, oxygen, into something that cannot be sensed, and the whole premise of the intuition is pulled away while the surface scene does not change at all. The MAIB report attributes the three deaths to the act of entering the enclosed space itself, which amounts to a ruling: in this setting the correct action is a veto on intuition, and the equipment (a gas detector) and the procedure (call first, then enter) exist to carry out that veto.

The under-deduction in case four reads differently under each criterion. The old calculation table is the specimen for path two, a conversion convention carried through years of tender offers. The new instrument form, short call options appearing in the consideration of an offer, is the rule change of path three, and the internal procedure does not define how the new form converts, which is the system admitting at its own level that judgment is still running on the old library. FINRA's penalty, $80,000 of which $40,000 went to FINRA, is small in scale; the value of the case is its generality. Financial instruments are innovated at a pace that runs steadily ahead of the pace at which internal definitions are updated, and the gap between the two paces is the interval in which this class of case is generated. Device 3's new column on the change form, the list of existing experience affected, is designed for exactly that interval: the day a new position type enters the system, the review of the conversion definition should be triggered.

The risk that went into the PHA with no independent guarantee mechanism deserves one further step along the administrative-control audit. After all 11 controls have been asked what guarantees their effectiveness, most organizations' lists shrink to two or three lines, and what survives is the set of items that carry a genuine independent guarantee. The shrinking is not alarming. It makes the real water level of risk control visible for the first time. What follows visibility is the watershed: add engineering controls, which are expensive, slow and effective, or accept the level and write it into the risk assessment, which is cheap and honest. The worst choice is to leave the list untouched, because the gap between the paper water level and the real one is the stock of future accidents.

One organizational detail in case two is worth enlarging: an operator without experience was placed in an independent post on the grounds that he had completed standard training. That sentence quietly merges two concepts, training hours and intuition built. Standard training delivers explicit knowledge, the rules, the sequence, how to read a label; intuition needs repeated exposure in the environment plus feedback, and the two take construction periods an order of magnitude apart. Scheduling pressure looks only at the first, because the first has a certificate attached that can be checked. A corresponding check for the reader's own organization: in the roster for a key post, is the basis for working independently a certificate or a count of exposures? The first is the system's assumption, and the second is the system's reality.

#### Case 4 (Finance): The Short Options Left Out of the Deduction (Condensed)

fi-012. Cowen and Company. The FINRA penalty of August 2026 (monthly summary PDF read for this book): in a partial tender offer the internal procedure did not define how 100,000 short call options should be converted into a net long position, the calculation followed the old convention, the firm over-reported by 150,000 shares, and the penalty was $80,000, of which $40,000 went to FINRA. The decision point is the moment the net long calculation met a new instrument form. Minimum counterfactual: the internal procedure triggers a review of the conversion definition automatically when a new position type is added. Primary source: FINRA, Disciplinary and Other FINRA Actions – October 2026, 2026, link at Appendix B fi-012.

What the four cases share is this: every judge was fully confident at the moment of judging, and the source of that confidence, visual experience, post common sense, the rescue instinct, the old calculation table, lay inside the range of criterion 1 or criterion 3. Recognizing a failure zone does not require doubting the judge's intelligence. It requires checking which environment the experience behind the judgment was formed in.

## 4 What Can Be Seen Earliest

Eight weak signals, each with the reason that usually waves it through.

1. The reasoning behind a decision contains analogy rather than measurement ("this is much like that one back then"). Reason given: "experience is the fastest theory there is."
2. The environment's rules, equipment or population changed recently, and the people in the key posts did not. Reason: "core competence is transferable."
3. A veteran answers data in a meeting with seniority ("I have done this for twenty years"). Reason: "respect for practice."
4. A class of decision has never had feedback: it was made, and nobody has ever checked whether it was right. Reason: "it has always run fine."
5. Training hours get compressed on the grounds that the system will handle it. Reason: "efficiency." (Chapter 26 develops this.)
6. A newcomer asks a basic question and the experts present answer vaguely. Reason: "the detail has gone; the direction I am certain of."
7. The induction period for someone crossing domains is an order of magnitude shorter than the period their old domain took to build. Reason: "he was brought in to step straight in."
8. The organization keeps no record of its expert judgment: the judgment was made, the basis was not written down, and afterwards nothing can be calibrated. Reason: "the business moves fast."

Items 4 and 8 together are the definition of calibration. No record means no feedback, no feedback means no calibration, and confidence without calibration is only temperament.

Put the eight signals back against this chapter's cases. Signal 1, analogy instead of measurement, maps onto case one, where the sense of when to flare is analogy in its fastest form. Signal 2, environment changed while the post did not, maps onto case two, where the new three-valve screen met an operator population that had not changed. Signal 3, seniority answering data, appears in case five in a variant: experience answering checklisting, with the checklist read as distrust of clinical judgment. Signal 4, decisions without feedback, maps onto case five, where the missed diagnosis killed the patient after transfer and the emergency department never heard about it. Signal 5, compressed training, maps onto case two, where administrative controls replaced depth of training. Signal 7, induction too short across domains, maps onto case two's new operator. Signal 8, unrecorded judgment, maps onto all of them, since the basis of an intuitive judgment cannot be reconstructed after the event.

Read by discovery cost, the eight signals have a reading peculiar to this chapter. Signals 1, 3 and 6 come by ear, in meetings and conversations. Signals 2, 5 and 7 come by eye, from the organization chart and the roster. Signals 4 and 8 come by checking records. A failure-zone audit is therefore a division of labour among three senses: hear the analogies and the seniority talk, see the mismatch between environment and people, check for the absence of feedback and records. The three correspond to a conversation audit, a post audit and an archive audit, and any one of them done alone covers a third of the ground.

The conversation audit has three opening questions for a reader who wants to start on Monday. Ask the expert: "if this judgment is wrong, when is the earliest you would know?" A judgment that cannot name a time has no feedback loop, and criterion 2 lights up. Ask the expert: "when did you last meet a case of this kind?" An answer of never puts the judgment under criterion 3, to be handled as a hypothesis awaiting verification. Ask the team: "if someone more junior made this judgment, how would he get it wrong?" The list of errors in the answer is the list of experience the judgment actually rests on, and the longer that list, the more exposed the judgment is to the failure zone. None of the three needs a tool. All three need a questioner who is not under seniority pressure.

The standard move in an archive audit is to sample judgments, not events. Draw a number of significant judgments at random from the past year and ask one thing about each: how much of the basis was recorded. Most organizations doing this for the first time come out with a glaring empty set, which is not a scandal but a starting point, since the empty set says the organization has no recording system for its judgment assets, and weak signal 8 has something to remediate at last. Sampling judgments reads cleaner than sampling events, because an event arrives with its own demand for attribution, while a judgment sits quietly inside the process and reflects the organization's real habits best.

## 5 How to Defend Against It

Device 1: an intuition admission form. Add one mandatory line to the review form for significant judgments: "Does the experience this judgment rests on hold in the current environment (rules, equipment, people)? What is the basis?" A judgment that cannot produce a basis is downgraded to a hypothesis awaiting verification, under the tiered release of Chapter 13. The cost is very low, one line. What it stops is the whole class of failure zones.

Device 2: convert intuition into a checklist. A veteran's intuition is an asset, so bring it out and write it as explicit check items, which lets newcomers benefit and lets the veteran be checked. The method is interview extraction: have the expert talk through what he is looking at while he works, and the fixation points recorded become the candidates for check items. Whatever will not go down on paper is marked as the non-reliable part and signed by the expert himself. The signature is the step that matters, because it turns "only tacitly known" from a disclaimer into a risk statement.

Device 3: environmental change triggers retraining. For every change of rules, equipment or interface, list the intuitions that depend on the old environment and assess item by item whether retraining is needed. The interface change in the Shell Monaca case and the instrument innovation in the Cowen case should both have triggered this step. The trigger's carrier is the change form: add a column reading "list of existing experience affected", to be filled in by whoever raises the change.

Device 4: give the failure zone an instrument. A four-gas detector for a confined space, approach aids for a narrow runway, automatic conversion for a net long position: inside a failure zone, replace recognition with measurement. The budget ordering rule is to start with scenes where criterion 1 holds, since failure there is the most complete.

Now put the four devices onto this chapter's cases. Device 1 on case one: add one mandatory line to the approach briefing, "how far is this runway's width from the usual one, and how does the flare reference change". One line, twenty seconds, and what it stops is the entire class of perceptual illusion. Device 2 on case two: ask an experienced operator to say aloud what he is distinguishing while he works the three-valve screen, and write the fixation points down as check items; whatever will not go down gets his signature, "not reliable here", and after that signature the system must be fitted with a physical interlock. Device 3 on case five: every revision of a differential checklist lists the existing judgment habits it affects, and the triage group's habits are compared against it item by item. Device 4 on case six: embed the scoring tool in the emergency system and make its use mandatory, moving the sorting from recognition to measurement.

The failure modes of the devices need writing down as well. The admission form dies of inflation in the answer to "what is the basis", since if anything counts as a basis then nothing was asked. The checklist dies when nobody maintains it after extraction, with the environment moving again and the list standing still, which is where Chapter 15's fork grows. Change-triggered retraining dies when the "experience affected" column stays empty all year. Instrumentation dies when the instrument's alarm gets turned down for the sake of good relations. The four deaths have a single common antidote: put device use into the audit, and have the auditor ask what the device last stopped, leaving aside the question of whether it exists at all. A device that has never stopped anything means either that the environment really is safe or that the device is already dead, and the audit's job is to tell those two apart.

Together the four devices point to one organizational document worth establishing, the intuition asset register. Each row records one significant intuition held by one expert: the environment in which it holds, the check items extracted from it, its failure conditions, and the date it was last reviewed. Maintenance costs little, one pass against the change forms each quarter, and the return is that the organization holds an inventory view of its judgment assets for the first time: whose intuition has been validated in which environment, whose intuition is owed an extraction, and which posts' judgments have no register at all. The intuition asset register belongs to the same family as Chapter 11's deviation ledger and Chapter 12's definition register, since all three turn key states that were scattered across individuals into entries that can be searched, reviewed and handed over.

The order in which the register gets built matters. Start with the posts that hold few people and heavy judgments, such as emergency command, release signatures and investment decisions, then cover the posts that hold many people and scattered judgments, such as front-line operation and review. Every row in the first group can correspond to a major consequence, while the value of the second group is the statistical view that coverage gives. Most organizations start with the second group because people are easier to organize, and the register comes out thick with the critical judgments still unrecorded. The first iron rule of the register is to order by magnitude of consequence, not by headcount.

## 6 Boundaries with Adjacent Mechanisms

Against extrapolating past the validated domain (Chapter 23): extrapolation carries a conclusion into a new environment, at the data layer, while this chapter carries the judge into a new environment, at the person layer. When a scene occupies both, check the person first, meaning his experience premises, and the conclusion second, meaning its validation boundary. Fixing the person is cheap and fixing the conclusion is expensive.

Against practical drift (Chapter 15): drift is the fork between practice and text, an organizational phenomenon, while the failure zone is one person's recognition capacity crossing its edge, an individual phenomenon. A whole crew can drift together; a failure zone shows up only in specific people. The reverse holds too, since individual intuition inside a drifting organization can stay perfectly precise, precisely executing a practice that has already left the text behind.

Against all the talk that surrounds expert confidence: this chapter has nothing against experience; what it opposes is experience overstepping. The test is always the three criteria rather than an attitude toward experts, which means neither deferring to them nor dismissing what they say because of who says it.

## 7 The Five Self-Checks

1. Do the two premises, environmental regularity and feedback quality, still hold for the judgment you rely on most? What was the last change to either?
2. Whose data have you answered with "I have done this for twenty years"? Looking back, was that intuition or a failure zone?
3. After your team's last environmental change (new system, new rules, new population), which pieces of old experience were reassessed? If there is no list at all, where did the reassessment happen?
4. Which decisions in your post are once in a lifetime? What are they made with now, measurement or recognition?
5. Can you write one veteran's intuition as a checklist? Pick one and try it. Will he sign "not reliable here" over the parts that will not go down?
