# Chapter 27: Sunk Cost and Escalation (Staw)

> What this chapter covers: how money already spent takes the next decision hostage; Staw's classic experiment, the criteria, three field sites, and the design of exit conditions up front.
> When to turn back: at a project renewal review, on a motion to top up the budget, or when you hear "just a little longer".
> Reading time: 50 minutes.

The experimental evidence for the sunk cost effect is fifty years old. Barry Staw's 1976 experiment in *Organizational Behavior and Human Performance* ("Knee-deep in the big muddy", volume 16, pages 27–44, Crossref record verified) put subjects in the role of head of a corporate R&D project. Half were told the project had failed and the responsibility was theirs. The other half were told responsibility lay with other people or with circumstances. Everyone was then given the chance to decide whether to commit more. The result held, and it is uncomfortable: the group carrying the responsibility chose to commit significantly more often. Admitting the earlier misjudgement cost too much, so future money went on keeping past standing alive. Fifty years on, the conditions of that experiment can be reproduced as they stand in any boardroom, command post or project review.

## 27.1 What It Is

Definition: the sunk cost effect means a decision driven by past investment instead of future return, where the size of what has already gone in becomes itself the argument for putting more in. Chapter 8 split escalation into seven rungs (the process view: explain it away, shrink it, find a scapegoat, promise more, change the measure, enlarge the commitment, seal off information). This chapter returns to the decision point: why exit is so expensive in the mind and in the institution, and how to make it cheaper.

The criteria, all three present and the escalation structure stands:

① The investment already made is publicly visible, on the books, in buildings, in promises already published.

② The main argument for new money is "we have put in this much already", rather than a calculation of the return on the addition.

③ Exit is treated as an admission of error, and there is an identifiable person who pays for the admission.

Criterion ③ has an identity layer worth peeling back. Escalation is usually read as a question of face, and its actual structure is harder than face. Part of a sponsor's exchangeable value inside an organization is stored in the project's narrative: he is the man who built X, or the man building X. Exit is an asset write-down on that narrative, and what gets written down is not standing but the quotation on the job market. The structure explains why a transfer or a promotion so often fails to break escalation, since a new seat leaves the narrative where it was. Real unbinding waits for the narrative to depreciate by itself, as a newer project covers the older one, or it comes from Device 4 in this chapter, a reward for termination that makes exit a story worth telling. The two routes differ in cost by an order of magnitude, and which one an organization chooses is its true price for escalation.

Prospect theory, Kahneman and Tversky's loss aversion, supplies the last piece on the psychological side. Money already spent sits in the loss region of the mental account, and the decision curve in that region is not the curve in the gain region: sensitivity to further loss diminishes, so the larger the loss, the less each additional unit of it hurts. That is the shape of the escalation slope. The first top-up hurts most, and each one after it goes down more easily, because an account already that deep in loss makes each further unit of loss less noticeable. The implication for defense is exact. The exit window is widest before the first top-up, and once the first top-up is made the resistance to exit doubles. The trigger point for an exit clause (Device 1) therefore belongs at a node before topping up starts, at the project's original milestones rather than the middle of the budget cycle.

## 27.2 How It Grows

Three generating conditions.

Condition one: the decision-maker is bound to the project. The project is his record, his body of work or his career wager, so failure at the level of identity equals personal failure, and no accounting treatment settles a question of identity. The "responsibility is yours" manipulation in Staw's experiment corresponds inside an organization to the answer to one question: whose project is this.

Condition two: the exit mechanism is missing. The approval document carries no exit conditions, or carries them with an interested party as the arbiter. Exit can then only arrive as a coup, by changing the person, changing the accounting line or waiting out a term, and the organizational cost of a coup makes everyone prefer to wait.

Condition three: external oversight is weak. No party is both able and willing to ask the same question at every review: if we were starting from zero today, would we still choose this project. The question costs nothing and its frequency of appearance is close to zero.

The structural side needs one more observation. Missing exit has a plausible stand-in, and the stand-in is called adjustment. What separates adjustment from exit is the continuity of the account. Adjustment continues the same mental account, same project, different method. Exit closes the account, which means saying that this money stops here. Adjustment is therefore escalation's most respectable form: every adjustment opens a new story, the account never returns to zero, and the snowball of investment keeps rolling without ever getting smaller. The probe for respectable escalation is the age of the account. How long has this project's mental account been open, counted from the approval date, and how many times has it been adjusted, counted from the number of re-justifications. A project with a long account and many adjustments is probably feeding the substance of escalation on the respectability of adjustment. That reading goes straight into the zero-based re-ask (Device 3), where the age of the account is itself the first argument for re-asking.

The oversight side has a subtler point. In an organization with weak external oversight, the opponent of escalation faces the project's coalition rather than the project: the sponsor, the contractors, the incumbent suppliers, the regional interests. The coalition makes the exit review table unequal by construction, one voice against many. Device 2, the external adjudicator, is then more than a procedural arrangement, since it rebalances the table. An adjudicator with no binding lets the coalition's narrative advantage expire, because how many times you have said the project is good has no bearing on whether it should continue today. The deeper reading is that governing escalation is a question of seats rather than of persuasion, which is Chapter 17's seating logic on its third appearance in this book.

## 27.3 Field Sites

### Case 1 (Public): After Two Resets

**Case ID**: po-004.

**When and where**: February 2020 to May 2026, a timeline joined by three resets: February 2020, October 2023, January 2025. London, the Department for Transport and the HS2 Ltd head office; NAO fieldwork covered review, assurance and contract meetings at both bodies between March and May 2026 (NAO p.8).

**What and who**: the Department for Transport is the sponsor, holding the funding and the oversight; HS2 Ltd is the department's executive public body, responsible for design, construction and opening. In April 2026 a new company, Euston Delivery Company Limited, was set up to take over Euston station and the related works (NAO p.16, p.41). The auditor is the National Audit Office; the report was signed by the Comptroller and Auditor General, Gareth Davies, on 22 June 2026 and laid before the House of Commons (HC 52, NAO p.1, p.3). Responsibility is recorded by post: the Permanent Secretary at the Department for Transport, the Secretary of State for Transport, the chief executive of HS2 Ltd.

**What they were doing**: the third reset, launched in January 2025, was under way, with HS2 Ltd expecting to finish it in spring 2027. The reset has its own budget of £153 million, of which £101 million had been spent by the end of March 2026 (NAO p.18). Construction never stopped. Reset and construction ran in parallel, and four workstreams handled cost and schedule, supply chain and commercial, organizational capability and culture, and governance (NAO p.18, p.19). The cost and schedule workstream broke the project into 21 separate projects, fixed scope, schedule, cost and risk for each, merged them into a range, and had the result assured by an independent panel that included the Infrastructure and Projects Authority (NAO p.25). Five stages each carry a readiness review, and a stage that fails does not open the next one (NAO p.20, figure 3). The renegotiation of four civils joint venture contracts, begun in spring 2024, failed that year; 2025 brought a different approach, settling historic changes and actual costs first and renegotiating future spend afterwards, with about £500 million of non-allowable cost identified and a commercial reset aimed at about £2 billion of savings (NAO p.10, p.29). In January 2026 the Secretary of State asked HS2 Ltd to assess what a lower speed would save; in May 2026 the department agreed to cut the maximum operating speed from 360 km/h to 320 km/h, cancel automatic train operation and downgrade the signalling specification, an estimated saving of £1 billion to £2.5 billion against about £1.3 billion of long-term benefits lost (NAO p.9, p.25, p.26). In April 2026 Euston was separated from the main line; Euston works had cost £3.8 billion by the end of March and needed £4.1 billion more in public money (NAO p.44).

**The decision point**:

① February 2020, around the notice to proceed: the full business case for Phase One was submitted with a benefit-cost ratio of 1.2, sunk spending before the end of December 2019 excluded on Treasury rules, a cost estimate of £35 billion to £45 billion (2019 prices) and a funding envelope of £44.6 billion (2019 prices) (NAO p.22, p.27). The assessment also recorded that Phase One would make the rest of the network, and other rail projects, possible (NAO p.27). Signed by: the government, through the notice to proceed; on file: the accounting officer assessment of the Permanent Secretary at the Department for Transport.

② October 2023: the previous government cancelled Phase Two, ending the line north of Birmingham, including the western and eastern legs of 2a and 2b; the project that emerged was smaller in scope, more expensive and later (NAO p.16, p.17). The accounting officer assessment gave a benefit-cost ratio of 1.1 to 1.8, excluded £24.6 billion (2019 prices) of sunk spending to the end of September 2023, and deducted about £11 billion as the cost of undoing Phase One (NAO p.27); one month before the cancellation, the civils cost forecast had just risen by £6 billion (NAO p.29). Approved by: the government, announced to the Commons by the Secretary of State.

③ January 2025: the third reset began. In March 2025 the new chief executive finished a project assessment whose conclusions were that construction had started too early, that the contracts had not delivered the expected performance, and that the organization had not been set up to manage delivery effectively; the assessment listed four priorities and named capability gaps in contract management and assurance, with the delivery team short of people (NAO p.18, p.11). Approved by: the new chief executive of HS2 Ltd and the Permanent Secretary.

④ April to May 2026: Euston passed to the new company as a separate item; the department published a revised estimate of £87.7 billion to £102.7 billion and agreed the lower speed and the redesigned specification; the Permanent Secretary issued an accounting officer assessment with a benefit-cost ratio of 1.5 to 6.4, excluding £40.9 billion (nominal) of sunk spending to the end of September 2025 (NAO p.26, p.41, p.27). The same document produced a figure in the other direction for the first time: had the costs of today been known in 2020, the benefit-cost ratio would have been 0.3 to 0.4, which is poor value (NAO p.27). The formal baseline for the revised estimate still waits for a first version in autumn 2026 and a final one in spring 2027 (NAO p.25).

**What was visible then**:

In January 2020 the NAO report *High Speed Two: A progress update* (HC 40) had already warned about cost and governance, and stated that schedule and cost had both drifted (NAO p.16, footnote 3). When the notice to proceed was issued, that report and the £35 billion to £45 billion range were in hand at the same time (NAO p.16, p.22). In September 2023 HS2 Ltd's own estimate showed main civils works alone up £6 billion (2019 prices) on 2020 (NAO p.29). In April 2024 the department formally notified HS2 Ltd under the Development Agreement that it was in breach of its delivery obligations, a contract-level document rather than an internal judgement (NAO p.20, figure 3, note 2). That same year, with the four civils joint ventures in renegotiation, HS2 Ltd had no reliable total cost estimate to negotiate with (NAO p.29). In June 2025 the Stewart Review reported that the governance structure was not fit for purpose and that a different arrangement was needed (NAO p.21, footnote 5). In February 2026, 45 of the 124 recommendations from four external reviews had been implemented, 36 per cent (NAO p.21). In May 2026 the cost of cancelling Phase One was estimated at £33 billion to £58 billion, comparable to completing it, and HS2 Ltd itself described this as a highly uncertain high-level estimate with no comparable project as a benchmark; in the same assessment the case for the government to carry on was written as a benefit-cost ratio above 1.5 rather than the range (NAO p.27, p.28, p.26).

What was learned afterwards: opening dates fall between May 2036 and October 2039 (Old Oak Common to Birmingham) and between May 2040 and December 2043 (Euston to Birmingham and the connection north to the West Coast Main Line), three to 13 years later than the 2020 plan; the cost increase decomposes into underestimation 32 per cent, inefficient delivery 23 per cent, scope change 11 per cent and inflation 34 per cent (NAO p.23, p.24, p.23 figure 4). Both items come from the body of the June 2026 report.

**What happened**: by the end of March 2026 the department and HS2 Ltd had spent £46.8 billion (nominal), of which £2.6 billion went on the cancelled Phase Two (NAO p.22). The revised cost to complete is £87.7 billion to £102.7 billion, with HS2 Ltd's own target at £93.2 billion; against the 2020 estimate of £35 billion to £45 billion, the figure is still up £35.9 billion to £37.2 billion after inflation, a rise of more than 100 per cent at the low end (NAO p.22). Full opening is three to 13 years later than planned in 2020 (NAO p.23). The Phase Two shutdown carries a bill of its own: about 560 properties to be disposed of, a disposal programme costing about £9 million, with about £170 million expected back (NAO p.47). The audit conclusion is that both bodies are avoiding further cost increases and delay, but that the reset will not be complete until spring 2027, and this time it has to be done right (NAO p.14). Back to the three questions. The most common answer to the first is another reset review with a fresh baseline; in this case that action is priced at the £153 million reset budget plus the time to spring 2027 (NAO p.18). The item most easily missed in the second is the £33 billion to £58 billion cancellation estimate, which looks like a technical parameter and is in fact the body of the argument for continuing. The third lands on the October 2023 cancellation: had the from-zero benefit-cost ratio been put on the table beside the continuation option, 0.3 to 0.4 would have appeared two and a half years earlier.

**Options ruled out**: cancel the whole line, which the May 2026 accounting officer assessment does treat as a formal counterfactual, offset by the £33 billion to £58 billion cancellation cost, a magnitude obtained by dividing that cost by the future cost, where the cancellation cost itself has no comparable benchmark (NAO p.26, p.27, p.28); keep 360 km/h and automatic train operation, dropped after the assessment between January and May 2026 on the testing cost against the £1 billion to £2.5 billion saving (NAO p.25, p.26); dispose of all the Phase Two land, with the Secretary of State announcing in January 2026 that 2a would be held for a future north-south line and more than 350 compensation cases settled by HS2 Ltd (NAO p.13, p.47); pause construction and restart once the baseline is set, not adopted, since reset and construction continue in parallel and the Euston tunnels were still being bored in early 2026 (NAO p.18, figure 3, note 1; p.16); run the civils contracts on their original terms without renegotiation, replaced after the 2024 failure by the 2025 approach of settling historic changes first and negotiating future spend afterwards (NAO p.10, p.29); treat the £1.3 billion of lost benefits from the lower speed as grounds for rejection, with the loss written into the assessment and the conclusion unchanged (NAO p.26); keep Phase Two alive on paper and decide after Phase One, where the October 2023 cancellation was described as final and the new government reintroduced the 2b western leg bill in July 2024 for a different line (NAO p.17, figure 1, note 3).

**Early signals**: ① January 2020, the NAO progress report naming cost and governance risk, overridden by the project's cross-party support and a notice to proceed already issued. ② November 2021, the Integrated Rail Plan cutting the eastern leg of Phase Two, so that Leeds would no longer be reached directly by HS2 but through the East Midlands Parkway direction, filed as a scope optimization with the project continuing as a whole (NAO p.17, figure 1, note 1). ③ September 2023, the main civils cost forecast up £6 billion, overridden as something a reset and a contract renegotiation could absorb, with the same contracts failing at renegotiation in 2024 (NAO p.29, p.10). ④ April 2024, the breach notice, filed as an organizational capability problem that left scope and schedule untouched, the breach standing until the reset is complete (NAO p.20, figure 3, note 2). ⑤ March 2025, the chief executive's assessment stating that construction had started too early, split across four workstreams and taken as input to the reset rather than as a trigger to stop (NAO p.18). That last sentence reads afterwards like an exit condition; at the time it was read as a diagnosis for the reset. What the five share is a translation. Every one became a way to continue better, and none became a question of whether to continue. The third is the cleanest: £6 billion is HS2 Ltd's own number, one month before Phase Two was cancelled, and it was used to support continuation. Laid side by side, the five compress one standard of judgement into five forms: what counts as under control.

**The point of escalation**: May 2026, when the Permanent Secretary signed the accounting officer assessment that wrote the cancellation cost into the continuation case's benefit-cost ratio as a deduction (NAO p.26, p.27). Three numbers hold the step in place. The cost of undoing Phase One was about £11 billion when estimated in 2023 and £33 billion to £58 billion in 2026, comparable to completing it (NAO p.27). Spending rose over the same period from £24.6 billion (end September 2023, 2019 prices) to £40.9 billion (end September 2025, nominal) (NAO p.27). The NAO states plainly that this cancellation estimate has no comparable benchmark (NAO p.28). The escalation point sits here: the first assessment in which the price of exit was put on a level with the price of completion. The date is May 2026; the action was publishing the revised estimate and the lower-speed redesign; the approvers were the Permanent Secretary, through the accounting officer assessment, and the Secretary of State, through the January instruction on speed. Every top-up after this is argued from an exit price that keeps rising, rather than from new benefit. The three numbers point the same way: the more that has gone in, the dearer exit becomes, the more rational continuation looks, and every one of the numbers was measured by the side arguing for continuation. The report also notes that the fourfold rise in the cancellation cost is a significant factor in the rising benefit-cost ratio (NAO p.28).

**Failure chain**: technical layer: the scope and quantities of the main civils were added to repeatedly after work started, and 66 per cent of the cost increase came from something other than inflation, namely underestimation 32 per cent, inefficient delivery 23 per cent and scope change 11 per cent, where scope change means necessary works that the original scope had missed (NAO p.23, figure 4, note 3). Organizational layer: governance was judged not fit for purpose by the Stewart Review, whose 28 governance recommendations were more than half implemented by February 2026 with the rest in progress; before that, a decision had to pass HS2 Ltd internal assurance, the department's approval points, IPA review and Cabinet Office and Treasury approval, a chain up to nine months long that rarely changed anything substantive (NAO p.11, p.36). The April 2024 breach notice and the 2025 organizational assessment point at the same thing, that the body was not built to manage work at this volume (NAO p.18, p.20). In February 2026, 45 of 124 external review recommendations had been implemented (NAO p.21). Incentive layer: the argument for continuing strengthens as construction advances, and the price of exit rises as construction advances, with both measured by the same people. Reset money is booked to the continuation account; cancellation money exists as an estimate with no benchmark, and the NAO says so (NAO p.18, p.27, p.28). The 2023 cancellation was made by one government, and in July 2024 the next one reintroduced the 2b western leg bill (NAO p.17). The break sits between the organizational and incentive layers: the people entitled to reprice exit are the people delivering the continuation, so no seat at the review table holds an interest on the exit side, and while the accounts are updated every quarter the seats never change.

**Minimum counterfactual**: in October 2023, when Phase Two was cancelled, add one line to the Permanent Secretary's accounting officer assessment: the benefit-cost ratio recomputed from zero today, set beside the continuation option. One action, inside the month after the decision to continue, and no luck required. The department showed in May 2026 that the number can be produced, and produced 0.3 to 0.4 (NAO p.27). The same demand model and the same cost estimates were in hand at the time; what was missing was one more pass with sunk spending taken out of the denominator. The line costs a page. It would not have stopped the project by itself. It would have put 0.3 to 0.4 into the parliamentary record two and a half years earlier. The difference those two and a half years make is where the record sits: the 2023 figure would have been in the same document as the Phase Two cancellation, without waiting for the auditor to recompute it in 2026. Where the worry is overreach, the line can stand as a footnote that changes no conclusion, with the date and the signatory untouched. The line is the zero-based re-ask in written form.

**Outcome and sources**: the project continues, with potential cost up to £102.7 billion and full opening pushed into the 2040s (NAO p.22, p.23). Primary source: Comptroller and Auditor General, *High Speed Two reset*, Session 2026-27, HC 52, National Audit Office, 29 June 2026 (signed 22 June 2026, Commons printing order 25 June, NAO p.1, p.3). The report runs to 56 pages and carries 8 recommendations, on re-evaluating the reset timetable, capability gaps, cost and commercial management, train formation and network integration, governance adjusting by stage, and benefits strategy (NAO p.15). The previous HS2 report was published in July 2024, when the funding envelope was still £44.6 billion (2019 prices); between the two reports sit two scope changes and one change of government (NAO p.8, p.22). The audit scope covers the planning, assurance and implementation of the reset, and excludes integration with the existing network after completion (NAO p.8). Page numbers in this card are PDF pages; the link is in Appendix B under po-004.

The HS2 numbers deserve one pass with this chapter's scalpel. The ratio between £46.8 billion already spent and a potential ceiling of £102.7 billion is criterion ① (visible investment) in its national form: at that size the visible commitment makes the word stop hard to pronounce in any parliament, since the cost of cancelling is counted in tens of billions, and escalation becomes the default across successive governments. The structural value of the NAO report is that it has written the decision chain of each reset into a public record, and that record can be laid on the table at any future escalation review. The auditor's role in governing escalation is visible from this. It cannot block a decision, since democratic authority sits with the government, but it can make the ledger of each decision permanently readable, and readability is a deterrent, because the next government faces a recorded history one chapter longer.

### Case 2 (Aviation): After the Runway Change

**Case ID**: av-025.

**When and where**: 27 December 2025, 1130 UTC (British winter time, the same locally); Talgarth Airfield, Powys, Wales, the northwest runway and the field adjoining it to the west. The airfield elevation is 970 feet and the northwest runway is 360 metres long. This was the first tow of the day, before gliding operations began (AAIB p.1, p.2, p.7).

**What and who**: towing operations at the Black Mountains Gliding Club. The tug was a Eurofox 2K, registration G-CMAX, with a Rotax 915 iS engine, club-owned and used only for towing gliders (AAIB p.1, p.5); the ASK13 two-seat glider on the rope carried two licensed gliding instructors (AAIB p.2). Responsibility by post: the duty instructor, who assessed the day's flying conditions; the tug pilot, commander of the combination until release. Guidance comes from the British Gliding Association (BGA); the investigator was the AAIB field investigation team.

**What they were doing**: flying started the way the club usually starts. The morning briefing was run by the duty instructor and covered the weather and the day's threats; the site briefing pack and video were mandatory viewing, and the airfield sits on the western slope of the Black Mountains, with ground shaped like an upturned saucer, smaller than most gliding sites and prone to ridge rotor turbulence (AAIB p.7). The airfield has no official weather source and a short runway, and conditions in the takeoff area are sensitive to rain; the club assembles the day's conditions from hillwalking forecasts, the Met Office aviation pages, two webcams and the clubhouse weather station (AAIB p.6, p.7). The plan was to take off to the east, into wind. During setup the takeoff area was found wet and soft and unusable (AAIB p.1). The duty instructor and the tug pilot conferred and switched to the northwest runway, accepting a 90° crosswind (AAIB p.1–2). The glider was moved to the new takeoff area. At the same time G-CMAX took off alone from the northwest runway for the club's customary 10 to 15 minute conditions check, whose purpose was to look at the rotor turbulence (AAIB p.2). The check flight came back on a reverse approach: the crosswind was at right angles to the runway, so neither approach direction had an into-wind component, and on final the aircraft was shaken hard enough that a member commented on it to another member at the time (AAIB p.2, p.10). The aircraft went around, approached again and landed. It taxied to the waiting ASK13 and the tow rope was attached; the combination accelerated slowly on the wet soft grass, with the launch point already as close to the boundary fence as it could be (AAIB p.2).

**The decision point**:

① Morning of 27 December 2025, during setup: change the takeoff runway from the east to the northwest and accept a 90° crosswind, rather than stop flying for the day. Setting: a conversation in the takeoff area between the duty instructor and the tug pilot, recorded in the report as a discussion with no written record; the premise was that the original runway was unusable. Approved by: the duty instructor, as the person assessing the day's flying operations, and the tug pilot (AAIB p.1–2).

② The same morning, the check flight: take off alone, enter the airspace the tow would later climb through, and meet severe turbulence there. Setting: the club's customary conditions check, 10 to 15 minutes; approved by the tug pilot as commander (AAIB p.2). The report records no written conclusion from the check flight, only its purpose, to confirm rotor turbulence near the site before gliding began (AAIB p.2).

③ The same morning, after the check flight landed: attach the rope to the ASK13 and begin the ground run. Setting: the takeoff area; approved by the tug pilot. BGA guidance states that until release he is commander of the combination and, after consulting the person in charge, has a duty to stop the tow if he does not wish to continue, without waiting for the club to call a halt (AAIB p.9, p.2). Two instructor pilots were on the field, both with more than 20 years of flying (AAIB p.2, p.8). Past this step there is no ground option: the grass runway is 360 metres, and the ground run had already begun near the boundary fence (AAIB p.2, p.7).

④ 1630:37 UTC: throttle reduced to 21 per cent for about two seconds, then back to 100 per cent (AAIB p.4). The record shows power coming off before it went on, and the report considers that the first may have been an attempt to land back on the field and the second an attempt to clear the trees and the single-storey building ahead (AAIB p.10). Approved by: the pilot. Those two seconds of reduced power, and the full power after them, are the only explicit record in the flight of a decision about whether to land (AAIB p.4, p.10).

**What was visible then**:

The morning briefing was run by the duty instructor and covered the weather and the day's threats (AAIB p.7). The clubhouse weather station read 12 to 16 knots with gusts of 23, wind from east-northeast, across the runway in use, air temperature 4°C (AAIB p.6). The station records every 5 minutes and the day's trace can be read back, with speed swinging widely and direction steady (AAIB p.6, p.10). Larkhill, about 2 nautical miles from the accident site, recorded 14 knots from the west with gusts to 28, and cloud rising from two oktas to four between 1500 and 1600 (AAIB p.13). The windsock was visible at the start of the takeoff run (AAIB p.6). Flight manual: maximum takeoff crosswind 15 knots, maximum tailwind 4 knots (AAIB p.5). BGA guidance: tugs are prone to loss of control in attitude, light tugs more so; the cumulative factors include turbulence in the takeoff area, rough ground, a significant crosswind and a short tow rope, and three or more of them should prompt a decision not to launch (AAIB p.8, p.9). Performance data: towing the ASK13 needs 190 metres on asphalt, 270 metres on grass with the upslope correction, and 340 metres with 70 metres added for obstacle clearance, and those figures only get worse on wet soft ground (AAIB p.6). The turbulence on the check flight's approach had been seen at first hand (AAIB p.2, p.10).

What was learned afterwards: airframe and engine were serviceable (AAIB p.4, p.10); the wind after liftoff may have exceeded 15 knots (AAIB p.10–11); the left wing drop came from crosswind and turbulence together (AAIB p.11); pulling to clear the obstacle cost airspeed and led into the stall (AAIB p.10). None of it took shape until the data were read and the wreckage examined after the flight.

**What happened**: after liftoff the tug rolled left about 60° without command, the glider released and landed safely on the field, and the tug turned left about 60° into the lee, where the tailwind component grew; the mainwheel briefly touched the ground with trees and a single-storey building ahead; the aircraft lifted off again, climbing almost vertically as seen from the launch point, cleared the trees and the hangar, then the left wing dropped and it went into a field beyond the boundary. The left wingtip struck the top of the fence first, the nose hit the ground, and the fuselage came to rest having turned through 270°; both wings were displaced about 45°, the cockpit was badly damaged, one blade of the four-blade propeller had broken off about a quarter of its length from the hub, and the sole pilot was seriously injured (AAIB p.1, p.2, p.3). AAIB conclusion: the takeoff was made towards a turbulent area in gusty crosswind conditions, and the wing drop after liftoff led to loss of control (AAIB p.11). Back to the three questions. The most common answer to the first is to wait another ten minutes and watch the wind; in this case that action buys little margin, ten minutes on 360 metres of grass, and the real cost is admitting the day is not flyable. The item most easily missed in the second is the turbulence the check flight brought back, which looks like a routine test flight and is in fact a measurement taken ten minutes earlier on the same track. The third lands before the rope goes on: compare the gust value on the station with the 15 knot limit, and if it is over, end towing for the day.

**Options ruled out**: stop flying for the day, waiting for the wind to drop or coming back another day, not adopted, since the report records the day's gliding conditions as favourable with two gliders and their people already on site, so stopping meant the two gliders and their crews waiting through a morning of not flying, and the forecast thermals were to last until 1800 (AAIB p.11, p.13); launch in a gap between gusts, where the station records every 5 minutes and the webcams show wide swings in speed, and the decision was still made on the judgement that the surface wind was below the station reading (AAIB p.10); go back to the east runway or use a third one, where the east runway was wet and soft and no other runway enters the record (AAIB p.1, p.7); shorten the tow rope, use a heavier tug or reduce the glider's weight, all adjustable items on the BGA factor list that the report does not record as discussed that day (AAIB p.8–9); exercise the commander's authority to call enough, and cancel towing for the day, which the BGA explicitly grants and which was not used (AAIB p.9); put the launch off until the afternoon when thermals weakened, not adopted, since the day was recorded as one of favourable gliding conditions (AAIB p.11, p.13); hand the judgement to the club collectively, where the report records no collective review at all and both the conditions assessment and the launch decision sat with one person (AAIB p.6, p.9).

**Early signals**: ① the takeoff area wet and soft and the east runway unusable, handled as a change of runway rather than a change of day (AAIB p.1); ② the station reading 12 to 16 knots gusting 23 against a 15 knot limit, overridden by the possibility that the surface wind along the runway was lower (AAIB p.6, p.10); ③ the check flight shaken hard on final, with a member commenting at the time, overridden by the aircraft having landed safely, the report's own words being that it was shaken enough for one member to comment to another (AAIB p.2, p.10); ④ 360 metres of grass against 340 metres of obstacle clearance requirement, a margin of 20 metres, overridden because the launch point was already as close to the boundary fence as it could be (AAIB p.2, p.6, p.7); ⑤ 1 hour in the previous 90 days and 0 hours in the previous 28, against 54 hours on type, overridden by a licence held for 45 years and more than 12,000 tows (AAIB p.1, p.8). The report adds a sentence of its own: operations had succeeded at this site in similar conditions many times before, which may have supported a view that today would be no different (AAIB p.10). The hardest of the five is ②, since the limit is printed in the manual and the reading was on the clubhouse screen, with one comparison between them. The reason it was overridden is in the report: the surface wind along the runway may be lower than the station reading, and the surface wind could not be measured (AAIB p.10–11). The one thing overridden was the criterion itself. The softest of the five is ①, which was already a concession, recorded as an arrangement of the site. Next softest is ③, where one landing was taken as proof that this track was acceptable.

**The point of escalation**: 27 December 2025, one or two minutes before the accident time of 1130 UTC, at the moment the rope went onto the ASK13 and the combination began its ground run on 360 metres of grass. Action: launch. Approved by: the tug pilot, commander of the combination. In the ten minutes before, he had been shaken by turbulence in that same airspace and had landed (AAIB p.2, p.10). The investment here is counted in hours: the day's journey, the rigging, the towing and one check flight, plus two gliders, two instructors and the club's expectation of an operation. The price of exit was not flying that day, and the person who had to say it was the only person entitled to say it. Past that step every remaining action is in the air, and only the record can reconstruct it. The report goes one step into this: the day's gliding conditions were favourable, and the goal of towing the glider may itself have kept the pilot from stopping to re-examine the danger from the crosswind and the turbulence (AAIB p.11). Date, action and approver are all present, and the money column is empty: at personal scale the top-up is counted in hours and in trips. The reasons for going were more than one. The wind might be lighter, the aircraft had just flown, the people were on the field, the glider was on the rope. Each stands on its own, and only together do they make a launch.

**Failure chain**: technical layer: a 90° crosswind means no into-wind component on the takeoff run, wet soft grass lengthens the ground run, obstacle clearance needs 340 metres against a 360 metre runway, a margin of 20 metres; liftoff leads straight into ridge rotor turbulence, and the pull to clear the obstacle after the wing drop costs airspeed and leads to the stall (AAIB p.6, p.7, p.10, p.11); the engine record rules out power as a factor (AAIB p.4, p.10). Organizational layer: the site has no official weather source and conditions are judged by the duty instructor on local experience; the daily briefing and the mandatory site briefing were both in place, and neither carried a quantitative launch gate; the BGA's three-or-more-factors advice carries no releasing authority, and the same guidance leaves the enough-is-enough call to the person on the spot (AAIB p.9); club oversight of towing rested on one person, who both assessed the conditions and made the launch (AAIB p.6, p.7). Incentive layer: gliding conditions were favourable, people and equipment were in place, and towing is the club's core activity; the cost of stopping was paid that day and the probability of loss of control did not show that day; nobody else held a formal veto; and the improvements the report records stop at information and self-questioning, with no hard limit introduced (AAIB p.9, p.11). All three layers point at one gap: the criterion was handed to experience and the veto to the person executing. The break sits between the organizational and incentive layers, since the authority to stop was written into the guidance and the cost of using it was paid on the spot by the person holding it.

**Minimum counterfactual**: after the check flight lands and before the rope goes on, compare the gust value from the station reading, 23 knots, with the 15 knot crosswind limit in the manual, and end towing for the day if it is over. One action, doable within ten minutes, with the reading on the club website and the clubhouse screen that day and the limit printed in the manual (AAIB p.5, p.6). It needs no better meteorology, only the use of a reference value as a limit. It needs no different person either, since the one doing it is still the one entitled to stop towing. If the club writes that line at the end of the morning briefing, tomorrow's action depends neither on memory nor on who happens to be there. The step does not touch the incentive layer: the cost of stopping still falls on the person on the day, and only the trigger moves from judgement to a reading. The surface wind could not be measured then (AAIB p.11), so judgement had to rest on experience, and until that changes the same launch will come again.

**Outcome and sources**: the sole pilot was seriously injured, the aircraft was damaged, and the two people in the glider were unhurt (AAIB p.1, p.2). After the accident the club improved its weather reporting, providing a live two-minute mean wind and ten-minute maximum gust, broken down by headwind, tailwind and crosswind for each of the three runways, and promoted an internal question of whether to fly rather than whether one can (AAIB p.11). Primary source: AAIB, *Bulletin 09-26*, AAIB-31440, Eurofox 2K G-CMAX, Talgarth Airfield, 27 December 2025, published 20 August 2026 (AAIB p.11). The report is a field investigation bulletin with no safety recommendations, and the safety actions listed were completed by the club (AAIB p.11); the aircraft had flown 482 hours and made 3,379 landings, with airframe and powerplant records normal (AAIB p.5, p.10). Dates and times in this card come from the report, and page numbers are PDF pages; the link is in Appendix B under av-025.

Case two (av-025) at personal scale needs a footnote of its own: the day's flying was already scheduled, with two gliders, two instructors and one check flight on the field, and the launch was decided on top of what had already gone into the day. At personal scale the investment is not money; it is the journey, the rigging, the towing and the flight already flown. The price of exit is paid the same day, which is no flying today, and the probability of losing control does not show itself that day. The personal-scale defense has to move to the ground before it matters, as a decision rule set before takeoff: a reading over the limit ends towing for the day, no exceptions. In the air there is no room to negotiate, so moving the rule forward shifts the ground of escalation from the air to the ground, from a moment's judgement to a sheet of paper.

One execution detail on that sheet belongs in the text. The exception clause of a personal rule has to be harder to invoke than the rule itself. A glider version written as "unless the weather suddenly improves" turns the exception into an exit taken every time, since every time there is a reason. A harder form requires a second signature from whoever else is on duty that day, so that an exception on the ground borrows another pair of eyes. The general form: the exception procedure of a personal commitment involves a second person, and its cost is set deliberately above the cost of compliance, so that complying is cheaper than excepting.

Cheap compliance has a social design layer as well. Make the personal rule public, with club members and crews knowing each other's rules, and the published rule acquires peer custody: the person flying with you knows your rule and can remind you. A network of custody upgrades individual discipline into group discipline, and the line about everyone here having that rule, heard in gliding clubs and in fishing communities, survives fatigue better than any individual willpower. The organizational version of group discipline is Device 4 in this chapter, since an organization that praises the person who stops a project is issuing peer custody to every member's personal rule.

### Case 3 (Maritime): Sleep Debt and the Trip

ma-003. The prawn trawler Crystal Stream (LH 147), 6 June 2025, Loch Fyne, Scotland. The MAIB report (Report 11/2026, pages read for this book) records that the skipper, alone on watch, fell asleep and the vessel grounded, a constructive total loss, with the skipper slightly injured (the casualty table was checked and reads minor injuries); sleep debt had been accumulating through the week before. The economics of fishing make every trip hard to abort: fuel, the boat's schedule and the market window for the catch. The sunk and expected costs of a trip together make continuing the default, and continuing presupposes the skipper still on watch, even if that means working on overdrawn sleep.

Stop at the decision point: the morning before the accident trip left port. On the table: a week's fatigue account, the trip's fixed outlay, and what aborting would do to the schedule and to income. Outcome: grounding, constructive total loss. Primary source: MAIB, Report 11/2026, 2026; link at Appendix B, ma-003.

Case three (ma-003) has an economic detail worth writing out. In the market structure for catch, the cost of aborting a trip is amplified: the landing window is short and the price swings, so losing a day can lose the season's price. The structural inducement to escalate, a real opportunity cost, stacks on the psychological one, sunk cost. The distinction matters for defense, since psychological inducement is treated with the four devices and structural inducement with institutional design: reserve capacity inside a rotation system, a pool of relief skippers, the scheduling defense from Chapter 15 case six. Leave the structure alone and the psychological line stands naked in front of a real opportunity cost every day. Change the structure and the psychological line has ground under it.

#### Case 4 (Fintech): More Money for an Unvalidated Model (condensed)

st-001. The legal tech startup Atrium, about 200 employees and about 450 clients. The Failory Cemetery archive, quoting the founder Justin Kan's public statements, read for this book: the company closed after raising $75.5 million. The founder's retrospective is a textbook sample of criterion ②: adding more money to a situation of lack of product-market fit only means spending the money faster. The structure of the funding rounds and the founder's reputational investment together made the fuel, since every round arriving on the account reinforced the reasoning that one more push would do it, while the core problem of product-market fit was never solved. The decision point is the board meeting that approved the next expansion with product-market fit unvalidated. Minimum counterfactual: tie each expansion tranche to a product-market fit validation milestone (the same lesson as Chapter 24 case seven). Primary source: Failory Cemetery (quoting Justin Kan's public statements), 2020; the link is in Appendix B under st-001 (evidence grade E).

The four cases sit on three scales of this chapter's spectrum: national (po-004, a £46.8 billion state project), organizational (st-001, a $75.5 million company) and personal (av-025, a launch after a check flight; ma-003, one week of sleep). That the three scales share one chain of criteria is the corner this chapter most wants to push the reader into. Escalation is a disease of decisions. You have a small-scale version of it running now.

### The Evidence Gap

Primary evidence on escalation is thin by nature. Institutions rarely publish the decision chain of why they did not exit, which is another form of Chapter 2's survivorship bias: exited projects vanish and escalated ones leave reports. Wartime and large-policy escalation cases are scattered across archives and retrospective inquiries, and within this book's collection scope none has a verifiable full text, so the gap is marked as it stands. Directions for further collection are in, legislative renewal inquiries and long-tail loss studies from the insurance industry.

## 27.4 What Shows Up Earliest

Six weak signals, each with the reason that usually overrides it.

1. "Just a little longer" and "we have come this far" appear more often in meetings. Reason given: "morale".
2. The assumptions in a re-forecast are made more optimistic by one side, and the person who adjusts them does not sign. Reason given: "a model update".
3. Somebody writes an exit option and nobody puts it on the agenda. Reason given: "the timing is not right".
4. Milestones are redefined: the standard for reaching the original target is replaced by a standard that can be reached. Reason given: "a pragmatic adjustment".
5. People who argue against renewal are moved to another project. Reason given: "workforce optimization".
6. The project's outward narrative switches from the language of return to the language of persistence ("strategic value", "we cannot leave it half done"). Reason given: "one message". The switch in language comes before the budget request, and it is the joint between Chapter 13 and this chapter.

Now put the six signals against the four cases. Signal 1, the rising frequency of "just a little longer", belongs to case two: the same airspace had just shaken him into a landing, and the rope went on again. How many times the sentence was said inside his head can only be inferred, and the shape of the judgement chain is written in the launch record. Signal 2, assumptions made optimistic by one side, belongs to case one: every re-justification inside each reset was an occasion for retuning the return calculation on the remaining section. Signal 3, an exit option nobody tables, belongs to case one: both reset agendas were agendas for finishing the job, and a full cancellation was never formally laid beside them. Signal 4, redefined milestones, belongs to case four: product-market fit never arrived, and the expansion milestones have been through three generations. Signal 5, the dissenter moved, takes the form in a public project of the dissenting department's budget being rearranged. Signal 6, the language switch, is the standard prose of case one's reset press releases, and at national scale the switch from the language of return to the language of persistence is carried out in legislative language.

Reorder the six by the cost of evidence. Signals 4 and 6 are cheapest, read from the wording of documents and the text of milestones. Signals 1 and 2 are middling, read from minutes and from the version history of the model. Signals 3 and 5 are dearest, needing agenda records and the trail of personnel moves. Start with signal 4: pull three years of a project's milestone text and read the versions side by side, and the loosening of the achievement standard is plain. Every loosening should carry a signed reason, and one without a signature is handled under criterion ② of this chapter.

## 27.5 How to Defend

Device 1: exit conditions up front. The approval document must contain an exit clause: which signals trigger an exit review (quantified), who adjudicates (named), and a statement that at the review the money already spent no longer counts as an argument (stated in the text). The clause is agreed at signing, when all parties are calmest, and it is the approval-stage version of Chapter 8's Device 3. On wording: the trigger signal must be externally observable, in schedule, in external assessment, in a third-party milestone, and never "when management considers it necessary".

Device 2: an external adjudicator. The person who adjudicates an exit review must have no history with the project, having neither signed its approval nor spoken for it, and the appointment covers the project's whole expected life. His only agenda is to answer the zero-based re-ask, not to balance the parties.

Device 3: the zero-based re-ask. The first item on every review agenda is fixed: "if nothing had been invested as of today, would this project be started?" Answer that before discussing reasons to continue. The HS2-style reset review lacks exactly this step, since every minute of the reset discussion goes to allocating how the money already put in gets finished.

Device 4: a reward for termination. Put the person who stopped their own project into the same recognition and promotion channel as the people who succeed. Without it, exit clauses decay into text automatically, because the career interest of the person executing the clause runs against it (Chapter 19's alarm logic applies here too: whoever executes an exit has to be protected first).

The four devices applied to this chapter's cases. Device 1 on case one: a rail project's approval clause would name trigger signals, a ceiling on full cost per kilometre and the external audit conclusion at an opening milestone, with the trigger obliging a review, and the review carrying the zero-based re-ask. Device 2 on case four: the board's product-market fit review brings in an outside member who took no part in the funding narrative, with the zero-based re-ask as the only agenda. Device 3 on case three: translate the question into a morning rule on a fishing boat, whether this trip would leave port if the fuel and the schedule had not yet been committed, one question asked in a moment. Device 4 in an organization: put the record of a stop into the standard material for promotion review, so that the person who stopped and the person who succeeded are ranked on the same sheet.

The failure modes of the four. An exit clause dies when its trigger is reinterpreted, with management declaring the signal inapplicable once the quantified threshold is met; the patch is to bind the adjudication of the signal to an external data source, a third-party milestone or an audit conclusion, so that the reading is not the project's to make. An external adjudicator dies when the list of unbound people runs out, since in a large organization nobody is unbound from every project; the patch is borrowing across organizations, independent directors, outside experts, the counterpart post in a sister body, and the cost of borrowing is far below the cost of being bound. The zero-based re-ask dies of formality, with item one completed and item two arguing from sunk cost as before; the patch is a structured minute, where the answer to question one has to appear and any appearance of the phrase already invested among the reasons to continue sends question one back for a second answer. The termination reward dies of being spoken rather than written, praised in the room and ignored in the appraisal; the patch is the stop record entering the personnel file as standard material, bound to Device 4's clause as written. All four patches share one check sentence: has this device ever actually changed a project's fate.

Installation order matters as well. Device 3 first, the zero-based re-ask, which costs nothing and works immediately; Device 1 next, exit clauses, applied to any new project; Device 2 next, external adjudicators, appointed annually; Device 4 last, the termination reward, the largest piece of institutional engineering. The order follows the depth of the intervention: change the question at each review, then change the project's birth certificate, then change who sits at the table, then change the organization's career incentives. An organization that reverses the order, calling for the reward first while its project documents carry no exit clause at all, gets one award ceremony and zero exits.

## 27.6 Boundaries with Adjacent Mechanisms

Against the escalation ladder (Chapter 8): Chapter 8 handles the process, which rung you are standing on; this chapter handles the decision point, why you are still climbing. Test in that order, grade with Chapter 8 first, then treat with the exit machinery here.

Against narrative before evidence (Chapter 13): escalation's outward expression is usually carried by narrative ("strategic value"), and narrative also preheats escalation, since announcing an expectation first makes exit a broken promise. Separate by object: managing expectations belongs to Chapter 13, accounting for the investment stays here.

Against normalization of deviance (Chapter 11): the cost overruns and delays of an escalating project get normalized one at a time ("big projects are all like this", which is the NAO's own wording on Sizewell), and normalization is escalation's lubricant. On large projects the ledger of Chapter 11 is overrun and delay themselves.

## 27.7 Five Self-Checks

1. On which page of your project's approval document is the exit clause? If there is none, is there still time to write one today?
2. At which meeting did you last argue for a budget increase with "we have put in so much already"? Did the project turn around after it?
3. Who last adjusted the assumptions in your project's re-forecast? How many adjustments went one way and how many the other?
4. What post does the last person in your organization to stop their own project hold now?
5. Zero-based re-ask: if nothing had been invested as of today, would you start the project you are holding? Put the answer beside your reasons to continue for one night, and read both in the morning.
