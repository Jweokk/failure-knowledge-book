# Chapter 25: Margin Erosion (Dekker)

> What this chapter covers: how margin is eaten one bite at a time with nobody seeing the whole; Dekker's drift view, three criteria, four domains, the margin ledger and the cumulative-view review.
> When to come back: at every reduction review, at every "this one is special" exception request, and on the day a system crosses a boundary with no warning at all.
> Reading time: about 50 minutes.

No system that fails was pushed off the cliff by a single decision. Sidney Dekker called the real motion drift into failure: each departure legal under the rules of its day, each cut cleared by its review, each "just this once" survived in fact. Margin is not decided away. It is forgotten away. It has no ledger, no guardian, and no meeting at which anyone looks at it whole. On the day the boundary arrives, everyone in the room faces a thing that has never happened before.

## 25.1 What It Is

Definition: margin erosion is the design margin of a system, meaning time, strength, distance, redundancy, room to act, eaten back cut by cut. Each cut looks harmless on its own, and nobody holds the running total.

Three criteria for activation. Any one of them, and the mechanism is already running.

(i) No single cut is enough to cause the event on its own. The defence made for each cut is usually true.
(ii) Nobody holds the cumulative quantity. There is no ledger, only the assessment made at the time of that one cut.
(iii) "Still enough" becomes the basis for the decision, and "enough" is measured against the state left by the previous cut.

Criterion (iii) is the self-locking part and deserves separate treatment. Every cut resets the baseline; the baseline then serves as the starting point for the next cut. Before the first cut the margin is the design value. After the tenth, the reference point has slid to wherever the sixth left it, and nobody remembers the origin. That is the difference between a margin being reduced and a margin being eaten: reduction is an action, erosion is a failure of memory.

Dekker's angle needs two more sentences. Writing after normal accidents (Perrow) and the normalization of deviance (Vaughan), he turned the lens on where a system travels during the years in which nothing happens. His observation: every adjustment inside a system is made against current practice, and current practice has already absorbed all the earlier adjustments, so the reference frame itself is drifting. That observation gives criterion (iii) its floor. A sliding baseline is not managerial negligence; it is what reference-to-practice produces by construction. The only way to stop the slide is to move the reference from practice to paper, which means the original design value. That is the principle behind Device 3, the minimum waterline: the number is taken from the design, never from any "current state".

Memory loss has an organizational face worth naming. Margin has no natural guardian. The designer created it, and when the designer changes jobs the knowledge disperses. The operating team maintains function (it still works), not margin (how much bad news it can still take). Management watches output and cost. None of the three treats margin as its own asset. It is a public good, and the fate of public goods is written in the economics textbook. The margin ledger is the device that turns the public good into owned property: every margin line carries an owner, with the clause from Chapter 20 copied across, and the owner's appraisal carries one line for the completeness rate of that year's ledger. Giving margin a person is the second thing that has to happen, after giving it a book.

## 25.2 How It Grows

Three generating conditions.

Condition one: cost pressure that never lets up. The damage comes from medium and small cuts in every budget cycle, not from one crisis-sized cut. Once the pressure is routine the cuts are routine, and routine is precisely what strips the signal value out of each one.

Condition two: the invisibility of margin. Design margin does not appear in any report and produces no revenue. Its existence shows only afterwards, in the event. Anything invisible has a settled fate under cost pressure. The invisibility of maintenance in Chapter 29 is the asset-side version of the same story.

Condition three: the institution of the single assessment. Each change is assessed for its own effect. No post, no form and no standing meeting owns the sum of the history. Every one of those conclusions can be honest, and the total is systematic self-deception.

## 25.3 How It Looks in Four Domains

### Case 1 (Maritime): Freeing Ports Cut Down One at a Time

ma-009. The twin-rigged stern trawler Ocean Way, November 2014, west coast of Scotland. MAIB report 23/2015 (page checked for this book): the vessel capsized and sank, three dead, two survived. The investigation pointed to the weight of water on deck. Water trapped there capsized the boat, and the report's own term is entrapped water. Progressive earlier changes to the freeing ports made that water more likely to stay. The liferaft cradle jammed and could not be released, and the EPIRB had no GNSS fix, so search and rescue had a position 47 minutes later. Each freeing-port change had its own reason. Together they ate the righting moment the boat needed in a bad sea, which is the margin her design had promised.

Stop at the decision point: the moment one of those changes was approved. On the table: the functional reason for this change, normal running afterwards, and a deck drainage drawing with no cumulative record. Outcome: capsize and sinking, three dead. Primary source: MAIB, Report 23/2015, 2015; link at Appendix B, ma-009.

An engineering debt is still owed on this case. Why the loss of righting moment stayed invisible. A trawler's stability is designed with a full set of margins (freeboard, initial metacentric height, limiting line), and the effective value of all of them moves with every change to deck arrangement. Stability review is triggered only by specific events, a modification registered or a class survey, and successive small freeing-port changes never reached the trigger line. The professional calculation existed; naval architecture can produce the number. The trigger mechanism was absent, since nobody was required to ask for it. So the lesson is not that the arithmetic was beyond them. Nobody did the arithmetic. Tying the stability review trigger to small modifications, so that accumulated change past a threshold forces a review, is the trawler version of the cumulative-view review. Another name for a trigger line: the arrangement that calls the professional calculation at the right time.

### Case 2 (Chemical): An Undersized Relief Path and an Absent System

**Case ID**: ch-006.

**When and where**: 14:39 on 12 November 2024 (the top relief valve moving closed with no command) to 14:57:41 (reactor 6 rupturing); the central manufacturing area of the Givaudan Sense Colour caramel colour plant, Louisville, Kentucky; reactor 6 and the combined control room and laboratory 40 feet to its east (CSB report p.28, p.31, p.69).

**What and who**: the Givaudan group, Switzerland; the wholly owned subsidiary D.D. Williamson & Co., LLC owns and operates the plant, acquired by Givaudan on 19 December 2021 (p.16, p.88). Batch reactors 1 through 6 on site; No. 6 built in 1978, run at the Modesto, California plant, stored here after that plant closed in 2008, modified and installed in 2021 (p.19). About 55 people on the register, with 30 employees plus at least four contractors and visitors on site at the time (p.16). The relevant posts: the plant engineer who took over the reactor 5 and 6 project in September 2020; the engineering manager who led the 2012 reactivity testing and left in April 2017; the operating crews and the maintenance technicians (p.25, p.88).

**What they were doing**: two things at once. One was restoring capacity. Reactor 6 finished installation in August 2021, received its first batch on 28 December and passed its pre-startup safety review (PSSR) on 29 December (p.88). The unit came from the closed Modesto plant together with reactor 5, was modified to meet this plant's design requirements, and went into the existing piping of the central manufacturing area. By design the two were to run a range of caramel colour products, not only 484 (p.27). The other was the day's routine batch. At 10:22 on 12 November the crew charged to the 484 batch record: water, invert syrup, sodium hydroxide, phosphoric acid, antifoam (p.28). Product 484 is a Class I caramel colour, made here at least since 2012, with the batch record issued on 27 January 2012; reactor 6 had run at least four 484 batches in the month before, without abnormality (p.28 footnote). At 14:17 the pressure setpoint went to 12 psig and the temperature setpoint to 300°F per the batch record; the PLC closed the top relief valve to build pressure and steam came on to heat (p.28). Pressure reached 12 psig at 14:24 and the valve modulated to hold it over the next 16 minutes. At 14:39 the valve moved toward the closed position with no command from the PLC; at 14:40 the PLC commanded full open and the valve did not move (p.28). The technical fault landed on that one valve. The plant collected no valve position data, and the report reconstructed position from surveillance video together with scrubber temperature and pressure (p.28 footnote).

**The decision point**: the report withholds individual names, so signatories can be recorded only to post and document. Four cuts, each with a date, a size and a carrier.

Cut one, 15 October 2007: the site took its ammonia inventory below the threshold of the EPA Risk Management Program (RMP) (p.88). The size of the cut was an entire obligation disappearing. After a reactor at this plant ruptured on 11 April 2003, killing one person, releasing 26,000 pounds of ammonia and sending 1,500 people to shelter in place, the plant sat under RMP and had to run a hazard review at least every five years; leaving coverage took that obligation to zero (pp. 23–25, p.57). The carrier was an inventory change, and the report records no post responsible for it (p.22). The recommendation CSB issued after the 2003 accident, to establish a hazard evaluation procedure, stayed open until August 2009, when it was closed as "acceptable action taken" (p.57, p.88). That barrier was patched on paper. The 2007 exit from coverage removed the external beat that kept it alive.

Cut two, 30 November 2012: an outside consultant used reactivity test data from product 034 to size relief, and recommended enlarging the orifice on the reactor that would make 034, from 4.186 square inches to 10.304 square inches, a factor of 2.46 (p.26 footnote, p.88). It was implemented in 2013 on reactors 3 and 4 only; reactors 1 and 2 were not making 034 at the time and stayed at 4.186 (p.27). In the same period the management of change (MOC) for reactor 3 carried an action item to "update the HAZOP", with the completion and date columns blank; the reactor 4 modification went through no MOC at all, against the plant's own policy of June 2006 (p.57, Figure 35 p.58).

Cut three, 14 April 2021: the plant engineer who had inherited the project ordered relief valves for reactors 5 and 6, orifice 4.186 square inches (p.88). The basis was the existing configuration of reactor 2, on the grounds that the two new reactors were close to it in size and made the same products. The site held no design basis document for the reactor 2 relief system (p.27, p.47). That order took the 10.304 validated for 034 nine years earlier and put 4.186 back in its place, which is 40 percent of the validated figure.

Cut four, 19 October 2021: the MOC for reactors 5 and 6 was closed. The MOC itself listed a process hazard analysis (PHA) as required, then concluded it was unnecessary because the design was "a copy of No. 2" (p.60, p.88). The PHA was never done. Two months later the PSSR of 29 December checked that the equipment had been built to the drawing, not where the drawing's basis came from (p.88).

**What was visible then**: the 034 reactivity curve of 15 October 2012, showing a self-sustaining temperature rise under closed heating and a pressure rise driven by non-condensable gas (pp. 25–26); the consultant's relief sizing recommendation of 30 November 2012, requiring an orifice larger than 7.4 square inches (p.26 footnote); the 2013 modification records for reactors 3 and 4, and the unclosed "update the HAZOP" item on the reactor 3 MOC (p.58); the HAZOP report of 21 February 2008, whose high-pressure deviation list included relief valve failure, blocked relief line and check valve failure, with sugar decomposition absent from it (p.25 footnote); the MOC policy text of June 2006, "design review completed before the change" (p.57); the missing design basis document for reactor 2 (p.47); the Safe Operating Limits document established in 2011, which set pressure limits at each vessel's maximum allowable working pressure and temperature limits at maximum allowable working temperature, giving 75 psig and 355°F for reactor 6, and which also said that if a reaction could not be controlled and pressure approached the safe limit, the fire alarm should be pulled and the plant evacuated (p.65); the control room and laboratory combined in 2012, 40 feet from the reactors and not blast resistant (p.69); the plant's 19 October 2015 statement closing out the EPA consent agreement, saying that all recommendations in the final work plan had been implemented by 17 September 2013 (p.88); the personnel record of the engineering manager's departure in April 2017 (p.88); the production schedule around 2021, on which reactor 2 had begun making 034 without an MOC (p.88); section 10 of the supplier's safety data sheet for invert syrup, which carried no decomposition warning, while a second supplier's sheet for the same material, which the report also found, did state the decomposition hazard (pp. 53–54).

What was learned only afterwards: invert sugar begins to decompose above 267°F, inside the normal operating range for 484 (p.54); in CSB's adiabatic calorimetry the third exotherm began at 243°F, with a 284°F temperature rise and a peak pressure of 1,509 psig (p.37); the relief area needed to stop this rupture was 16.74 square inches, requiring a 6R10 valve or a single 4-inch rupture disc (pp. 49–50); and why the relief valve failed cannot be determined, because the positioner was never recovered (p.75).

**Stop point.** You are sitting in this seat with the material above in front of you.

> 2. Which item here are you most likely to miss, and why does it look unimportant?

**What happened.** The relief device lifted at 14:54 and pressure dropped briefly, then rose again; pressure passed 75 psig at 14:55; temperature passed 355°F at 14:56; at 14:57:41 the reactor ruptured, with a recorded peak of 237 psig and 385°F (p.31, p.89). Six people were in the control room. Two died, a maintenance technician and a railroad operator, both crushed when the control room collapsed; the railroad operator was not found until about 10 hours after the event (p.32, p.34). Three were seriously injured: the reactor operator with fractured ribs, a maintenance technician with lacerations, and an administrative assistant in the neighbouring office with a concussion (p.32). Damage came to $30 million on site and $10 million off site; debris travelled up to 400 feet beyond the fence line, and a shell section of about 2,000 pounds landed 245 feet away in the yard of a private house (p.32). The plant has been shut down and demolished (p.9). Back to the three questions. The most common answer to the first is "pull the test data from that year and redo the relief sizing". In this case that action costs one archive pull and one calculation, and what it blocks is 237 psig. The most easily missed item under the second question is the number in the Safe Operating Limits document itself: 75 psig looks like the strictest line on the sheet, and it is in fact the vessel's rupture line, so a limit set at the boundary leaves no room to act. CSB calculated that pulling the fire alarm at 14:54:04 would still have left about 3.5 minutes before the rupture (p.66). The third question lands on the MOC closure of 19 October 2021: test the evidence behind that sentence, meaning whether the drawing being copied from reactor 2 has a design basis at all.

**Options ruled out**: reactivity testing across other products and individual raw materials, which was done only to the minimum required by the 2008 HAZOP recommendation and the EPA consent agreement, since 034 had already been identified as the most hazardous product for heat output and the rest were filed as lower risk (pp. 51–52). Enlarging relief on reactors 1 and 2 as well, which were not making 034, different products carrying different risk (p.27). A PHA for reactors 5 and 6, which the MOC identified and then judged unnecessary as "a copy of No. 2" (p.60). A separate relief sizing study for reactors 5 and 6, since the method and conclusions of the 2012 consultant study were in the plant and copying an existing design needs no new analysis; the report records no new analysis (p.27, p.47). An overpressure scenario list per vessel under the ASME code, which requires the owner to "identify all potential overpressure scenarios and the corresponding pressure relief means", while reactors 1, 2, 5 and 6 left no record of a relief design basis (p.47, p.59 footnote). Relocating or blast-hardening the control room, where the public reason for combining control room and laboratory in 2012 was to shorten the walk between the operator and the equipment and improve communication; the plant had left RMP by then, so no siting analysis was required by regulation, and the report confirms the plant never did one (p.69).

**Early signals**: 1. On the 2013 reactor 3 MOC, the "update the HAZOP" action item had both columns blank, and the responsible person column blank as well (Figure 35, p.58). Why it was pushed through: the modification was installed and running normally, and all that was missing was a text update; the 2015 closure statement then stamped "all recommendations implemented" over it (p.88). 2. In April 2017 the engineering manager, the only person who had led the 2012 testing, left, and the knowledge never reached a document (p.88). The reason given: the record "should be in the MOC", and that MOC was precisely the one never finished; a departure is routine in a personnel system and triggered no document review. 3. Before 2021, reactor 2 began making 034, with an orifice of 4.186 against a validated 10.304 for 034, and the change went through no MOC (p.88). The reason given: a product switch counts as scheduling, not as an equipment change, and the schedule has no change column. 4. On the order of 14 April 2021, 4.186 and the missing design basis for reactor 2 sat on the same piece of paper, which carried only valve model and orifice size, no overpressure scenario (p.47, p.88). The reason given: same size, same product, copying is compliance; a purchasing process does not ask for a design basis. 5. The 2011 limits document set the ceiling at 75 psig and 355°F with no automatic alarm attached (p.65, p.66). The reason given: the document already contained the instruction to pull the fire alarm and evacuate on loss of control, which was treated as a barrier that existed; when the control room and laboratory were combined in 2012 they stood 40 feet from the reactors (p.69).

**The point of escalation**: the MOC closure of 19 October 2021 put "no PHA" into the file, and that became the conclusion every later reader would see (p.60, p.88). The first batch went in on 28 December and the PSSR passed on 29 December. The PSSR was the last gate that could have stopped this, and it checked equipment against drawing, while the drawing's basis was already missing from the file, so the gate had nothing to say about the central question. The acquisition closed just before that batch: Givaudan completed the transaction on 19 December, and the archive it took over was in exactly this state, with a test curve and no design basis, an MOC and no PHA (p.88). From the day the MOC closed, "no PHA needed" stopped being one person's judgment and became the plant's documented conclusion; later readers found that sentence. For the 34 months that followed, reactor 6 ran on the normal schedule and never once called on the relief system. Normal running kept underwriting the cut, because a reactor that has run three years without trouble does not look like equipment short of margin.

**Failure chain**: technical layer. At 14:39 the relief valve closed with no command. Sugar decomposition began above 267°F, and the exotherm plus carbon dioxide drove pressure up on its own. Cooling water came on 9 minutes before the rupture, and heat removal ran below heat generation. The relief system lifted as designed, and the 4.186 square inch orifice fell about four times short of the 16.74 square inches required, so pressure kept climbing to 237 psig and the shell failed.

Organizational layer. The 2012 test results were never extrapolated to other products or to individual raw materials. The 2013 modification covered two reactors, left one action item unclosed and skipped MOC on one unit. Sixteen years passed after 2008 with no further hazard analysis. No post was accountable for executing process safety policy. Safe operating limits sat at the vessel's design limit, with no automatic alarm and no record of refresher training. The control room stood 40 feet from the reactors, was not blast resistant, and had never been through a siting analysis.

Incentive layer. Syrup is not on the OSHA process safety management list of named chemicals or on the EPA RMP list, so neither regulation covers a process of this kind (pp. 71–74). After the 2007 exit from RMP, the five-year hazard review stopped being a legal duty. Capital projects moved as "copy the existing reactor", the fastest and cheapest path available.

The break sits at the junction of organizational and incentive layers. The incentive layer supplied a legal path called "don't", and the organizational layer turned "don't" into documents one at a time: an unfilled action item, a product switch on a schedule, an order copied from reactor 2. The regulatory compliance list has no line for reactivity, so every "don't" passed. And the organizational layer had already accepted compliance as a substitute for hazard review. All three layers of margin were therefore absent at once: no data to set the waterline, no orifice to catch the flow, no post to keep the record.

**Minimum counterfactual**: before the MOC closed on 19 October 2021, run the PHA that the MOC itself listed, instead of closing it with "a copy of No. 2". One action, 37 months before the event, costing one cross-discipline review meeting, with no new equipment, no new budget and no new consultant. By the report's own definition of that PHA, it had four things to examine: earlier hazard analyses, MOC files over the years, reactivity test data, and the protection design about to be adopted (p.60). Any one of the four would have brought the 2012 curve and the 2013 modification to the table. The meeting would have had to answer one question: which document is the relief design basis for reactor 2. The answer was that no such document exists (p.47), and that empty answer would have put the 10.304 for 034 and the 4.186 on the order onto the same table.

**Outcome and sources**: 2 dead, 3 seriously injured; $30 million in on-site damage and $10 million off site; the plant demolished (p.9, p.32). CSB issued 12 new recommendations and reiterated 2 earlier ones (pp. 79–83), among them that Givaudan calorimetry-test all caramel colour products (R1), that a third party redesign emergency relief on every reactor (R5), that automatic alarms and training be added against the safe operating limits (R6), and that a siting study precede any new plant (R7). Primary source: U.S. Chemical Safety and Hazard Investigation Board, *Fatal Runaway Reaction and Explosion at Givaudan Sense Colour / D.D. Williamson, Louisville, Kentucky, November 12, 2024*, Investigation Report No. 2024-06-I-KY, released 27 May 2026, 96 pages; Givaudan has said it is considering rebuilding the plant elsewhere, and CSB attached a comment requiring the new site to be kept away from residential areas (p.11 footnote). The report and the test data sit on the same case page; link at Appendix B, ch-006.

The three layers of margin in this case are worth restating in the ledger format of this chapter, because the case is the standard specimen of what a margin ledger should look like. Row one, process safety information margin, meaning whether reactivity data exists. Original design requirement: do it. Current value: none. Change history: never listed. Row two, physical margin, meaning the relief capacity's coverage of the runaway rate. Original design value: estimated from existing parameters. Current value: insufficient. Change history: no review after the design was fixed. Row three, organizational margin, meaning a process safety management system in place. Original requirement: recommended by industry practice. Current value: written policy, no accountable person, and outside both OSHA PSM and EPA RMP. Change history: October 2007, ammonia inventory taken below the RMP threshold and coverage exited, with no item raised since. All three rows share one signature, an empty change history. A margin with no change history has not necessarily stood still; nobody looked either way. The ledger's columns follow from these three rows: item, original value, current value, change history, owner. With all five in place, a blank in any row has nowhere to hide.

### Case 3 (Software): Three Unpaid Debts in Security Control

sw-011. France Travail. The CNIL closing decision (page checked for this book): a €5 million fine for inadequate security of jobseeker data, with the findings including adviser accounts without multi-factor authentication (MFA), access rights wider than the duties required, and no active monitoring of tool activity logs; the case closed in September 2026 after remediation. All three are margins. Authentication margin, where a single factor was enough to log in and the second factor was skipped. Permission margin, where broad access was handed out for convenience and least privilege was skipped. Logging margin, where records were kept and nobody read them, so active monitoring was skipped. Each of the three was "running normally" before anything happened, and each omission had a defensible reason at the time.

Stop at the decision point: the configuration decision, taken while the account system was being built, that made MFA optional rather than default. On the table: the usability request, the deployment cost, and a tiering assumption that only high-risk posts needed it. Outcome: a €5 million fine, closed after remediation. Primary source: CNIL, 2026; link at Appendix B, sw-011.

Those three omissions share a scale property worth naming. Each was a one-time configuration decision, an option chosen while the account system was being built, and the margin each consumed is continuously exposed, since every day of operation accumulates risk in an unprotected state. Configuration margin has a distinctive shape: the decision costs almost nothing and the regret costs a great deal. Ticking MFA is one click; remediating after a leak is five million euros. Decisions of that shape need their own review rule, and the rule is that every security-relevant default starts on, with opting out requiring a stated reason. The philosophy of defaults is this chapter's mechanism translated into a configuration screen: the freedom to eat margin should be off by default, and anyone who wants it should have to apply.

#### Case 6 (Maritime): The Seam the Inspection Plan Did Not Have (condensed)

ma-013. A sling failure: a deck crew member was struck by a towed block and killed, October 2023, United Kingdom. MAIB report (2026, page checked for this book): a quick release link lost strength under long-term high bending stress and parted, and the vessel's inspection plan did not cover that defect form. The coverage of an inspection plan is the degree to which margin has been itemised: a check dimension the plan does not have, here link fatigue under bending stress, is where margin drains away in silence. Minimum counterfactual: add the stress-form dimension to the inspection plan for load-bearing links, with periodic strip-down and non-destructive testing. Primary source: MAIB, 2026; link at Appendix B, ma-013.

#### Case 7 (Aviation): A Fuel Margin That Was Calculated Low (condensed)

av-013. An instructional flight, July 2023, France. BEA report (2026, page checked for this book): the instructor's fuel consumption estimate was low, usable fuel ran out and the engine stopped, the aircraft ditched at sea and was destroyed, and the instructor and student were injured. The fuel plan is the most explicit margin in flying, the legal reserve, and here the margin was eaten at the planning layer: the consumption figure came from an optimistic reading, so the reserve met the book on paper and evaporated in the actual burn. The cheapest place to defend this margin is calibration of the planning parameter, backfilling estimates with real consumption, and one calibration costs one spreadsheet. Primary source: BEA, 2026; link at Appendix B, av-013.

### Case 4 (Aviation): A Margin Whose Going Cannot Be Rebuilt

av-015. Reims/Cessna FA150L (I-FFSQ), 1 November 2019, Santa Severa, Italy. ANSV investigation report (Relazione d'inchiesta, PDF checked for this book): on a sightseeing flight the aircraft struck a power line on approach, crashed and caught fire, and both people on board, the pilot and the passenger, died (the original reads "Le due persone a bordo (pilota e passeggero) perdevano la vita"). The report states plainly what it could not establish: why the aircraft came in below the height it should have flown. The mechanism of margin erosion ran to completion, with height margin, track margin and visibility margin failing one after another, and where each one went cannot be reconstructed from the evidence.

Stop at the decision point: each segment of the approach in which height margin was progressively spent. On the table: the visual references of the moment and the judgment of the two people on board, precisely the part the evidence cannot fully restore. Outcome: two dead. The case is a proof by contradiction for the margin ledger: when even the investigator cannot rebuild where the margin went, the ledger kept beforehand is the only memory there is. Primary source: ANSV, Relazione d'inchiesta I-FFSQ, 2023; link at Appendix B, av-015.

The unreconstructability carries an evidentiary corollary worth writing down. Margin is spent in real time during flight, with height falling and track drifting, but if nobody is monitoring the live data, with no altitude alert triggered and no track deviation watch, the spending happens only in the physical world and in no record at all. The investigation can use only what was recorded (radar data, wreckage position), and the granularity of the record sets the ceiling on the reconstruction. Here even the investigator could not confirm why the pilot was below the height to be flown, and the limit of the granularity is the limit of the reconstruction. The corollary for any real-time system: consumption of a critical margin should be recorded as it happens, even if the record is automatic and unmonitored, because the record is the raw material of every future reconstruction. The direct channel to original records in Chapter 16 gains a technical argument here.

#### Case 5 (Maritime): The Pipe from 1998 (full card)

**Case ID**: ma-004.

**When and where**: about 05:30 UTC on 24 March 2024, when the steel stern trawler Opportune sank about 36 nautical miles east of Lerwick, Shetland (60°16.6N 000°02.3E); the bilge level alarm had sounded on the bridge 45 minutes before the sinking. The water at the site is more than 100 metres deep and the wreck was not examined. The trip sailed from Scalloway at 01:00 on 20 March with Peterhead as destination (p.7, p.8, p.11).

**What and who**: the owner Quiet Waters Fishing Company Limited, which had held and managed the vessel since October 2020; UK flag, built in Spain in 1998, length overall 27.85 metres, registered length 23.95 metres, 331 gross tonnes, no class society (p.7, p.11). Eight people on board: the skipper held a Deck Officer Class 2 (Fishing) certificate of competency, the mate also served as engineer, and six deckhands made up the crew (p.11). Inspection and certification fell to the Maritime and Coastguard Agency (MCA) under MSN 1872 (F) Amendment 1, treating the vessel as existing; a local shipyard and a piping contractor hired by the owner also appear in the record (p.11, p.15).

**What they were doing**: two timelines were running at once. The first was a trip spent managing the stern shaft. The vessel sailed at 01:00 on 20 March and fished grounds to the east for three days; at 18:00 on 23 March, hauling about 100 nautical miles northeast of Lerwick, a stern shaft high-temperature alarm came up on the bridge, the mate measured the stern seal with an infrared thermometer and found it above normal running temperature, the stern seal oil was partly emulsified, and the crew changed the oil. The skipper spoke to the owner ashore and decided to stop fishing and steam for Peterhead to have the shaft examined out of the water. For the first hour they read stern seal and shaft temperature every 10 minutes, then every 30 minutes for two hours, then hourly once the temperature held below 40°C (p.8, p.9).

The second timeline belonged to the machinery and had not moved in twenty-six years. The engine room sits under the main deck at the stern, mostly below the waterline, with a watertight door forward and one aft, and the door to the steering gear space behind it usually open (p.12). The main seawater intake manifold is 200 mm galvanised steel pipe, with cast iron sea valves either side of the main engine below the turn of the bilge, piping and valves under the floor plates about 4 metres below the waterline, installed as originally fitted at build (p.12). The same report carries numbers for the shell: 12 mm at the keel and 8 to 10 mm at the waterline when built, sacrificial anodes renewed in November 2022, impressed current cathodic protection also fitted (p.12). Somebody measured the shell. Nobody measured the pipe.

**The decision point**: the report names no individuals, so signatures can be recorded only to post and document. Four cuts, each with a date, a size and a carrier.

Cut one, 1998, when the build was fixed. Clause 4.1.11 of MSN 1872 (F) sets the default for seawater piping on a new vessel at aluminium bronze, cupronickel or a similar corrosion-resistant material, and the same clause leaves a gap: heavy-wall mild steel may be used for cross-ship intake mains, provided the bore is not less than 100 mm and the interior is galvanised after assembly (p.15). This main is 200 mm and satisfied both conditions. The size of the cut can be measured against SAN 29 of 2016: galvanising has a limited life, and within a 20-year installation life galvanised steel piping will almost certainly be replaced more than once (p.18). In 2024 the vessel was 26 years old (p.6), 6 years past that assumption. The carrier was the build drawing and the build survey, and the report records only "as originally fitted at build" with no approving post (p.12).

Cut two, October 2020, a one-day intermediate survey with the vessel lying at Scalloway. The MCA surveyor raised 38 deficiencies, three of which were written down: piping leaking, repair required; impressed current cathodic protection not working; lifejackets not stowed where readily accessible (p.14). The size of the cut lay in how the items were closed. The owner emailed photographs of the rectified defects to the MCA and the MCA closed them (p.14). The leak was treated as a local repair, with no pressure test and no thickness measurement. The carrier was the deficiency list and the email with photographs.

Cut three, November 2022, the five-year renewal survey completed at Fraserburgh, with one day out of the water and, six days later, one day in the water plus an inclining test (p.14). The carrier was the Fishing Vessel Hull Condition Report, and this was the largest and most quantifiable cut of the four. Shell plating inside and out was judged "good for age", with thickness measurement done by a local contractor six months before the survey and found compliant; shaft and rudder clearances independently checked by the yard and within tolerance; sea valves stripped, examined, found satisfactory and refitted; inclining test confirming the stability book still valid (p.14, p.15). Five items reached a conclusion one by one, hull, thickness, shaft and rudder, sea valves, stability, and the seawater piping item reached none; the certificate's own words are that no comment was made on the condition of the vessel's seawater piping (p.15). Over the same period, MCA internal guidance SAN 29 required the revised hull condition report to add a seawater piping section (p.18). The signatory was the MCA surveyor.

Cut four, 21 August 2023, when the vessel grounded while shifting berth in Fraserburgh harbour and a car tyre wrapped around the propeller; she was lifted out, the shaft removed, examined and repaired (p.15). The owner engaged a local contractor to examine the engine room piping: several pipe runs above the fuel tanks were removed, cleaned, examined and refitted, while the main manifold ring feeding the whole system stayed where it was, limited by the arrangement under the floor plates, and received a high-pressure water flush and rodding to clear mussels (p.15). The size: only the removable part could be inspected, and the 200 mm main ring was neither removed nor measured. The carrier was the contractor's examination and cleaning work, whose content the report records but whose work order number it does not. Approval sat with the owner, as the employer and the party responsible for seaworthiness (p.14).

**What was visible then**: the October 2020 list of 38 deficiencies, piping leakage and failed cathodic protection among them; the November 2022 hull condition report with five conclusions and one blank column; the record of the 21 August 2023 grounding, lifting out and shaft removal, together with the contractor's scope covering only the runs above the fuel tanks (p.14, p.15). The safety folder: 52 risk assessments, two of which name flooding as the hazard ("seawater intake valve failure", "stern damage while trawling"); three monthly drill subjects, man overboard, abandon ship, fire; no seawater piping failure assessment and no flooding drill (p.12, p.24). The engine room itself: sea valve handwheels sit below the floor plates and are worked with an extended bar kept beside the generator through a small hinged flap in the plating, with no valve position markings on the plating and no arrangement drawing at the entrance (p.13); both 25 m³/h electric bilge pumps could be started and stopped only inside the engine room (p.14). Public material: MGN 165 (July 2001) records that 18 of the 33 fishing vessel losses in 1999 came from flooding, and requires weekly pump trials, monthly sea valve exercise and sea valve inspection when the vessel is out of the water (p.17); of 230 fishing vessel flooding reports between 2013 and 2022, 78 (34 percent) led to the loss of the vessel, and 68 of those (87 percent) were more than 15 years old (p.19); in August and September 2012 the Spanish-built twin trawlers Audacious and Chloe T, built to the same design, sank one after the other, the combined investigation found seawater piping failure, neither crew could close the flooded intake valve, and that case produced SAN 29 (p.19). Another vessel of the same design was altered instead: fresh water cooling with box coolers removed the need for the two sea valves, and a fixed emergency bilge suction was fitted that could be worked from the upper deck with an independent power pump, without opening the engine room door (p.19). RNLI material from 2009 gives about 8 tonnes per minute through a ruptured 177.8 mm pipe (p.18). Total pump capacity on this vessel was about 66 m³/h (p.25). The MCA had held SAN 29 internally since October 2016; it was never merged into MSIS 27 and never published to owners (p.18, p.22).

What was learned only afterwards: the origin of the flooding could not be determined (p.6); the position, wall thickness and corrosion distribution of the failed section are unknown, since the wreck lies in over 100 metres of water and was not examined (p.11, p.21); SAN 29 had still not been merged into MSIS 27 as of September 2025 (p.18); the MCA never asked the owner for a piping diagram, and no piping test record existed before the renewal survey (p.23).

**What happened.** At 04:45 the bilge level alarm sounded. The mate went down to the engine room and found water at gearbox height, with the turning shaft throwing spray and water rising from under the floor plates; he went back for the skipper and minutes later the two went down together, and when the door opened there was white odourless steam, so they judged the space could not be entered (p.9, p.21). The skipper decided everyone should go to the bridge for lifejackets and immersion suits and prepare to abandon; at 0458 the mate sent a DSC distress alert on VHF; shortly after, propulsion and electrical power were lost (p.9, p.21). At 0510 the eight mustered on the bridge; the 10-person liferaft went over the port side and six deckhands jumped into it; the skipper activated the EPIRB and passed flares into the raft; the painter was torn away and parted, and the raft drifted off (p.9). At 0530 the stern went under and the bow floated for another 10 to 15 minutes; at 0556 a helicopter sighted two rafts; at 0623 all eight were recovered; at 0700 they landed at Sumburgh (p.11). No one was hurt, the vessel was a total loss, and pollution was minor (p.6, p.7). Back to the three questions. The most common answer to the first is "go over the pipes again", and in this case that action costs one pressure test or a set of thickness readings; the report records that before the renewal survey there was no record of any piping test (p.23). The item most easily missed under the second question is the phrase "rapid and practical means" in clause 2.2.6 of the MSN for existing vessels: a portable extension bar was on the register and formally reached the valve, and it stopped reaching it as soon as the floor plates flooded (p.16, p.24). The third question lands on the blank column in the November 2022 report, and the report supplies the time account: 45 minutes from alarm to sinking, about 15 minutes from alarm to loss of power (p.21).

**Options ruled out**: replacing the seawater piping as a whole, where the report records no section replacement at any point; the reasons can only have been cost and dock time, and the sentence about replacement more than once in 20 years was something the owner could not see (p.22, p.23). Upgrading to the new-build standard, since the corrosion-resistant material requirement in clause 4.1.11 applies to an existing vessel only when the seawater piping is replaced, and no replacement means no trigger (p.15). A pressure test or thickness measurement at the 2022 renewal, where SAN 29 itself required vessels over 10 years old to have internal examination and pressure testing or gauging on sections not replaced within 5 years; the report records no such requirement from the MCA and no piping diagram supplied by the owner (p.18, p.23). Removing the main ring during the August 2023 lift-out, where the piping lay under the floor plates and the contractor could take out only a few runs above the fuel tanks (p.15, p.23). Converting to fresh water cooling with box coolers, which a vessel of the same design had done, with conversion cost and survey route unrecorded in the report (p.19). Fitting a permanent extension bar or remotely operable valves, where the MSN asks existing vessels only for "rapid and practical means" and makes permanent access mandatory only on new ones (p.16, p.24). Making the bilge pumps startable from outside the engine room, which the report records as a current state and does not discuss (p.25). Adding a piping risk assessment and a flooding drill, where chapter 8 of the MSN requires monthly drills including flooding and MGN 570 has a flooding drill, and the folder held neither (p.12, p.16).

Five of these six have "satisfactory" as their keyword and appear as certificates and photographs. The one thing actually missing appears as a blank, and a blank enters no checklist.

**The point of escalation**: the moment the renewal certificate was signed in November 2022. Before that the piping had merely gone unrecorded; after it the piping was an absence inside a valid certificate. For the next 16 months (p.23) any reader saw five items each concluded, an inclining test confirming stability, and a certificate within its validity; in the same round the sea valves were stripped, passed and refitted, which stamped formal compliance on the arrangement (p.15, p.16). Through those 16 months the vessel worked the fishing season as usual, sailing, fishing for three days, hauling, raising an alarm, making for port, every step in the report inside procedure. A second chance came 7 months later, on 21 August 2023, when the grounding put the vessel out of the water, took the shaft out and brought the contractor aboard, and the main ring stayed in place and got a wash (p.15, p.23). The owner hired the contractor to examine the engine room piping, and what could actually be examined was a few runs above the fuel tanks. After that lift-out and before the sinking, the vessel never had another opportunity to show the pipes the air. Seven months later a 200 mm pipe breached 4 metres below the waterline, the breach position could not be confirmed, and the wreck sits in more than 100 metres of water (p.11, p.21).

**Failure chain**: technical layer. The 200 mm galvanised mild steel pipe thinned by corrosion in seawater, unmeasured here, with no wall thickness figure anywhere in the report, and breached under the floor plates 4 metres below the waterline. On the RNLI basis a hole of that size admits about 480 t/hour, and the vessel's total pump capacity was about 66 m³/h (two 25 m³/h electric pumps plus an 8 m³/h manual pump and an 8 to 10 m³/h portable one), roughly one seventh of the demand, with the two electric pumps together at 50 m³/h covering about one tenth (p.14, p.18, p.25). The report judged that even with the pumps running, meaningful dewatering before the loss of power would have been difficult (p.25). Closing the valve was the only means available, the handwheel sat under the floor, and reaching it took a hinged flap and an extension bar, while by 04:45, when the water was found, the floor plates were already submerged, so the valve was out of reach at the moment it was discovered (p.24). About 15 minutes separated the alarm from the loss of power (p.21).

Organizational layer. The hull condition report had no column for seawater piping (p.15). SAN 29 had existed since October 2016, was never merged into MSIS 27, and remained an internal document as of September 2025 (p.18). Piping shared a cramped space under the floor plates with the fuel lines, so visual inspection and non-destructive testing could cover only part of it, and the harder a section was to reach the easier it was to miss (p.23). There was no piping maintenance and inspection plan, no piping failure risk assessment and no flooding drill (p.12, p.24). The owner's records went down with the vessel (p.11).

Incentive layer. Replacement cost falls due at once and corrosion risk arrives later; about 75 percent of UK registered fishing vessels are over 15 years old (p.19); the rules drop a grade for the existing fleet, asking only for "rapid and practical means" and reserving permanent access for new vessels (p.16, p.24).

The break sits between the organizational and incentive layers: the rule downgraded "accessible" to "temporarily accessible" and the survey turned "piping" into a non-item, each move legal on its own, and together they left a 200 mm hole 4 metres below the waterline under the floor plates.

**Minimum counterfactual**: at the November 2022 renewal survey, do what SAN 29 required for vessels over 10 years old, a pressure test or thickness measurement on seawater piping sections not replaced within 5 years, and write the result into the piping section newly added to the hull condition report. One action, 16 months before the event, with the vessel already out of the water on her cycle, no extra dock time needed, and the cost of one pressure test or one set of thickness readings (p.14, p.18). The report confirms that the requirement already existed and that no record shows it was ever carried out (p.18, p.23). The same SAN 29 also required the owner to submit a piping diagram marking ferrous sections and their ages; the MCA did not ask and the owner did not submit, so neither side took that step (p.18, p.23). The report places this gap in the inspection framework, since SAN 29 was never merged into MSIS 27 and a surveyor might not know the document existed (p.23). Had a section failed, a 200 mm section could be replaced within the same docking; had it passed, that column would be the first set of pipe wall numbers any later reader could see.

**Outcome and sources**: the vessel was a total loss, all eight were rescued unhurt, and pollution was minor; the wreck lies in more than 100 metres of water and was not examined (p.6, p.11, p.21). MAIB issued five recommendations: 2026/107, merge SAN 29 into MSIS 27; 2026/108, require owners of vessels over 10 years old to provide piping diagrams marking the age of each ferrous section, and revise the hull condition report to add a seawater piping section; 2026/109, revise the safety guide for fishermen to require a valve position diagram posted outside the engine room entrance and a record of when each section was replaced; 2026/110, require owners to add flooding drills and a piping failure risk assessment and to implement MGN 165; 2026/111, require owners to compile piping diagrams and record the replacement date of each section (p.28). The investigation also issued a safety leaflet to the fishing industry (p.27, Annex A at pp. 30–31). These conclusions rest on crew statements, survey records and comparison with vessels of the same design (p.11, p.21, p.23). ## 25.4 What Shows Up Earliest

Six weak signals, each with the reason normally used to push it aside.

1. Design margin has no written account and lives only in the original designer's memory. Reason given: "it's all on the drawings."
2. Each assessment looks only at the change in hand, and nobody builds the cumulative view. Reason given: "we assessed every one of them."
3. "Still enough" has no quantified basis, or its basis is the state after the last cut. Reason given: "practice proves it."
4. The list of exceptions and exemptions is getting longer, and exemptions have started citing other exemptions. Reason given: "it's a special case."
5. Recovery from disturbance is slowing, so the same small fault now takes longer to clear. Reason given: "coincidence."
6. Near-miss reports are falling while operating intensity holds steady. Reason given: "safety is improving." (The cognate signal of the Chapter 11 criterion.)

Put the six back against the seven cases in this chapter. Signal one, margin only in memory, maps to Case 5, where knowledge of the seawater pipe's wall thickness lived on a 1998 drawing. Signal two, assessment of the change only, maps to Case 1, where each freeing-port change was assessed by itself. Signal three, "enough" measured from the last cut, maps to Case 2, in the reuse logic that sized relief from existing parameters. Signal four, exemption citing exemption, is most visible in finance and compliance, and the tiering assumption in Case 3, that only high-risk posts need the extra factor, is its configuration-screen version. Signal five, slower recovery, maps to every case, and it is the only living signal that runs across all seven. Signal six, fewer near-miss reports, maps to Case 7, where a low consumption figure with no near-miss backfill will come out low again next time.

Rank the six by cost of detection. Signal five is cheapest, since most systems already hold recovery time data. Signals two and three come next, costing a read of the review material. Signals one and four are most expensive, needing archive and ledger comparison. Start with the cheap ones as usual: pull your system's fault recovery times and plot five years of them, and the year the curve turns up is the year margin started going; every "running normally" after that turn deserves one more question.

## 25.5 How to Defend

Device 1, the margin ledger. Write the system's critical margins as numbers that can be added up: structural margin, time margin, staffing margin, financial margin, each with its original design value, its current value and its change history. The person who keeps the ledger is separate from the person who applies to cut it (the review-independence principle of Chapter 17). I-FFSQ demonstrates the necessity: with no ledger, even reconstruction afterwards is impossible.

Device 2, the cumulative-view review. When a reduction application reaches the table, the pre-read must include the reduction history of that margin, and the object of review shifts from "this cut" to "this line". The test shifts from "is there enough left after this cut" to "how many more cuts does this line allow". The curve itself is the deterrent, because anyone who cuts on a curve signs a cut with a name on it.

Device 3, the minimum waterline for margin. For margins that cannot be restored (structure, relief capacity, emergency capacity), set a level that may not be crossed and put it in the rules: any reduction below the waterline needs an external independent signature (the second confirmation seat for points of no return in Chapter 7). The waterline's design basis is the original design value, never the value from the last review, and that is what separates it from "still enough".

Device 4, recovery time monitoring. Treat the time a system takes to recover from an equivalent disturbance as the live indicator of margin: slower recovery is an early reading that margin is being eaten. It does not need to know which cut did the damage, only that the boat is sitting lower. The baseline needs periodic remeasurement and archiving, or even "slower" becomes unanswerable.

The four devices on this chapter's cases. Device 1, ledger, on Case 5: build a pipe wall thickness ledger by measurement point, with three columns, original design thickness, each measured value, and minimum waterline. Device 2, cumulative review, on Case 1: any change to deck arrangement arrives with the stability margin history curve attached. Device 3, waterline, on Case 2: set a waterline on the coverage factor of relief capacity against runaway rate, with startup prohibited below it, and derive the waterline from reactivity test data, which makes testing a precondition (no test, no waterline). Device 4, recovery monitoring, on Case 3: use the time to close a security gap, from discovery to fix, as the live indicator of security margin, and raise an alarm when the stock of open gaps grows.

The four failure modes. The ledger dies when it drifts from the physical object, with old numbers against new steel; the patch is to bind it to thickness measurement and survey readings, writing measured data straight into the ledger. The cumulative view dies when the curve misses cuts, recording only approved reductions and losing the ones that happened in fact, drift in execution and unregistered changes; the patch is a two-source curve, approved plus measured, with an alarm whenever measurement departs from approval. The waterline dies when its basis drifts, with someone proposing to reset it against current state; the patch is that any revision of the waterline needs an external independent signature on the same terms as a cut below it, making a drifting basis the same offence as the cut. Recovery monitoring dies when the baseline goes stale, with a five-year-old baseline used to judge today's recovery; the patch is periodic remeasurement written into the device's own operating instructions. The four patches share one health question: when was the ledger's number last calibrated against the physical object.

Margin has an accounting side worth one more passage, because it decides whether the ledger can enter the budget process at all: record margin as a liability. Seen from accounting, margin is insurance already paid for and not yet used, since the strength, distance and redundancy bought at design time were real money. Eating margin is selling that insurance for cash. Selling insurance has a defined bookkeeping form (a liability falls); in most organizations the consumption of margin has no account at all. Give it one, a safety margin liability statement, and every reduction automatically generates an entry (debit cash, credit margin), so the budget process sees a price for margin for the first time. The account does not need precise valuation, a range will do. It needs to exist: once it exists, consumption leaves a trace, and with a trace the defence concentration indicator of Chapter 20 finally has a denominator.

## 25.6 Boundaries with Adjacent Mechanisms

Against the invisibility of maintenance (Chapter 29): a maintenance backlog is work that should have been done and was not, the asset-side version, while eating margin is quantity that should have been kept and was not, the design-side version. The two often share a source and an outcome (Surfside takes both chapters), and the test is the object: time and actions go to Chapter 29, margin and strength stay here.

Against practical drift (Chapter 15): drift moves the distance between practice and text, while margin erosion moves the distance between the system and its boundary. Drift normally comes first, with practice loosening before margin is cut, and the two ledgers are worth reading together, since the line that loosened first is usually the one cut next.

Against the normalization of deviance (Chapter 11): normalization supplies the psychological permission to eat margin ("it was fine last time"), and this chapter supplies the physical result ("this time it was not enough"). Criterion (iii) in Chapter 11, the standard quietly lowered, lands in engineering terms exactly on the ledger in this chapter.
