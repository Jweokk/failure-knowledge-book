# Chapter 20 The Slack of Success (Failure of Success, Sitkin)

> What this chapter covers: how a long accident-free run makes defensive spending shrink; three criteria that show the mechanism is running, four carriers that let it shrink without anyone deciding, and the four devices that keep a defence alive through the years when nobody needs it.
> When to turn back: at the budget review where someone proposes cutting the backup, the drill or the inspection that "has not been used in years"; or when you hear yourself call one of your own processes a formality.
> Estimated reading time: 50 minutes.

Every line of defence fights an unwinnable campaign for its own existence. The standby generator has not started in ten years. The drill has found nothing in three. The inspection process has never stopped anything from leaving the gate. On the budget sheet these items carry a single name: no output for years. Their real output is the events that did not happen, and a non-event enters no ledger anywhere.

Lyle Sitkin's argument in the organizational learning literature sits exactly there. Success weakens learning. What an organization learns during a good run is "this way of working is fine", not "this way of working has produced nothing because the defences are in place". The two lessons differ by a clause. Some years later they differ by an entire accident.

## 20.1 What It Is

Definition: the slack of success (failure of success) is the structural tendency of success to weaken learning. During a good run the organization treats defensive spending, meaning backups, drills, inspections and redundancy, as cost with no output, and trims it or turns it into ritual. The cost of the shrink arrives late, and by the time it arrives the cut can no longer be traced to the decision that made it. The mechanism runs on the time axis. It is the organizational-learning version of margin erosion (Chapter 25). That chapter eats the reserve. This one eats the willingness to maintain the reserve.

Three criteria for onset. Any one of them means the mechanism is already running.

1. A defensive item has produced no visible value for a long time. There is no record of it ever catching anything.
2. It starts to be treated as surplus. A cutting proposal cites it as a reason, or in practice its execution has become a walk-through.
3. The first accident after the cut or the drift lands more than two years out. The causal chain has already broken in time, and nobody will join the ends.

The number in criterion 3 deserves a pause. Why two years? Budget cycles are counted in years, staff turn over on a two-year rhythm, and memory decays by the quarter. By the time the consequence of a cut appears in year three, the person who decided is gone, and the person who inherited the arrangement faces a condition that, as far as anyone there can tell, has always been the condition. Criterion 3 does not describe the size of the risk. It describes a structural guarantee that accountability will never land.

Sitkin's argument has two lines of context that matter here. What he describes is an asymmetry in organizational learning. Learning from failure is easy, because failure is concrete, vivid and has a party to attribute it to. Learning from success is hard, because success is diffuse, multiply caused, and offers no attribution point. The consequence of that difficulty is not that nothing gets learned. The wrong thing gets learned. The absence of consequence is attributed to current practice rather than to the defences and to luck. The output of that bad learning is a stable false belief: our way of working is safe. Once the belief sets, a defensive item's identity slides from necessary cost to historical leftover, and every cutting proposal at the budget table becomes a market test of the belief. A belief that is never contradicted keeps appreciating.

The attribution problem around "nothing happened" needs one finer note. The output of a defence and the output of luck cannot be told apart in advance, and are hard to tell apart afterwards, because they share one form of evidence: nothing occurred. The only window for telling them apart is the defence's catch record, the drill that found a fault, the sample check that stopped a batch, the review that corrected an error. An organization whose window has closed can keep its record going only on luck, and nobody meters the consumption of luck. That is the deep logic of Device 1 in this chapter, the ledger for non-events. The ledger does not exist so a defence can claim credit. It exists so the organization knows how much of its safety is design and how much is coincidence. Both proportions have value. Mixed together they are worth nothing, because the design share gets cut by mistake and the coincidence share gets trusted by mistake.

## 20.2 How It Grows

Three generating conditions.

The good run has to be long enough. A defence is treated as necessary only while the memory of the accident is fresh, and memory decays faster than accidents recur. The gap between those two rates is the growing season.

The cause of the good run has to be unattributable. The organization cannot say how much of "nothing happened" was the defence and how much was luck (Chapter 11's two hypotheses: margin to spare, or a good draw). If it cannot say, it cannot book the defence. An item that cannot be booked is an item waiting to be cut in any budget review.

Budget pressure has to recur. Something has to go every year, and the safest thing to cut is the line with no accident attached to it, because the argument is unanswerable in the room. It is true, and it cannot be refuted, unless the organization runs a system that books the non-events.

The consequence of slack has one precise property. It lands outside the decision-maker's tenure (Chapter 28), and the party it lands on is usually not the party who decided. That is what lets the mechanism repeat generation after generation inside one organization, with each generation convinced it is the first.

Slack travels in four carriers, and budget is only the most visible.

The first carrier is budget: line items cut, easiest to observe. The second is attention: inspection slides from measurement to signing in, and the four years in case 1 are a history of attention slackening. The third is skill: a drill slides from rehearsal to performance, which is the defensive side of Chapter 26's skill decay, and what is lost is the ability to do the task under pressure, not the ability to do it at all. The fourth is upkeep: standby equipment stops being powered up, switched over, turned over, which is the pump in case 3.

All four carriers share one property. They can shrink without a decision. Nobody has to vote for the cut. Busyness and a clean record only have to act together for long enough. That property also sets the difficulty of the fix: fighting a shrink that was never decided requires mechanisms that are decided, a ledger, a test period, a lookback date, and the whole function of those mechanisms is to turn unconscious drift back into conscious action.

The generational repeat has a further mechanism inside organizational memory. Accident memory is stored in people, and people move faster than accidents recur. The old hand leaves with the story of that time, and the story did not enter the file, or entered as one line. The newcomer faces a defence system that has process but no stories. Process can drift, because it specifies actions. Only a story commands respect, because it specifies the reason. The point of Device 4, institutionalized memory, is therefore not to write the story down. It is to write the story as a causal account a newcomer can use: what this defends against, what happened here, what happens if you cut it. One page against one resignation cycle is a poor ratio. It is also the only archive that can speak at a budget table.

The odds on that page can be narrowed further. Its real battlefield is somewhere else: the first ninety days of each new management team. A new manager has no memory of the defence system, and a first budget instinct runs toward cutting, which is the general preference of a new broom facing old accounts. Whether the page reaches the hand inside that ninety-day window decides whether the defence system survives each change of guard. The delivery mechanics are copied from Chapter 17: put the page in the standard attachments of the handover checklist, as a fixed column of the handover form. The incoming manager who signs for the handover signs for the page, and a signed receipt removes the excuse of not having seen it. A change of guard is a high-risk moment for a defence system (Chapter 28's tenure mechanism). A page in the handover pack is this chapter's seatbelt for that moment, at the cost of one procedure revision.

Beyond procedure revision, the page gets one gift of an update opportunity: after every relevant accident or industry event. An event makes memory briefly fresh, which is the gap described in condition one, and at that moment rewriting the page is cheapest and most accurate, because what the defence prevents has just been demonstrated. Write the trigger into Device 4's terms: within one month of a comparable accident anywhere in the industry, the relevant page is reviewed by compulsion, and the old version is archived once the reviewer signs. The month of an accident is when memory is most expensive and most honest. What gets written into "what it prevents" in that month is the highest fidelity the page will ever hold. An industry has few such months in a year. Catch them and the page stays accurate across two changes of guard.

The industry-level tuition in case 4 has one last layer: industry memory and company memory are stored in different places. A company's "never had an accident" rests on its own operating record, which in that case was the operator's past safety record. An industry's "never had an accident" needs a failure-mode library built across companies. The first is narrow by construction, since one company's luck is not the industry's rule. The second is the evidence base a certification decision should stand on. Building the library is the job of an industry body, which most sectors already have in their safety organizations. The company-side duty runs both ways: submit, meaning report your own abnormal events to the library even when they had no consequence, and read, meaning consult the library before certification and before a design change. Two-way flow is the circulation system of industry-level defensive memory. A blockage in either direction, and most companies read without writing, distorts the library's coverage, and a distorted library then misleads the next company's certification judgement.

The last link in that circulation is a compulsory lookback after an industry accident, and the grounding in case 4 was one industry-wide lookback. Once a new failure mode is demonstrated, the library is updated and every company's certification assumptions and inspection procedures are refreshed against it. The efficiency of the lookback depends on one quantity: the lag from event to procedure update. In an industry where the lag is measured in months, the industry layer compresses the slack window. In one where the lag runs to years, the second company is still working from the old assumptions, and the several similar events after case 4 are what that lag costs. The lag is a measure of industry governance, and shortening it needs no new technology. It needs the update process registered in advance: which procedures a library update triggers, who owns the rewrite, how many days are allowed for it, all written into the industry's own procedures so that when the event comes the update runs on the registered route.

The bill for the five cases can be settled side by side here. Case 1, four years of idling for one dangerous occurrence, with no injuries. Case 2, five days of retention and a second injury risk. Case 3, one vessel. Case 4, a fleet grounded for months and one injured firefighter. Case 5, two hundred million in deposits and a returned licence. The spread in scale is the point. Slack does not choose an industry and does not choose a size. It chooses time. Any organization whose good run is long enough and whose defensive accounts are unclear sits on the same spectrum, whether it runs a fishing boat or certifies a new aircraft type. The spectrum has no terminus, only the start of the next good run, and the four devices and three inserts in this chapter are the whole toolkit for making that run cost something different from the last one.

The procurement list is worth pricing for the finance reader. Device 1 is a ledger, costed in person-days. Device 2 is the live-testing regime, costed in equipment hours and labour, scaled to the length of the list. Device 3 is a lookback register, costed in paperwork. Device 4 is a page, costed in writing time and induction time. The four together usually cost less than one exercise. On the benefit side sit the five bills: one dangerous occurrence, one retention, one vessel, one grounding, one licence. This is not a precise cost-benefit analysis. It is an order-of-magnitude sanity check. Between the annual cost of defensive spending and the single-event cost of a defence failing lie two or three orders of magnitude. What is genuinely worth arguing about at the budget table is never whether to buy these tools. It is who maintains their ledgers and their dates once they are bought, and that post is written into the second sentence of Device 1 in section 5: a ledger needs an owner, and the owner needs to be assessed.

Assess the owner on this chapter's own measures: the ledger's update rate, the on-time rate of live tests, the execution rate of lookbacks, the timeliness rate of page reviews. All four are process measures, and the defect of process measures, that done is not the same as done right, is covered by sampling, with Chapter 11's quarterly spot check auditing the same post once a year. The owner brings a second benefit, which is that responsibility for the defence system lands inside this chapter's criteria. Every device has a name, the name has a second line, a deputy, and the organization's defensive ledger stops being a set of dates with no owners.

Once the dates have owners, the defence system stands on the same management table as every other asset: equipment depreciates, software renews, defences carry dates. The managerial meaning of that table needs one closing note. It turns safety from an atmosphere into a set of manageable state variables. An atmosphere slackens in a good run, necessarily, because it is a function of attention. A state variable does not slacken, because it is a function of the ledger. All of this chapter's effort comes down to that one translation: moving defence from atmosphere to variable. In an organization where the translation is done, the slackening still happens during a good run, since that is what people are like, but the floor it reaches is held by the ledger. Attention can leave. Dates do not move themselves.

The successor is the other beneficiary of dates that do not move. A new management team facing a dated defensive table starts the cutting discussion from a different question: whether these dates can be afforded, rather than whether the items are worth keeping. The ledger converts the argument about a defence's existence into an accounting of maintenance cost, and an accounting of maintenance cost is always cheap for a rational manager. That also fills in one column of Chapter 28's bill handover list, the column headed which defences I cut, since the entries can be exported straight from the ledger by date. This kind of mutual parts supply between mechanisms is normal in the later chapters of Part III. As the slack chapter, this one supplies more parts than most: the ledger to Chapter 11, the lookback to Chapter 28, live testing to Chapter 22, the page to Chapter 30. That is why it is one of the most cited nodes in the book's network of countermeasures. The time property of a defence is needed by nearly every chapter.

The most cited node is also the one most easily read as a universal remedy, so draw its boundary clearly. The ledger, live testing, the lookback and the page defend against a defence dying slowly. They do not defend against a defence that was wrong from the start. A defence designed against the wrong risk, sited in the wrong place, or run at a frequency too low to cover the exposure will show up on the ledger as a long run of zero catches, and under Device 1's convention a zero has two readings, genuine calm or nothing being caught. This book offers no automatic way to detect the third reading, that the defence itself is misdesigned. What that reading needs is an industry comparison, a catch baseline from comparable organizations, and the boundary check in Chapter 23. With the four tools plus an industry comparison, the correctness of a defence is fully monitored. Install the tools without the comparison and an organization will sit contentedly guarding a lighthouse that turns to no purpose.

The lighthouse image can light the whole of Part III. The twenty-four mechanisms are twenty-four ways for a lighthouse to fail, and each chapter's four countermeasures are the materials for rebuilding it. This chapter's lighthouse is the strangest, because its failure is the quietest. The others are broken by waves, which is what an accident is. This one goes out slowly on its own, eroded by the absence of accidents. Quiet is its most dangerous property, and the entire justification for the four tools. A quiet thing has to be made to make a noise on a schedule. Drills, live tests and lookbacks are the bells rung for a defence at fixed intervals. An organization that rings them is not necessarily safe. An organization that has been silent for years is certainly going slack.

There is one piece of good news for the slackening organization, and it makes the real ending of the chapter. Slack is reversible. The ledger can start being kept today. Live testing can be scheduled from the next cycle. The lookback can be logged from the next cutting proposal. The page can start with one hour tomorrow. None of the four tools waits for an accident. That is the largest difference between this chapter and most of the others. The countermeasures in a disaster chapter are easiest to accept after the disaster. The tools in this chapter are worth most before it. Every accident-free day the reader is having right now is the best installation window the four tools will ever get. Not acting while the window is open is the mechanism this chapter defines.

## 20.3 The Shape It Takes in Four Domains

### Case 1 (construction): Four Years in Which Nobody Noticed the Correct Ramp Was Not There

ce-007. Norwood Junction station, London Overground. RAIB report 09/2026, read in full for this book. The mobile ramp used by wheelchair passengers to board and alight had not been available since September 2021. On 1 July 2025 a train left the station carrying a ramp that should not have been on it, which is a dangerous occurrence, and by good fortune nobody was hurt. The report's key sentence is worth quoting whole: "The correct ramp had not been available since September 2021 and none of the station checks in the period up to the accident had identified this." Four years of absence, and every platform check in the period missed it. Over a long accident-free stretch, the check had become a signing-in. Its output was a record that somebody had looked, not a chance that somebody had found something.

Stop at the decision point: the platform check after the PGI of 14 January 2022. On the table: the ramp photographs in the check record, the missing model information in those photographs, and a question nobody asked, which is what a photograph of this can prove.

**What happened**: a dangerous occurrence, no injuries; the absence and the failure of the checks both enter the report. Primary source: RAIB, report 09/2026, 2026; link at appendix B, ce-007.

#### Case 1, full card: Four Years and One Tick

**Case ID**: ce-007.

**When and where**: September 2021, the month the platform 1 ramps were replaced and the month from which the report dates the absence, through 09:52 on 1 July 2025. Platform 1, Norwood Junction station, London Borough of Croydon, the station standing 8 miles 55 chains south of London Bridge (RAIB p. 10). The report was published in June 2026 and runs to 52 pages.

**What and who**: Arriva Rail London (ARL), operating the London Overground concession for TfL since November 2016 and the station operator at Norwood Junction, employing both the driver of 9D18 and the station staff who deploy the ramp (RAIB p. 11). TfL is the concessioning authority and the rolling stock owner (p. 11). The mobile platform ramps are provided by Govia Thameslink Railway (GTR) under contract (p. 26), and the manufacturer is Portaramp UK Limited. Carlisle Support Services supplies contract station staff at the station (p. 11). The train was class 378 five-car electric multiple unit 378148 (p. 11). Posts involved: station manager, who carries out the PGI, station assistant dispatcher (SAD), train dispatcher (TD), driver.

**What they were doing**: Norwood Junction has six platforms, and platform 1 is the only one reachable without stairs and a subway. There is no level boarding between the platform surface and the floor of a class 378, so a wheelchair user can board and alight only by the mobile ramp (RAIB p. 18). The ramp is kept locked on the platform and opened by station staff with a key. It is used a few times a day, with no pattern, and a driver would not meet it daily (p. 18).

On the morning of 1 July 2025 a passenger who lives in Norwood arrived by mobility scooter about 10 minutes before 9D18 was due, passed through the gateline and asked the SAD for assistance under TfL's turn up and go service (p. 13). The passenger travelled this line regularly and had boarded at Norwood Junction and at other stations before (p. 13). The SAD telephoned the ARL passenger assistance team, gave the destination and the service, then took the ramp from its store with the key and waited with the passenger towards the rear of the platform (p. 13). 9D18 was the 09:46 West Croydon to Highbury & Islington, and it entered platform 1 at 09:51:35 (p. 9, p. 13). Once the alighting passengers were clear, the SAD went to the accessible door of the third car, put the ramp's locking feet into the holes in the door threshold, then stood on the ramp to test that it was secure (p. 13, paragraph 22 and figure 5). For the driver this was the fourth trip of the day: booked on at New Cross Gate depot at 06:44, then 07:15, 07:45 and 08:47 back to back, with 9D18 the last turn round (p. 13).

**The decision point**: four moments.

1. September 2021, platform 1, GTR replacing the platform ramps. Before the replacement the station held two ramps, one compatible with class 377 and one compatible with class 378 (RAIB p. 26, paragraph 57). After it, both were a type compatible with class 377 only. The report's word for this is unintentionally (p. 26, paragraph 57). The scope was the two ramps on platform 1. The report does not record the replacement as an equipment change, and records no work order, no approving post and no accepting officer. What remains is the result: the class 378 ramp with the extension arm was no longer on the station. Where the extension arm came from is stated. In 2014 ARL's predecessor, London Overground Rail Operations Limited, specified a ramp with an extension arm for class 378 trains (p. 26, paragraph 54). The report's conclusion states the step as a finding, "The correct portable access ramp for class 378 trains had been removed", and lists it as the first causal factor (p. 43, paragraph 122a).
2. 14 January 2022, a PGI, the station manager's planned general inspection, and the first record after the replacement to leave images of the ramps. Two photographs were taken. Neither ramp in them had a door-blocking arm, and the model was not recorded (p. 27). The same record set on 16 July 2020 had recorded three types, one Overground, one Southern, one Thameslink, with the arms on the latter two visible in the photographs (p. 26). The criteria did not change between the two occasions. What the record contained did.
3. 10 December 2024, the platform risk assessment for Norwood Junction, prepared against SQE 04.11-A, Train Dispatch and Platform Train Interface Risk Assessment, issue 2, January 2024 (p. 39, paragraph 108). The report states that the assessment did not consider the risk arising from ramp use, and that no other assessment covering the task was found.
4. From February 2025, ARL issued a new step-by-step ramp procedure: hold the ramp before the train arrives, stand where the driver can see you (p. 28, paragraph 68). By the time of the accident, 261 of the 291 directly employed station staff had been briefed. The SAD on duty that morning was not among them (p. 28, paragraph 68). The report records neither the post that approved the procedure nor any technical check of it against ramp type.

**What was visible then**: the two ramps on the station looked almost identical, the locking feet and the threshold holes were the same size, and a class 377 ramp could be fitted to a class 378, take a passenger's weight and work (RAIB p. 25, paragraph 51). The ramp label carried a serial number and a model, and the model denoted size, not the trains it suited (p. 27, paragraph 59 and figure 17). The PGI criteria came from RIS-3703-TOM appendix B, and the two questions touching the ramp asked whether it was secured correctly and whether it was in good condition and compatible with the trains calling at the platform. The standard does not explain what compatible means (p. 40, paragraph 110).

Three PGI records carry ramp entries and photographs: 16 July 2020, 14 January 2022 and 16 February 2023 (pp. 26–27). In 2020 a train had left Edmonton Green with a ramp still on it, and ARL had issued a Safe Every Time bulletin afterwards requiring a high-visibility vest and a position where the driver could see the staff member (p. 28, paragraph 67). On the driver's side the document in force was SQE 20.6, the dispatch action list, which includes a z-scan of the monitors, a check that the timestamp is running, and the words "never rely only on the interlock light" (p. 20, p. 22, paragraph 39). Before February 2025 the only formal document on the ramp was that 2020 bulletin, and the report records no refresher training in the interval (p. 28, paragraph 68, p. 38, paragraph 102). Station staff had no training in matching a ramp to a train type (p. 27, paragraph 61, p. 38, paragraph 102). The platform was not busy that morning, and the SAD was the only person in the dispatch corridor (p. 33, paragraph 87).

What was learned afterwards. September 2021 is the start of the absence. Class 377 doors are of the plug type, with the leaves closing inwards, so a ramp lies inside that sweep and needs no extension arm, while class 378 doors slide into pockets in the bodyside and a ramp without an arm does not block them (pp. 23–25). The driver had used a personal phone on duty, with five calls lasting from 33 seconds to 25 minutes (p. 31). The monitor on the driver's side cropped the staff member's raised arms out of frame (p. 37). A reconstruction measured the ramp's vertical image height on the screen at 2 to 3 millimetres, below the lower limit of 2.6 to 3.6 millimetres, while the report also judged the ramp clearly visible in the reconstructed image (p. 36, paragraphs 91 to 92).

**What happened**: at 09:52:05 the driver pressed door close, with the SAD still standing on the ramp carrying out the weight test and the scooter passenger still on the platform. At 09:52:13 the train started away with the ramp still on it (RAIB p. 14). The ramp was dragged to the platform end, struck the end stop and fell into the side drain between the running line and the railway boundary (p. 16). Nobody was injured. The SAD was beside the ramp as it began to move, and a passenger sitting on a platform bench was warned by the TD's whistle and pulled her legs back before the ramp reached her (p. 7, p. 14). One door and a section of bodyside panelling on 9D18 were lightly damaged, and the ramp was written off (p. 7). Platform 1 was closed for about 25 minutes and reopened around 10:15 (p. 16). The passenger was assisted onto a later train, and told the RAIB that she now travels by train less often and experiences severe anxiety when she does (p. 17). The driver was screened for alcohol and drugs at Silwood sidings with a clear result (p. 17), and has not driven a train for ARL since (p. 46). The report classifies the event as a dangerous occurrence and makes six recommendations and two learning points (p. 1, p. 7). Recommendations 3 and 4 sit at the level of regulation and industry standard: the accessibility technical specification requires a ramp to be secured on the train but does not require door closing to be prevented while a ramp is in place (p. 25, paragraph 52, p. 48).

Back to the three questions. The commonest answer to the first is to add photographic evidence to the checklist, but a photograph prevents a false tick and does not prevent a look that never happens. The action has to be defined first, as walk, look, compare, and then a photograph has something to be a photograph of. The most overlooked item in the second question is the four years. They show that the idling was structural rather than personal: the system had never defined what the check was checking. The third question lands on the definition of the checking action. Every tick should correspond to one physical action and one comparison standard, and any check item whose definition is missing should come off the list, since such items produce nothing but a feeling of safety. Primary source: RAIB, report 09/2026, 2026; link at appendix B, ce-007.

**Options ruled out**: adding a functional check to the PGI, fitting the ramp, closing the doors and seeing whether the interlock is obtained, never reached the table. The PGI worked to the RIS-3703-TOM criteria, and the standard asks about securing and condition only, not about door closing (p. 40, paragraphs 110 to 111). Marking ramps by train type was also never tabled, since the label held a serial number and a size model (p. 27, paragraph 59). What ARL did after the accident was exactly that, adding the trains a ramp suits on ramps at multi-type stations and hanging a tag on the handle of any ramp incompatible with class 378 (p. 46, paragraph 130). Station staff telling the driver when a passenger was boarding had been ARL practice at its stations and faded over time, and the report's most likely explanation is the lengthening of class 378 units from three cars to five (p. 28, paragraph 65). The February 2025 procedure required only that staff stand in the driver's sightline, and did not restore the verbal notice (p. 28, paragraph 68). Making ramp deployment a safety-critical task never reached the table either. ROGS guidance does not clearly list ramp deployment among safety-critical activities, so ARL kept it out of its competence system, and the practice on the station was that anyone who had been shown the job could do it (p. 39, paragraphs 104 to 105). Driving on the interlock light alone was ruled out on paper: SQE 20.6 states that an object under 30 millimetres trapped in a door still allows the interlock to be obtained, and adds "never rely only on the interlock light" (p. 22, paragraph 39).

**Early signals**:

1. 14 January 2022: neither ramp in the PGI photographs had a door-blocking arm, while the photographs of 16 July 2020 showed the arms on the Southern and Thameslink ramps (pp. 26–27). The two sets sit side by side in figure 16 of the report (p. 27). Reason it was pushed past: the criteria asked about securing and condition, both ramps fitted and took weight, and "in good condition" was satisfied.
2. 16 February 2023: the PGI checked the ramp labels, and the label detail went unrecorded (p. 27). Reason it was pushed past: the act of checking the label entered the record, the model column was blank, and the record looked complete.
3. A class 377 ramp on a class 378 worked perfectly, except that it did not block the door (p. 25, paragraph 51, p. 27, paragraph 61). Reason it was pushed past: fitting was taken as compatible, and the report records that staff therefore had no reason to raise it (p. 27, paragraph 61).
4. The Safe Every Time bulletin issued after the Edmonton Green event of 2020 reached the mailbox of the SAD involved, and the report found no evidence that it was read, with no refresher training between then and February 2025 (p. 28, paragraphs 67 to 68). Reason it was pushed past: the bulletin had been issued, and the covering was booked.
5. The depth of the PGI had been moving since 2020. The 2020 record listed three ramps and their uses, the 2022 record listed no model, and the 2023 record noted photographs and a label check without the label's detail (p. 26, paragraph 58, p. 27). Reason it was pushed past: the depth of a check is the station manager's call, and either depth counts as done.

**The point of escalation**: February 2025, when ARL began issuing the new step-by-step ramp procedure (RAIB p. 28, paragraph 68). This was the last attempt before the accident to put ramp knowledge back out onto the platform, and it reached 261 of 291 directly employed station staff. Thirty were not covered. The SAD on duty was one of the thirty (p. 28, paragraph 68). The illustration in the procedure shows the staff member holding the ramp, standing where the driver can see, which is the same instruction as the 2020 bulletin, and neither mentions train type (p. 28, paragraph 68, figure 21). The report records no approving post for the procedure and no technical review by anyone. On the day, the SAD's position was screened by passengers and by the platform canopy, so the instruction to stand in the driver's sightline did not hold (p. 28, paragraph 66). Over the four months that followed, the only ramp information circulating on the platform was that it fitted. The difference between fits and matches was carried by no document. Go back one step further, to September 2021: once only one model remained on the station, the PGI photographs of 2022 and of 2023 recorded it as this platform's standard fit (p. 27).

**Failure chain**: technical layer. Class 378 doors slide into a pocket in the bodyside and need an arm reaching out from the doorway to block them. A class 377 ramp has no arm, but its locking feet match the threshold holes, so it fits, takes weight and still lets the doors close and the interlock be obtained (RAIB pp. 23–25). The door system detects only an obstruction thicker than 30 millimetres (p. 23, paragraph 47). The ramp hangs outside the vehicle, beyond the gauge, and sweeps people and objects off the platform as soon as the train moves (p. 23, paragraph 44).

Organizational layer: the ramps are provided by GTR under contract and used by ARL staff (p. 26, paragraph 57), and the ARL PGI did not require anyone to check which ramp the station should hold (p. 40, paragraph 111). The PGI criteria came from appendix B of RIS-3703-TOM, compatible was never defined, and the standard does not ask for door closing to be checked (p. 40, paragraphs 110 to 111). Staff had no training in matching ramp to train type (p. 27, paragraph 61). Ramp deployment was outside ARL's competence system (p. 39, paragraphs 104 to 105). The platform risk assessment of 10 December 2024 did not touch the ramp (p. 39, paragraph 108).

Incentive layer: the ramp is used a few times a day with no pattern (p. 18, paragraph 31). Under DOO the driver alone is responsible for dispatch, and station staff have no role in the dispatch process (p. 18, paragraph 35). Identifying the train type produced nothing in the period, since the ramp still took weight, while the cost fell in the period, because reading a label, comparing types and reporting all had to fit inside the few minutes of one arrival.

Three PGI records across three years treated "the station has a ramp" as a verified fact. Not one of them was required to answer which ramp it was. The boundary breaks in the organizational layer: the word compatible in the criteria never landed on any document, so the check passed every time.

**Minimum counterfactual**: in September 2021, after GTR replaced the platform 1 ramps, the station manager carries out one comparison of the ramp against the trains that call, and writes the result into that month's PGI record. One action, in the month of the replacement, and no luck required. The ramp label carries the model (p. 27, paragraph 59), the three train types calling at the station are known (p. 23, paragraph 48), and the PGI record of 16 July 2020 already listed what each of the three ramps was for (p. 26). Every PGI in the four years after had a chance to see the same thing (pp. 26–27). The report does not put this action forward. It is reconstructed from what the PGI could already do: the station manager was taking photographs and writing records, and in 2020 wrote down the purpose of each ramp (p. 26, paragraph 58). The report's own landing point runs the same way. Recommendation 1 asks the operator to review its management processes to confirm that ramps fully compatible with the trains that call are available (p. 47).

**Outcome and sources**: no injuries. One door and a section of bodyside panelling lightly damaged, one ramp written off, platform 1 closed for about 25 minutes. The report lists a direct cause, three causal factors and three underlying factors (p. 43). Six recommendations go to First Rail London, items 1 and 2, the Department for Transport, item 3, the RSSB, items 4 and 5, and the ORR, item 6, with two learning points besides (pp. 47–49). After the accident ARL audited the ramps at every London Overground station, annotated the trains each ramp suits at multi-type stations, and tagged the ramps incompatible with class 378 (p. 46, paragraph 130). It also carried out a ramp use risk assessment and agreed a proactive maintenance and inspection plan with the manufacturer (p. 46, paragraph 129). Primary source: RAIB, report 09/2026, Dangerous occurrence involving a portable access ramp at Norwood Junction station, 1 July 2025, June 2026, 52 pages, Department for Transport, full PDF read.

Case 1 carries an extension that runs across industries. Every check item of the confirm-it-is-there kind, whether the ramp is there, the extinguisher is there, the spare part is there, the permission is there, shares one failure mode. Presence is the state most easily assumed, because presence is not consumed by use. Items that measure consumption, oil level, pressure, remaining margin, have physical decay to remind people. Presence items have none. Their state changes at the moment the object is taken, and the moment of taking is exactly when the organization is least likely to be watching, since that is a transfer, a loan or a redeployment. The design requirement for a presence item is therefore higher than for any other: a physical action definition, meaning walk to it and look at it, plus a change trigger, meaning any movement of the object must be logged. With both in place, four years of idling has a chance of being stopped by a sample check in year four.

### Case 2 (healthcare): Counting Drifts in an Accident-Free Period

me-004. UK obstetrics. HSIB investigation, Detection of retained vaginal swabs and tampons following childbirth, 2019, archived snapshot read for this book. A vaginal tampon was left in place after delivery and was not discovered until five days after discharge, in the report's words: "It was left in and not discovered until five days after leaving hospital." The swab count and tracking process exists to prevent exactly this, and its rigour varies naturally during a period with no accidents. No decision is needed. Only busyness. During the five days the patient attended twice outside the hospital, and neither attendance thought to check something that ought to have been cleared already.

Stop at the decision point: the moment during a delivery when a counting action was skipped or simplified. On the table: the pace of the labour, the count sheet present, and the belief that this has never happened here.

**What happened**: five days of retention; the report makes the counting and tracking system an investigation focus. Primary source: HSIB, 2019; link at appendix B, me-004 (archived link, grade A−).

The phrase from case 2, no decision needed, only busyness, deserves one further note. Busyness is slack's best disguise. A count simplified on a busy day differs from a count skipped on a quiet one, because the busy simplification has a wholly legitimate reason, an urgent labour, and the person doing it believes the reason. A justified simplification is the hardest kind to defend against, because it bypasses every control designed for laziness. Nobody in the room thinks they are breaking the process. Everyone thinks they are saving the patient. The defence therefore belongs in structure rather than supervision: design the count as a physical sequence that cannot be simplified, with the swabs on a physical stub carrying a count strip, so that simplification is physically impossible and busyness cannot compress it. An engineered defence does not depend on anyone's conscientiousness, which is Chapter 9's three-layer model speaking to this chapter again. Any action guarded only by conscientiousness is missing an engineering constraint.

### Case 3 (maritime): A Standby Pump Nobody Had Verified

ma-006. Stern trawler Freedom II (CN 111), 21 February 2024, 11 nautical miles south-west of Oban, Scotland. MAIB report 8/2026, read in full for this book. The suction pipe of the deck wash pump lacked support, fractured and let water in, and the vessel sank. The crew was rescued by liferaft and nobody was injured. The investigation also found that not every pump fitted on board was serviceable. Paper and steel had parted company over the accident-free years. The list said the pump was there. The deck said it was not. The only way to make those two lines agree, which is to start the thing once, had not been scheduled for years.

Stop at the decision point: the most recent moment at which the standby pump's availability was assumed rather than verified. On the table: the entry in the equipment list, the distant record of the last live test, and the day's work priorities.

**What happened**: the vessel was lost, with no injuries. Primary source: MAIB, report 8/2026, 2026; link at appendix B, ma-006.

The split between list and steel in case 3 has an intermediate state worth naming: serviceable on paper. An equipment list is an account, and reconciling an account against objects has a mature institution in fixed assets, which is the stock check. In safety equipment that institution is often missing. Who confirms that a standby pump, a life-saving appliance or an emergency supply is available, when, and by what action, is a question most lists do not answer. The gap in case 3, a reconciliation action that was to start the machine once and was not carried out for years, is on the same scale as the four years in case 1. A small fishing vessel without a stock-check regime can be forgiven, but the same structural gap exists in systems far larger than a trawler, as case 4's certification system shows. A stock-check regime for safety equipment is the institutional form of Device 2 in this chapter: every claim of availability carries the date of a live test, and availability without a date is treated as unverified.

The automatic downgrade of unverified status needs a recovery channel beside it: a retest restores the status. Downgrading is a prompt, not a penalty. Its organizational meaning is that this line of defence is currently guaranteed by the ledger rather than by a test, and once the guarantor has changed, a decision-maker citing the defence sees unverified instead of available, which makes the citation honest by itself. Honest list states get at the root of this chapter's mechanism. Most of slack's harm travels through lists, since both case 1 and case 3 are lists telling lies. Once lists stop lying, the remaining harm is the shrink itself, which is visible, measurable and repairable.

#### Case 5 (finance): Cost Slack in a Cheap-Money Era (Condensed)

st-004. Xinja, an Australian neobank. Failory Cemetery archive, quoting a customer email from CEO Eric Wilson, read for this book. The bank raised A$204.7 million, took about US$200 million in deposits in its first month, and then, as capital conditions tightened, stopped taking business and returned its licence. The CEO's email names the model's dependence on its environment: "increasingly difficult capital-raising environment". This chapter's reading: the success of the loose-capital years, measured in the speed of deposit gathering, hid a slackening cost structure. A customer acquisition model built on high deposit rates works only in years when funding is cheap. The longer the good run, the looser cost discipline became, and the deeper the model's hidden dependence on its environment. A tightening environment is not a black swan. It is the normal state of an interest rate cycle, and the model held no margin for the normal state. The decision point is the moment high-rate acquisition was written into the growth model without an environmental sensitivity test. Minimum counterfactual: require an environmental sensitivity test as an attachment to the growth model, covering the effect of rising funding costs on the model's survival. Primary source: Failory Cemetery, quoting the CEO's customer email, 2020; link at appendix B, st-004 (evidence grade E).

### Case 4 (aviation): Assumptions Not Verified at Certification

**Case ID**: av-002.

**When and where**: November 2006, the key test, to 26 August 2011, when the type certificate was issued. The event occurred at 10:21 on 7 January 2013, US Eastern time. Seattle, Washington, for the certification review at the FAA aircraft certification office, and gate 8A, terminal E, Edward Lawrence Logan International Airport, Boston, Massachusetts, for the event itself.

**What and who**: the Boeing Company's 787-8 programme and its electrical power system (EPS) safety assessment team; Thales Avionics Electrical Systems, designer of the electrical power conversion subsystem; GS Yuasa, designer and manufacturer of the main and APU batteries; the FAA's Seattle Aircraft Certification Office and the Boeing authorized representatives acting under its organization designation authorization (ODA). The aircraft was operated by Japan Airlines (JAL).

**What they were doing**: the 787 programme was launched in April 2004, first flew in December 2009, was certified in August 2011 and was first delivered in September 2011 (AIR-14/01 p. 17). In September 2004 Boeing told the Seattle certification office that the main and APU batteries would be lithium-ion. This would be the first transport category aeroplane with permanently installed rechargeable lithium-ion batteries (p. 60). The FAA reviewed 14 CFR 25.1353 and concluded that the existing rules covered several failure, use and maintenance characteristics of lithium-ion batteries insufficiently, and went to special conditions under 14 CFR 21.16 (p. 60). Issue paper SE-9 was issued on 31 March 2006, draft special conditions on 23 April 2007, and the final special conditions 25-359-SC on 28 September 2007, nine in number and effective 13 November 2007 (p. 61).

Three strands ran in parallel through certification. A means of compliance was proposed for each of the nine special conditions, by analysis or by test, with the deliverables list submitted alongside the certification plan. GS Yuasa and Thales carried out battery-level analysis and qualification testing, with Boeing reviewing and approving the results. Boeing then folded those results into the EPS safety assessment (p. 62). The report's later judgement is that traceability between the special conditions and the deliverables was never established (p. 86). Battery qualification evidence came in two classes, abuse testing (external short circuit, overcharge, overdischarge, high-temperature storage) and the safety assessment, and the compliance conclusion for each of the nine conditions rested on one class or the other (p. 62, p. 83). The FAA approved the initial certification plan on 22 December 2005 and revision C on 8 January 2007 (p. 62). On 16 September 2009 the EPS safety assessment was finalized, containing a failure modes and effects analysis (FMEA), a functional hazard assessment (FHA) and a fault tree (p. 62).

**The decision point**: three irreversible moments, each attached to a document, five years apart.

The first came in November 2006. GS Yuasa ran a research and development nail penetration test: a steel nail driven through the electrodes of a fully charged cell, with the battery fully charged, ungrounded and of pre-production build, at an ambient temperature of 50–59°F, the temperature of an electrical and electronic equipment bay on a typical flight (p. 64, footnote 108). The penetrated cell's surface heated, the cell and the battery case emitted smoke, and adjacent cells heated without venting (p. 65). The test was run once (p. 64, p. 65). It was not a certification requirement but an R&D test (p. 64, footnote 107), and its result went into Boeing's EPS safety assessment as the measured basis for the conclusion that the effects of an internal short were limited (p. 65). The reviewers were a Boeing project engineer, a safety, reliability and maintainability engineer, and an authorized representative acting for the FAA (p. 64, footnote 109). This is the moment a single-point reading was promoted to a general conclusion. Boeing, Thales and GS Yuasa later said further battery-level R&D nail penetration tests had been carried out, but no records survived that the NTSB could examine (p. 81, footnote 135).

The second came on 16 September 2009, when the EPS safety assessment was finalized (p. 62). The FHA classified venting with smoke and fire as catastrophic, and venting with smoke but no fire as hazardous (p. 63). Three probability requirements followed: venting below 1×10⁻⁷, smoke below 1×10⁻⁷, and flammable liquid spill below 1×10⁻⁹. The assessment concluded that the only known failure mode leading to venting with smoke and fire was overcharge, so overcharge protection was designed to 1×10⁻⁹, and internal short circuit was grouped with the venting without fire case (p. 63). In the same assessment the battery-level FMEA states that no single-point failure of the main or APU battery leads to catastrophic consequences (p. 64).

The third came on 26 August 2011, when the FAA issued the type certificate, with a certification basis of Part 25 plus 25-359-SC (p. 61). The issuer was the FAA, and the compliance conclusions were made by Boeing's authorized representatives, Boeing having received ODA approval in August 2009, which allows its representatives to make FAA compliance findings on specified reports and tests (p. 65).

All three occasions carry a date and named hands. What they lack is one document on the same table. None of them required the assumption to be written down, that thermal runaway in a single cell does not propagate to adjacent cells, and none required a test of it (p. 12, p. 86).

**What was visible then**: on 31 March 2006 the FAA wrote the danger itself into SE-9, the issue paper produced for this aircraft. Lithium-ion batteries are "significantly more susceptible to internal failures that can result in self-sustaining increases in temperature and pressure", and manufacturers of mobile phones and electric vehicles had already had problems (p. 61). GS Yuasa's service data showed more than 14,000 cells of the same design in industrial use, none of which had vented or gone into thermal runaway, and from that record a venting rate for the 787 battery of about once per ten million flight hours was extrapolated (p. 64). No analysis of how closely industrial duty and environment resembled airborne duty and environment accompanied the data (p. 64).

The text of special condition 2 reads "the design of the lithium-ion battery must prevent self-sustaining, uncontrolled increases in temperature or pressure" (p. 86, footnote 147). None of the nine conditions required battery-level test data under single-cell thermal runaway (pp. 99–100). In July 2009, during a whole-aircraft test at the UTC Aerospace Systems APSIF, a battery was inadvertently overcharged, one cell vented, and nothing propagated (p. 82). In September 2009 two engineering venting tests observed electrolyte leakage, the battery case was given additional sealing, and the pre-production battery's vent disk arrangement differed from the final design (p. 82, footnote 138). Four qualification abuse tests were run, two external short circuits, one overcharge, one overdischarge, all at 158°F, none of which produced thermal runaway (p. 83).

What was learned afterwards: the NTSB and UL repeated the nail penetration at 158°F on an ungrounded battery, and single-cell thermal runaway propagated to every cell in the case (p. 82, footnote 137). Two of the three heat transfer tests run by the Japan Transport Safety Board propagated, and both were on grounded batteries (p. 82). The failure in the accident battery began with an internal short in cell 5 or cell 6 (p. 91).

**Stop point**

> **Stop here. Decide before you turn the page.**
>
> 1. What would you do? Write the action, not "pay more attention".
> 2. Which item here are you most likely to miss, and why does it look unimportant?
> 3. If you may set one checkpoint, when is it and what does it check?

**What happened**: at 10:21 on 7 January 2013 the APU battery failed and the APU shut down automatically. Maintenance found heavy smoke coming from the battery case lid and two flames about 3 inches long at the connector. The battery was removed at 11:57, about 80 minutes after the first report, and the event was under control at 12:19 (p. 14, p. 17). The 183 passengers and 11 crew were already off the aircraft, and nobody on board was hurt. One firefighter suffered a minor injury, and a firefighting captain suffered minor burns to the neck when the battery "exploded" (p. 14, p. 17).

Grounding and modification: after the Takamatsu event of 16 January, the FAA issued emergency airworthiness directive 2013-02-51 the same day, Japan's Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism issued an equivalent the next day, and other national authorities required grounding in turn (p. 65). On 19 April the FAA approved a redesign of the battery system, on 22 April AD 2013-08-12 replaced the emergency directive, effective 26 April, and the fleet returned to service once modified (p. 66).

Back to the three questions. The commonest answer to the first is to repeat the test at 158°F, on a grounded battery, of final configuration. In 2009 that cost weeks of rig time and one battery. The saving from skipping it was paid in grounding, redesign and recertification. The most easily missed item in the second question is the three conditions buried in the 2006 test report: the temperature, the absence of grounding, and the pre-production battery. They sit in brackets and footnotes, they read like lab notes, and they are in fact the scope within which the conclusion holds. The third question lands on the certification plan. Every special condition needs to trace backwards to a measurable performance requirement and to a test, and any item that cannot be traced is a gap.

**Options ruled out**: a thermal runaway test at the 158°F maximum operating temperature was not on the qualification test list, because the assessment held the effect of that failure mode to be no worse than a single cell venting (p. 82). A grounded battery to reproduce the installation on the aircraft was not used; the batteries in the 2006 test and in the abuse tests were ungrounded (p. 83). Repeated tests to establish repeatability were not run, only the single test (p. 82). A retest on a final-design battery was not done, the test battery being of pre-production build with a different vent disk arrangement (p. 82, footnote 138). Writing "battery performance under single-cell thermal runaway" into the battery specification control document (SCD), with a test hung off it, was not done, and because the requirement was absent, no such item appeared on the qualification test list. The report notes that the absence is exactly why the need for a thermal runaway test was "less visible" to Boeing's authorized representatives and to FAA engineers (p. 86). A structured comparison of industrial cell service against 787 duty was not done, and the report found no such document (p. 83). The reason given for turning down each of these comes back to the same sentence: the assessment had already shown the effect to be limited, so no further test was needed.

**Early signals**:

1. 31 March 2006: the FAA wrote in SE-9, prepared specifically for the 787, that lithium-ion was "significantly more susceptible" to internal failure leading to thermal runaway, and listed the mobile phone and electric vehicle precedents (p. 61). Reason it was pushed past: 14,000 industrial cells with no failures.
2. November 2006: three departures in the test conditions, 50–59°F rather than the 158°F maximum operating temperature, no grounding, and a pre-production battery. The report's words are that the test "did not adequately represent the battery installation on the 787" (p. 64, p. 82). Reason it was pushed past: this was an R&D test and the conditions were representative.
3. July 2009: the APSIF test, with the battery inadvertently overcharged, one cell venting and nothing propagating (p. 82). Reason it was pushed past: another confirmation that nothing propagates. The report later notes that the test was not designed to assess propagation and cannot serve as confirmation of the 2006 test (p. 82).
4. September 2009: two venting tests showed electrolyte leakage and the case gained additional sealing (p. 82). Reason it was pushed past: the change touched sealing only, not the propagation judgement.
5. The failure mode list made overcharge the only entry into venting with fire, put internal short circuit under venting without fire, and the whole protection budget therefore went to overcharge monitoring (p. 63). Reason it was pushed past: a list ought to be classified that way, and classification needs no verification.

**The point of escalation**: 26 August 2011, when the type certificate was issued (p. 61). The assessment of 16 September 2009 was the last document that could still have been changed. Once the certificate was out, "single-cell thermal runaway does not propagate" stopped being a reading from a 2006 R&D test and became an established conclusion in the airworthiness file. The certification basis recorded on the certificate is Part 25 plus nine special conditions, checkable one by one. Condition 2 requires prevention of self-sustaining rises in temperature or pressure, and does not say by what test (p. 61, p. 86). Any later change has to go through the change process, that process is triggered by an objection, and an objection must be aimed at an assumption written on paper. This assumption was never written. The escalation had no separate signing ceremony, since issuing a certificate is routine FAA work and making a compliance finding is routine work for a Boeing authorized representative, and the two together fixed an unverified inference in place until an event. Less than eighteen months after the certificate, with the fleet under 52,000 flight hours, two battery venting events occurred within 9 days (p. 10, p. 25).

**Failure chain**: technical layer. An internal short in cell 5 or cell 6, thermal runaway propagating cell by cell inside the case, venting, electrolyte spill, fire (p. 91). Nothing propagated under the 2006 test conditions; the nail penetration repeated at 158°F propagated to every cell (p. 82, footnote 137).

Organizational layer. The key assumption never entered the safety assessment (p. 12). An assumption that is not written has nothing for a reviewer to challenge, and the several independent rounds of review by Boeing authorized representatives and FAA certification engineers found no gap (p. 85). The gap then ran down the document chain: no requirement in the SCD for battery behaviour under single-cell thermal runaway (p. 86), so no thermal runaway test on the qualification list, so nothing of the kind in view when the certification plan was approved. The regulation says to prevent self-sustaining rises in temperature or pressure (p. 86, footnote 147) and does not say what proves it.

Incentive layer. The zero-failure record of industrial cells is the cheapest reliability evidence there is, needing no rig and no time. Verifying the assumption needs 158°F, grounding, final configuration and repetition, which is the most expensive class of evidence available. Someone had to ask for the expensive evidence. The place to ask was the SCD, and the SCD's source was the assumption nobody had written.

All three layers break. The boundary breaks between the organizational and the incentive layer: no document required that test to take place, and the operating record of nothing having happened signed for it instead.

**Minimum counterfactual**: before the EPS safety assessment was finalized in September 2009, write "thermal runaway caused by an internal short in one cell does not propagate to adjacent cells" as an explicit assumption, and hang a verification on it: repeated nail penetration at 158°F on a grounded battery of final configuration. One action, in the week the assessment was finalized. It was doable at the time. 158°F is the battery's own stated maximum operating temperature (p. 82), grounding is the installation on the aircraft (p. 86), and nail penetration was a method GS Yuasa had already used (p. 64). No luck is required. The cost is weeks of rig time and one battery. The output is a test report that can be filed beside the 2006 report, and the difference between the two sets of conditions is itself the basis for deciding whether the design has to change. The report's causal judgement runs the same way: had the assessment included the possibility of propagation, the authorized representatives and FAA engineers would very likely have required additional testing, or a design able to accommodate full-case cascading thermal runaway (p. 84).

**Outcome and sources**: the NTSB's probable cause finding is that an internal short circuit in a single cell of the APU lithium-ion battery initiated thermal runaway that cascaded to adjacent cells, releasing smoke and fire; Boeing had not incorporated into the design a requirement to mitigate the most serious consequences of a single-cell internal short circuit, and the FAA had not identified that deficiency during type design certification (p. 92). The report also states that GS Yuasa did not test the battery under the most severe conditions likely in service, and that the test batteries differed from the final certified design (p. 11). The report was adopted on 21 November 2014. Safety recommendations A-14-113 to A-14-130 go to the FAA, Boeing and GS Yuasa, of which A-14-119 to A-14-123 address the identification, verification and traceability of assumptions, and A-14-129 requires Boeing to revise its safety assessment process for new technology designs (pp. 93–95). Primary source: NTSB, Auxiliary Power Unit Battery Fire, Japan Airlines Boeing 787-8, JA829J, Boston, Massachusetts, January 7, 2013, NTSB/AIR-14/01, adopted November 21, 2014; link at appendix B, av-002.

The industry-level tuition in case 4 has one layer worth separating out. In a certification setting, substituting analysis for measurement takes the form of analysis coverage. A safety assessment covers a failure mode by analysis, meaning models, analogy and prior data, and measurement is carried out only where the analysis shows it to be necessary. Whether it is necessary is itself a judgement about the failure mode, so where understanding is thin the analysis will show measurement to be unnecessary. That is a closed loop referring to itself: a gap in understanding decides the absence of testing, and the absence of testing preserves the gap in understanding. There is one way out, which is to require measurement for failure modes that are new, and the lithium thermal runaway in this case was new, whatever the analysis says. The test for novelty has to be independent of the analyst, which means an industry-level list of failure modes kept current. That is institutionalized memory for a certification system: the industry stops letting one company's safety record vouch for the whole industry's assumptions.

## 20.4 What Shows Up Earliest

Six weak signals, each with the reason that usually pushes it aside.

1. The argument for keeping a defensive line item at the budget meeting starts to be "nothing has ever happened". Reason given: "that is a fact".
2. The actual execution of a drill, a sample check or a review slides from measurement to signing in. Reason given: "the process is being followed".
3. Standby equipment has not been powered up or tested for a long time, while the list says serviceable throughout. Reason given: "the equipment is new". (The pump on Freedom II.)
4. A new starter describes a defensive process as "the way we do things here" and cannot say what it prevents. Reason given: "just follow it".
5. The last record of a defensive action catching a problem is more than two years old. Reason given: "there are fewer problems now".
6. The year counts quoted in a cutting proposal leave out the events the defence caught and nobody totalled. Reason given: "there is no data". (The absence of data is itself criterion 2.)

Put the six signals back against the cases in this chapter. Signal 2, measurement becoming signing in, maps to case 1, four years of idling platform checks. Signal 3, a list saying serviceable while nothing has been verified, maps to case 3, the paper state of the standby pump. Signal 4, a new starter unable to say what the process prevents, maps to case 2, where the count process in a new nurse's hands means nothing more than a form. Signal 5, the last catch older than two years, maps to case 4, a certification system with no catch record for a new failure mode. Signal 6, the data gap, maps to all of them. None of the four cuts in those cases went through a budget meeting, and none of the shrink passed a decision point. That is the fine difference between this chapter and the one before it. The cuts in Chapter 25 have signatures. Most of the shrink in this chapter has none.

Ordering the six signals by cost of discovery gives a reading specific to this chapter. Signals 3 and 5 are cheapest, since they need only the dates in a ledger. Signals 1 and 6 are middling, since they need the budget papers read. Signals 2 and 4 are dearest, since they need site observation and interviews. Start with the cheapest, as usual. Pull the organization's list of defensive equipment and processes, mark against each item the date of its last live test or last catch, and mark in red anything older than two years. The length of the red list is the operating level of this chapter's mechanism. For most organizations doing this inventory for the first time, the number of red items comes as a surprise, and the surprise is itself evidence that criterion 1 holds.

## 20.5 How to Defend Against It

Device 1: keep a ledger for the non-events. A defensive line gets an output ledger: every problem a drill found, every batch a sample check stopped, every error a review corrected, all entered. The ledger has to separate two kinds of empty. Nothing to catch, which is genuine calm, and nothing being caught, which is ritual. The first is evidence that the spending works. The second is evidence that the spending is dead. Mixed together they can never be told apart.

Device 2: live testing of defences. Backups and standby processes are verified for real, on a cycle: the standby pump is started and run, the standby line is actually switched over, the backup staff actually take the post. Chapter 22's principle that redundancy must be rehearsed for real is repeated here because it is the line most often crossed in this chapter. Results go into the ledger and are bound to the claim of availability: an item not verified in the current cycle drops automatically to unverified.

Device 3: a two-year lookback on every cut. Any defensive cut, when approved, is logged with a lookback date two years out, and at the lookback the domain's actual risk indicators are compared against the pre-cut baseline. A lookback recovers no losses. It does give the next cutting meeting one more piece of history to be asked about, and each time the institution remembers, the next defence has to be a paragraph longer.

Device 4: institutionalize the memory. Write the reason a defence exists onto one page headed what this prevents, and keep it with the process, the same instrument as the why document in Chapter 30. A process whose purpose a new starter can read is a process that survives. A line item whose accident the organization still remembers is a line item that cannot be cut. The page's format is fixed: what it prevents, what happened before (an accident ID or an industry case), and the minimum cost of cutting it.

Set the four devices against this chapter's cases. Device 1 on case 1: the platform check ledger splits findings from checks, and four years of zero findings against continuous checks is written evidence of idling. Device 2 on case 3: the standby pump is started quarterly and the run recorded, with serviceable bound to the date of the last test. Device 3 on case 5: the cut to the high-rate acquisition model, the rate reduction, is logged with a lookback date, and the lookback compares deposit retention. Device 4 on case 2: the one page for the count process carries an industry retention case, this report being the material, and new starters read it as part of induction.

The four devices have four ways to fail. The output ledger dies of record inflation, as drills begin manufacturing findings to make the ledger look good and the record detaches from reality. The patch is to grade findings, with real findings and improvement suggestions in separate columns, and to put the ledger itself under audit. Live testing dies of a distorted test environment, as the standby pump passes an unloaded run and fails under working load. The patch is to design test conditions around the worst case, in the sense of Chapter 25's margin. The two-year lookback dies of formality, as the comparison indicator is chosen because the cut does not affect it. The patch is to lock the indicator at the moment the cut is approved, with no substitution afterwards. Institutionalized memory dies by turning into a leaflet: written, distributed, unread. The patch is to bind the page to training assessment and to require a rewrite after every relevant accident, since an accident is the moment that tests whether the page is still accurate. The four failures share one checkup question. Did this device last change a decision? A device that has never changed a decision is decoration.

## 20.6 Boundaries With Neighbouring Mechanisms

Against margin erosion (Chapter 25): eating margin is the cutting action itself, a fall in reserve that can be read off the accounts, while slack is the growth of the willingness to cut, the reason someone believed a cut was affordable. In ma-004 the seawater pipe is margin (Chapter 29), while "nobody since 1998 has felt the need to look at it" is slack. Keep the two ledgers on the same page.

Against normalization of deviance (Chapter 11): normalization files a deviation away, with the words the margin is still sufficient, while slack downgrades a defence, with the words we no longer need it. Both work from the same false evidence, that nothing happened, and they run in opposite directions: one loosens the defence against the abnormal, the other loosens the guard on the normal.

Against horizon mismatch (Chapter 28): the cost of slack is cashed late, and by the time it is cashed the decision-maker has left the building. That is the structural reason it repeats generation after generation, and it is the same time condition as Chapter 28. The bill handover list in Chapter 28 should carry a column headed which defences I cut.

## 20.7 Five Self-Checks

1. Name three defensive items in your organization that have not had an accident in years. When was each last tested, or last known to catch something?
2. At your last budget meeting, what did you approve or cut on the argument that nothing has ever happened?
3. When did your standby system last switch over for real, and was the switch made at peak load?
4. Take one defensive process and ask a new colleague what it prevents. Would you be comfortable with the answer?
5. If the defensive ledger from Device 1 were opened today, how many caught problems do you estimate it would record in its first year? The estimate is itself the answer.
