# Aviation | av

> Five columns on this page: record quality, feedback delay, coupling, the three deadliest mechanisms, and what outsiders most easily mislearn. Case IDs point to Appendix B.

## 1 Record Quality

Aviation keeps the most complete failure record of any domain in the book; all four devices of Chapter 3 are present. What follows is where the primary material sits and how far it can be checked. The retrieval mechanics are in.

United States, NTSB. Every accident carries an identifier, DCA21FA085 for instance. Formal reports in the AAR, AIR and AAB series are published as full-text PDFs, and the CAROL database covers the whole set of accident and incident records. One note from testing the channels: the NTSB's HTML pages carry anti-scraping limits, while the direct PDF links and the data interfaces answer normally. That is what decides the right path for automated collection. Locate the number in the database, then pull the PDF from the direct link.

United Kingdom, AAIB. Formal reports carrying AAR numbers, and general investigation reports, sit in full on gov.uk, in HTML and PDF together, with nothing in the way of collection. The list pages run newest first and cover every published report.

Japan, JTSB. Two tracks, the final report and the 経過報告 (progress report), full text in Japanese, hosted under the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism domain. The www.jtsb.go.jp domain does not resolve; the working entry is the mlit.go.jp path. The progress-report institution deserves attention. On a major accident, instalments come out before the final report exists, and the Haneda collision (av-037) was still at the progress-report stage almost two years after the event.

France BEA, Germany BFU, Italy ANSV, Spain AESA. Full text in French, German, Italian and Spanish PDFs. The depth is there. The language is where the cost lands.

CAAC. Reports and progress bulletins come out inside the ICAO Annex 13 frame, the MU5735 bulletins being one example (av-038). The official channel is usable, and how much of the text is published moves with the tier of the case.

Overall: the world's highest density of Grade A material, and the non-English material is as deep as the English. Below the reporting threshold, voluntary systems (NASA ASRS among them) fill part of the gap, and the skew in a voluntary sample has to be handled the way Chapter 2 handles it.

## 2 Feedback Delay

Aviation shows two extremes of delay at once.

Design and management decisions return their feedback in years. Lengthen a maintenance interval, issue an airworthiness directive, rewrite a training syllabus, and the consequences need years, sometimes hundreds of thousands of flight hours, before they appear. The lubrication-interval extension on Alaska Airlines 261 (av-034) ran from approval to consequence on a scale of years. The 737-9 door plug (av-030) took about eight months from the final-assembly work to the event, and from the standpoint of the design certification system the delay is counted in decades. So the people deciding at the organizational and incentive layers almost always meet the consequences of their own decisions after their own tenure has ended. That is the soil Chapter 28, horizon mismatch, grows in.

Crew decisions return their feedback in seconds. A takeoff, an approach, a go-around: the effect cashes in immediately. Because the delay is so short at this layer, the evidence chain for crew error is the most complete and the most reproducible in the book. An NTSB brief final report, five pages or so, will usually lay out the whole operational chain.

Between the two ends sits a middle band. The maintenance front line runs in weeks. The company safety management system runs in quarters. A reader borrowing aviation practice should fix which band they sit in before borrowing anything. Borrow interval management and the validation has to be planned over years. Borrow the checklist and it can be validated over weeks.

## 3 Coupling

Coupling inside one airframe is pressed down by design to a very low level: redundant systems, fail-safe design, segregation requirements. Seventy years of institutional spending produced that. Hidden coupling in Perrow's sense is controlled at the level of the single aircraft.

Coupling across aircraft is where aviation's real risk sits, and it takes the form of common-cause failure. Three diffusion paths. The same fleet: the worldwide grounding after the 787 battery events (av-002), the grounding of every 737-9 for inspection after the door plug left its frame (av-030). The same maintenance and management system: the interval extension on Alaska 261 applied across the fleet (av-034). The same training and operating culture: the automation dependence on Asiana 214 carried features common to an airline and to a region's training culture (av-031). Isolation instruments exist and are used as institutions, among them grounding orders, airworthiness directives and survey-style fleet inspections. Diffusion is usually cut off inside days.

The conclusion to carry into other industries is not that coupling here is low. It is high coupling already tamed by institutions, and taming costs what the devices of Chapter 3 cost. An industry whose coupling looks low without a matching institutional spend has probably not seen its coupling yet (Chapter 21).

## 4 The Three Deadliest Mechanisms

**Practical drift (Chapter 15).** Aviation carries the densest procedure set in the world, which makes the fork between formal process and actual practice the most instructive one to study. Samples: an air ambulance crew looking down in flight to consult the record book (av-020, the behaviour chain complete); getting out of an R22 with the engine running becoming routine (av-010, where the manufacturer's safety notice had stated the risk years before); a 737 whose takeoff performance was computed for the full runway while the takeoff ran from an intersection, with nobody stepping in (av-028). Every generating condition for practical drift is present here. The rules are written by people who do not fly, the workload is heavy, and a violation almost never carries a consequence.

**Margin erosion (Chapter 25).** Samples: approved extensions of the horizontal stabiliser lubrication and inspection intervals (av-034, where the cut had a document, an approver, and delivered itself flight by flight); a 747 freighter cargo door left unsealed in rain, letting water reach the electronics bay (av-026, no recheck required of the fairing and the seals). Aviation keeps a ledger tradition for margins. The ledger is held by the operator, and regulatory approval is the legal channel through which a margin gets cut.

**Automation and skill decay (Chapter 26).** Samples: the autothrottle inadvertently shut off during Asiana 214's visual approach (av-031); a helicopter cleared to a helipad that landed on a runway instead (av-017, no reconfirmation before final approach). Automation dependence combined with compressed training hours is the failure path this industry has validated most often. The industry's own countermeasures, manual-flying quotas and scenario-based simulator training, are worth taking as a complete set by anyone borrowing from this page.

## 5 What Outsiders Most Easily Mislearn

Mislearning one: taking the checklist for a culture. An aviation checklist works because three supports sit under it. The person signing has the authority to stop the line. Findings are recorded, and the record is not used to assign blame. Missed checks get caught by a data system, FDM, flight data monitoring. Remove the three and the checklist decays into a signature campaign (Chapter 32, compliant and unsafe). Transplanting the form without the system is the commonest cross-industry mis-transplant there is.

Mislearning two: taking one case's lesson for the mechanism. The safety recommendations at the end of a report address specific conditions; lift one out (say, "improve communication") and you have taken the prescription without seeing the patient. The right use is the mechanism field on the case card. Classify first, meaning decide which chapter the case hangs on, then read Section 5 of that chapter for the countermeasures.

Mislearning three: mistaking aviation's transparency for the industry norm. Aviation can publish full reports because a disaster paid for the institution (Chapter 4). Arguing inside your own organization that aviation publishes, so why can't we, points in the right direction. Do not overestimate how easy the publishing is. Behind every page of a public report stands a statutory power to investigate.

## 6 Three Questions from Readers Outside the Field

**What use is an aviation case to me, when my industry has no airworthiness rules?** The use sits in the mechanism, not the regulation. Normalization of deviance (Chapter 11) and margin erosion (Chapter 25) are isomorphic across industries; the regulation is only the parameter.

**Aviation's accident rate is already low, so is there anything left to learn?** The low rate is an institutional output, produced by the four devices of Chapter 3. What you learn is the mechanics of the institution, and copying the clauses is not the same exercise.

**Isn't this too technical, all that airworthiness vocabulary?** The twelve fields of the case card have already flattened the vocabulary into a sequence of actions. The one-sentence event description in field two is written for a reader outside the field, and the technical detail stays in the body text for verification.

The common answer to all three: aviation is the best specimen library of mechanisms in the book because its records are the best (Chapter 3), and for no other reason.

## 10 Reservations

Three limits apply to everything judged on this page. The book's case library samples toward events with rich public records, and the record skew is the subject of Chapter 2. The frequency ordering of mechanisms reflects the distribution inside this library, not the true distribution in the domain. Institutions and technology in the domain keep moving, so this page has a shelf life measured in years, and a reader quoting it should state the year of the quotation. All three limits are the book's general disclaimer, and the threshold check of Chapter 1 applies to the book itself as well.

## 11 How the Three Layers Sound in Aviation

Read aviation cases through the three-layer model of Chapter 9 and one accent stands out. Breakpoints at the organizational and incentive layers rarely appear alone; they almost always arrive together with a missing signal at the technical layer. The reason is the density of the four recording devices (Chapter 3). A technical signal nearly always leaves a trace, and the failure sits in whether the organization translated it, which is breakpoint one in Chapter 9. The typical incentive-layer form is the tension between a business model paid per departure and the redundancy that safety needs (Chapter 17, av-032), and that line is tightest in the regional carrier system. For a reviewer: on the nine-cell walkthrough of an aviation case, the incentive cell most often needs to be traced back to the day the commercial model was signed, which is the longest retrospective reach of any intervention.

## 13 Closing Address

The last word for aviation's governing layer. The four devices are thick enough already. What this domain has left to improve sits at the execution end rather than the recording end: tracking whether safety recommendations actually land (the implementation-rate logic of Chapter 19), and the real effect of training (the manual-flying quota of Chapter 26). Those two are where the next stage of spending pays. The value of these cases does not lie in telling aviation how to work, since aviation is more professional at this than the book is. It lies in letting other industries take away the mechanics: the four devices of Chapter 3, the protection logic of Chapter 19, the freshness regime of Chapter 26. Aviation's position in this book is that of a template rather than a specimen.

Aviation holds the most cases of any domain, 41 in the av series. That reflects where aviation sits in the library: the main ground on which the mechanisms are validated, with all twenty-four mechanisms having at least one aviation sample. A reader who wants to learn the whole mechanism set from a single domain should pick aviation for coverage. The same coverage has a side effect worth naming. It can leave the impression that failure happens only in industries with a formal investigation system. Read Chapter 2 (calibrating for record skew) after this page, or aviation's transparency will be mistaken for the normal state of the world.

## 16 Closing Notes on the Aviation Page

This page is the largest of the domain pages because its public material is the thickest. Formal reports from NTSB, AAIB, JTSB, BEA and BFU form a band of evidence that compares across borders, and one mechanism can be evidenced repeatedly, in different chapters of different reports. One warning goes with the material. Thick material breeds aviation exceptionalism, the belief that this industry prevents failure because of resources no one else has. Aviation's actual distinction is a single thing: the institutional density of mandatory reporting and independent investigation (first among the five precedent domains of Chapter 3). Institutional density can be borrowed by other domains. The thickness of the reports is only its output. When you meet any device on this page, ask first where it snags in another industry. Most of the time the answer is institutional density, and that answer can be changed.
