> Five columns: record quality, feedback delay, coupling, the three deadliest mechanisms, and what outsiders most easily mislearn.

## 1 Record Quality

The worst record in the book, and structurally so: the extreme form of the survival skew described in Chapter 2. First-hand material is mostly the founder's own postmortem (all four st cases here were collected and verified through Failory Cemetery, and all four carry the founder's own words). The sample is self-selected, the attribution self-reported, and the writing sits under emotional and legal constraint at once (non-disclosure agreements, refund clauses). Bankruptcy and liquidation filings give a factual skeleton with no narrative attached. Media retellings do not count as evidence, and the Chapter 19 rule applies to them too. The gap has a shape: the fastest and largest deaths, the cash-burnout type, are the least postmortemed. Read this domain's failure material with the three quality checks of Chapter 2 running the whole way.

## 2 Feedback Delay

The cash-flow clock is measured in months and it is not linear: when financing tightens, every company's runway shortens at the same time (st-004's Xinja attributed its shutdown directly to "an increasingly difficult funding environment"). Product-market fit is measured in quarters, but the growth narrative defers the reading (st-001's Atrium kept expanding while fit had not arrived). The regulatory clock, in crypto and fintech, runs in years and is exogenous (st-002's Koinex, where a freeze on banking channels carried regulatory uncertainty straight into the business). What separates this domain from the others is that the distribution of its feedback delays is set by variables the company does not control.

## 3 Coupling

Coupling between individual companies is low: one death does not directly infect the next. Coupling inside the ecosystem is high. The funding environment, channel policies and platform rules are an upstream shared by the whole industry, the form Chapter 33 describes as dependency concentrated on the VC cycle. For platform startups coupling rises with success. A company dependent on one acquisition channel, one cloud vendor, one ecosystem buys that dependence cheap while it is small, and discovers at scale that it has bought its own jugular (Chapter 33).

## 4 The Three Deadliest Mechanisms

Sunk cost and escalation (Chapter 27): money kept going in after product-market fit had failed to arrive (st-001, where the founder's own sentence is the most honest line of postmortem in this book). Narrative before evidence (Chapter 13): the growth story ran ahead of the product (st-003). Horizon mismatch (Chapter 28): the cost cycle of the acquisition model set against the cycle of the funding environment (st-004). Next in frequency: regulatory lag and capture (st-002's banking channels) and crossing a scale threshold (organisation and product failing on two fronts during expansion).

## 5 What Outsiders Most Easily Mislearn

Wrong lesson one: taking the attribution in a postmortem for the truth. A founder's account passes three filters, self-report bias, confidentiality clauses, and the needs of the story being told. Read a postmortem for the information environment of the time; do not read it for the cause of death. Wrong lesson two: treating a survivor's lesson list as the complete set (Section 2.2: what is missing is the deadliest death, and cash exhaustion, co-founder rupture and a regulatory veto have the lowest rates of appearance in public postmortems). Wrong lesson three: taking one case for a method. "Raise densely, validate quickly" appears in the sample as both success and st-001-style failure, and one case cannot give you the prior probability. The book returns to this point repeatedly. In this domain it is loudest.

## 6 Three Questions from Readers Outside the Field

**Why is the evidence grade in startup cases so low?** Public documents on startup death are thin by nature, and the survival skew of Chapter 4 is heaviest here. Every startup case in this book is marked E and used only to illustrate a mechanism. Marking the grade honestly is the protection the book offers its startup readers.

**Is the method for a product failure the same as for a safety accident?** The analysis of decision points and points of no return applies without change (Chapter 7: shipping, expanding, betting on a new market are all points of no return). narrative before evidence in Chapter 13 is at its most typical in a funding round. The method has no boundary.

**Will studying failure make a founder more afraid to start?** The aim is to make the cost of failure predictable. A founder who knows how each road ends tends to test faster than one who does not. Chapter 40 deals with the failures that should be encouraged. Fear comes from the unknown, and the unknown is what a case library removes.

## 10 Reservations

Three limits apply to everything judged on this page. The book's case library samples toward events with rich public records, and the record skew is the subject of Chapter 2. The frequency ordering of mechanisms reflects the distribution inside this library, not the true distribution in the domain. Institutions and technology in the domain keep moving, so this page has a shelf life measured in years, and a reader quoting it should state the year of the quotation. All three limits are the book's general disclaimer, and the threshold check of Chapter 1 applies to the book itself as well.

## 11 How the Three Layers Sound in Startups

The three-layer accent in startups is that all three layers are young at once and each accelerates the others. The founder's intuition (Chapter 14) becomes a product decision directly, in the technical layer. The product story becomes a funding promise directly (Chapter 13). The funding arrives and becomes an expansion budget directly (Chapter 27). The coupling distance between layers, ten years in a large company, compresses to eighteen months in a startup, and the life cycles of st-001 and st-003 are samples of that compression ratio. For the reviewer: in a startup case the time column of the nine-cell walkthrough comes out shockingly short, and the break in the incentive layer and the break in the technical layer can fall in the same month. A nine-cell table spanning a few months reads like an incident flash. The density of the lesson does not thin because the page is short.

## 13 Closing Address

A last word for founders: a low evidence grade is no reason to discount a case. Because the survival skew is heaviest in this domain, one startup failure honestly marked E is worth more than ten success books put together. The self-check for a startup reader is to run the decision table of Chapter 7 over your next point of no return: shipping, signing the lease, hiring the tenth person. The output of that check is not a stop order. It is an awareness of where the door closes behind you, and a founder who knows what they are doing gets up fastest after a fall. Chapter 40, on the failures worth encouraging, is waiting.

The cases on this page carry the lowest evidence grade in the book, E, and they come from startup graveyards such as Failory Cemetery: a CEO's words quoted, the page assembled by media. A low grade is no reason to leave them out. A grade A report on a startup failure does not exist, because a company that has fallen does not write its own investigation report. This is the survival skew of Chapter 4. The value of an E grade startup case is the first-handness of the CEO's words. Assembled by media they may be, but the tone and the logic survive, and the speaker's frame of understanding is part of the lesson. Treat the E mark as an alarm. Where the evidence is thin, complete it with your own judgement, and the completing is the startup education.

## 14 How the Mechanisms Vary in This Domain

The startup variation of sunk cost and escalation: the three funding deaths in the st volume (Atrium, ScaleFactor, Xinja, with Koinex outside them as an environment type) share one escalation signature, in which the size of the raise becomes the collateral of the promise. Atrium's $75 million and ScaleFactor's $103 million turned into irreversible organisational commitments the moment the money landed: expansion, pricing, headcount. The cost of a strategic turn is amplified by the size of the raise. How to read the variation: the first major pivot after a raise is more hostage to sunk cost than any decision made before it, and the test is the frequency with which "already invested" appears as an argument in the pivot meeting's materials. The external adjudicator of Chapter 27 has a startup counterpart, the board, and the board's independent authority over a pivot is the most valuable governance device in this domain.

The startup variation of narrative before evidence: narrative pressure here comes with a timetable, the funding round, and the moment the narrative closes is predictable, the final draft of the pitch deck. The lessons in the st volume cluster around the speed at which narrative and product evidence come apart. The gap between ScaleFactor's promise of automated bookkeeping and the degree of automation actually in the product was invisible inside the funding narrative. How to read the variation: if the power to define the core metric (what counts as "automated", what counts as an "active user") sits entirely with the side telling the story, the evidence system has already failed. The Chapter 13 device has a startup counterpart: write the definition of the core metric into an annex of the financing documents, so the definition itself becomes a disclosure.

The startup variation of horizon mismatch: Koinex is the template for an environment mismatch, where the ratio between the product cycle (weeks) and the regulatory cycle (months to years) deteriorates sharply and the premise of the business model is pulled out from under it. How to read the variation: list every dependency on regulation and platform policy in the business plan (banking rails, app stores, cloud terms), and assess the single-point exposure and substitutability of each one beforehand. The st volume shows that default optimism about these dependencies is an industry-wide habit. Xinja, which returned its licence, is the reverse case: it identified that the expected value of continuing was negative and exited, the cleanest stop-loss in the book's startup volume.
