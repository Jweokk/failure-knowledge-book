# Public Policy and Administration | po

> Five columns: record quality, feedback delay, coupling, the three deadliest mechanisms, and what outsiders most easily mislearn.

## 1 Record Quality

The public reports of audit institutions are this domain's distinctive asset. The US GAO and the UK NAO track the failure of government projects audit by audit, year by year and period by period, so one project accumulates several reports across several years (HS2 and the VA electronic health record each have multiple records in this book's library). The annual audit work report of China's National Audit Office is submitted to the National People's Congress in the name of the State Council and published as a PDF (po-008). The value takes a different form from other investigations: what is recorded is not a single accident but the year-on-year divergence between plan and execution, a public edition of feedback delay. Royal commissions and commissions of inquiry, the deep reviews of major administrative failure, are scattered across countries. Assessment: strong on tracking, weak on narrative. Read the domain by project, whole series at a time, aligned by year.

## 2 Feedback Delay

The longest in the book. Policy feedback is measured in decades (po-007's rail plan went more than ten years without being delivered), project governance in years (GAO's annual comparison), fiscal execution in years (the annual audit report). The institutional consequence: the decision-maker's term (Chapter 28) is naturally shorter than the cycle of the policy's consequences, and intertemporal debt (maintenance arrears, ageing populations and assets, committed obligations) has no line item in the budget. po-005's restoration arrears at Westminster are one form of it.

## 3 Coupling

Coupling inside a project is low to moderate: it can be phased, a section can be stopped. Coupling between policies is high. The same object is acted on by successive governments, several departments and several layers of governance, and the cost of a scope change is absorbed by whoever comes next (po-007). Cross-domain coupling becomes visible in a crisis. The cost of a public project's failure, service interruption and fiscal loss, is borne by an unspecified public, which makes public failure a political event by construction, and makes the pressure of narrative before evidence heaviest here.

## 4 The Three Deadliest Mechanisms

Sunk cost and escalation (Chapter 27): HS2's two resets and the cancellation of phase 2, with £46.8 billion already spent serving as the argument for continuing (po-004). Goal displacement (Chapter 12): an IT dashboard whose risk ratings diverged from actual risk (po-003), and a conceptual schedule handed to the oversight body (po-002). Diffusion of responsibility (Chapter 18): thirteen years without an agreed plan for the restoration, with authority split (po-005), and scope swinging across governments with nobody answerable for delivery (po-007). Next in frequency: the invisibility of maintenance (po-005), and information attenuation up the hierarchy (po-008's early-collection incentive and its information structure).

## 5 What Outsiders Most Easily Mislearn

Wrong lesson one: reading a single audit report as event news. The value of a GAO or NAO report lies in the series. Five years of reports on one project, read together, are what reveal metric drift and accumulating arrears; the latest report alone is a lab result with no medical history behind it. Wrong lesson two: attributing project failure to "government inefficiency". The attributions in audit reports are structural: governance structure, definitions, incentives. Mechanisms that are isomorphic across the public and private sectors (Chapters 12, 17, 27) simply keep a different set of books in the public domain. Wrong lesson three: underestimating the deterrent value of public audit. Periodic tracking plus published implementation rates for recommendations (Chapter 19) is one of the few workable intertemporal accountability devices the public domain has.

## 6 Three Questions from Readers Outside the Field

**What use are public policy cases to a corporate manager?** Horizon mismatch, diffusion of responsibility and path lock-in are all isomorphic inside large corporate projects. HS2 across several governments and a multi-vendor IT programme running for years are the same failure chain. Read Chapters 24, 27 and 28 together.

**Are audit office and NAO reports worth reading outside the public sector?** Yes. The reports are complete in structure, graded in evidence and numbered in recommendation, which makes them the best models available of what a competent failure report looks like (Chapter 3).

**Accountability here is visible. Where is the organizational learning?** Public accountability is step one. Step two is tracking whether the recommendations land. Of po-002's 18 recommendations, 4 were implemented, and tracking that rate is itself a learning tool (the implementation-rate logic of Chapter 19).

## 11 How the Three Layers Sound in Public Policy

The three-layer accent in public policy is that the break in the incentive layer keeps time with elections and changes of government. po-007's scope changes, po-004's reset and escalation and po-005's shelved plan all beat to a change of government or a budget cycle. Chapter 28's term mismatch reaches its purest form here. A company at least inherits a continuous KPI; a public project's KPI resets to zero when the government changes. For the reviewer: in the nine-cell walkthrough, the incentive-layer cell should record the election year and the decision year. Their overlap is the time seed of the failure, and a project with heavy overlap carries horizon mismatch in its design from the start.

## 13 Closing Address

A last word for policy readers: what these cases ask for is a mechanism designed to outlive a change of government. po-005's statutory deadline, po-007's trail of recorded changes, and po-004's zero-base re-ask are three designs in that family, each immune to an election. The vaccine costs one page of legislation or one clause in a charter. Its absence cost thirteen years of shelving and tens of billions of pounds of overrun. The self-check for a public project reader is to pull out the approval document and find which page the exit clause is on. If it is not page one, the accent in Section 11 is the agenda for your next budget meeting.

The cases on this page come from the NAO and the GAO, the supreme audit institutions of two countries, and their reports are highly standardised: findings numbered one by one, recommendations tracked one by one, evidence itemised. Public audit reports are the most disciplined evidence format among the book's sources, and Chapter 3 sets out their institutional value. One feature is peculiar to this page: what gets audited is usually an institution rather than a company. HS2 is not a company, and the VA EHRM is not a person. An institutional case has to be walked across governments, agencies and budget cycles, so the nine-cell table of Chapter 9 expands into a timeline matrix, one nine-cell per government, with each change of government acting as a line break. Bring a timeline. An institutional case without one does not read.

## 14 How the Mechanisms Vary in This Domain

The public policy variation of horizon mismatch: the big projects of the po volume (HS2, Westminster, Northern Powerhouse Rail) push Chapter 28 to the largest scale in the book. The decision-maker's election cycle runs in years, the project's consequence cycle runs in generations, and the mismatch multiplier is far beyond anything in a company. How to read the variation: does a full-life-cycle cost estimate exist in the approval document (po-001's lesson, 138 systems and no full-life-cycle estimate), and who triggers a reset (po-004's two resets). The device to argue for is a cross-term project contract, with scope, budget band and decision gates written into legislation or a cross-party agreement. Its weak point is politics itself, since the next government need not honour the last one's contract, so a policy project's register should be designed on the assumption that the contract will be torn up, with exit clauses to match.

The public policy variation of sunk cost and escalation: po-006, Sizewell C, shows the distinctive defence of policy escalation. The argument that a new model reduces risk is itself the narrative vehicle for escalation, while the fact that no comparable project has escaped staged overruns is put in a footnote. How to read the variation: the distribution of fact and narrative across the approval document, whether the decisive fact sits in the body or in a footnote, is a fast way to identify policy escalation. The external adjudicator of Chapter 27 becomes parliament and the audit office here. po-008 shows the annual audit acting as a continuous external adjudication of the national ledger, and the rate at which its recommendations are implemented is the core indicator of a system's capacity to correct itself.

The public policy variation of the invisibility of maintenance: po-005, thirteen years without a settled plan for the Palace of Westminster, is the template at political scale. The beneficiaries of the restoration are future users, who have no vote. The people who bear the cost are the current fiscal year, and they hold every veto. Here the invisibility is produced by the political structure itself; no manager has to be careless for it to happen. How to read the variation: a compulsion to report condition, meaning periodic, public and budget-linked condition assessment, is the only visibility device that works in this domain. po-002, with 4 of 18 recommendations implemented, shows that recommendation tracking already has a mature form here, in the GAO's recommendation ledger, and that publishing the implementation rate is itself the device.
