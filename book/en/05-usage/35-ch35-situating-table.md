# Chapter 35 The Situating Table

> What this chapter covers: a self-assessment table with six parameters. Fill it in and it points to the two or three failure mechanisms most likely running in your situation. This is the door that turns the book's twenty-four mechanism chapters into personal kit.
> When to come back to it: before taking over a new project, reviewing a new system, expanding the organization or changing the appraisal scheme; and at the first step of the thirty-minute walkthrough in Chapter 36.
> Reading time: about 35 minutes, including the time to fill in one table.

The door has a reverse meaning too. If you skip this chapter and go straight to a mechanism chapter in Part III because some case pulled you in, come back afterwards and fill the table once. Then ask whether the mechanism that attracted you is the main dish of your situation. Many readers are drawn in by a case's drama. The table's job is to make drama give way to relevance. What your situation needs defended matters more than which story moved you.

Only time can run the final test of relevance. Whether the mechanism your table hit eventually showed symptoms in your organization is a fact you learn six to twelve months later, and that revisit is how the table's accuracy gets calibrated. Calibration does not change the content of the mechanism chapters. It changes the precision of the map from parameters to mechanisms. The map has versions. Your own accumulated calibration data is the custom edition, and the custom edition beats the generic one, because your organization is not an average organization.

Average organization deserves one last pass, since it is what the table is measured against. The twenty-four mechanisms were distilled from cases in dozens of industries. Distillation pulls out the common structure. Common means it applies to the largest number of organizations, and the largest number means exact for none of them. The table is a bridge to your own organization: this book supplies the generic span, your calibration data casts the custom one, and the custom map is the warning system your organization actually needs. Building the custom edition begins with recording calibration data. That is how the chapter's deliverable turns from a table into a system.

## 35.1 Why Six Parameters

Checking twenty-four mechanisms every time is not possible. The table compresses your situation into six parameters, three levels each, and the combination points at a small number of chapters. The cost of that compression is missed judgment.

The table promises two things and nothing else: it puts the most likely mechanisms at the front of the queue, and it excludes the ones that clearly do not apply.

A third promise sits alongside them. The table is a reading route. The two or three hit chapters are required reading at 45 to 60 minutes each, so three chapters cost about three hours and the first deep pass fits in one afternoon. The time budget is therefore computable: half an hour to fill the table, half an hour to screen, three hours of close reading. One working day buys a targeted diagnosis of organizational failure, and it is the best-value day in the book.

There is a cross-chapter link worth naming. The candidate chapters you end with are your starting reading list, and the completion mark is not finishing them. It is a line written in the margin of the table: which level would I now fill differently. How often the levels move is a direct readout of learning. If the levels before and after reading are identical, one of two things is true. Your judgment of the situation was already accurate, or the chapter's criteria did not cut through your own narrative. Chapter 6's exchange test separates the two: hand the chapter to someone else and see whether their levels move.

A reader may already be forming a question the chapter does not raise. Could this be a tool that computes the mapping automatically? Technically yes, since the mapping is a lookup. The paper version has one reason behind it. Filling the table is the thinking. A tool returns an answer in a minute and what it saves is exactly the thinking; fifteen minutes by hand buys one pass of all six parameters through your head. Time saved and judgment trained do not arrive together. Use paper the first time, and the tool once the table is familiar.

What you pay for skipping it is worth counting as well. Your organization runs, the mechanisms run, and you do not know which ones. Chapter 16 already priced not knowing: the cost arrives late. The free version of the table is running the six parameters through your head, five minutes. An organization that will not spend even that has mechanisms running at full speed with nobody at the dashboard.

The table does not promise to be exhaustive, and the practical reason is that mechanisms manufacture each other's conditions. The normalization of Chapter 11 feeds the slack of Chapter 20. The misalignment of Chapter 17 drives the displacement of Chapter 12. A mechanism set behaves like a network rather than a list. What the table gives you is the entry mechanism, the one to check first. From there you follow the neighbours it pulls in, and section 6 of each mechanism chapter is the neighbour list. Entry plus neighbours usually covers the trunk of a situation.

Selection criteria for the six: each must be assessable by you alone, in half an hour, with no external audit. Any parameter you cannot answer with confidence gets the most dangerous level. The table exists to find risk, and an optimistic fill only deceives the person writing it.

Cross-filling is the antidote to optimism. Have a colleague from an unrelated part of the business fill your table, and read the gap between the two versions as your blind spot. The logic matches the newcomer audit in Chapter 33 and the exchange test in Chapter 30. The output is not a verdict on whose version is right. It is a list of differences, and that list is the best available opening for the next conversation between the two of you.

The six are not independent. Parameter 3, incentive, correlates strongly with parameter 4, time: an organization that rewards the short term usually also runs on short time pressure. Parameter 1, coupling, grows together with parameter 5, scale. Correlation means some combinations are naturally more probable than others. High coupling with large scale and systemic misalignment turns up far more often than low coupling with a single point and aligned incentives. That is the real distribution of organizations, and the table is designed to the distribution rather than to orthogonality.

Provenance sets how much weight the table can carry, so it is worth one pass. The parameters went through three rounds. Round one built a candidate list of nineteen: the six in use plus thirteen others, among them team culture, leadership style, industry cycle, technology maturity, staff turnover and outsourcing ratio. Round two cut against three criteria. Self-assessable: the person filling the table must give a level in half an hour without an external audit, which removed turnover and outsourcing ratio, since the accurate numbers sit with HR and procurement and an individual can only guess. Stable mapping to at least three mechanism chapters: this removed industry cycle, whose correspondence to the twenty-four mechanisms is unstable in every direction, with the same point in the cycle producing completely different exposure in different organizations. A determinable boundary between the three levels: this removed team culture and leadership style, which are continuous spectra that every person defines differently, so level judgment would collapse into a personality quiz. Round three back-tested against verified cases, refilling every situation that has a full card in this book and checking whether the mapping pointed at the mechanism that actually ran. Candidates with a poor hit rate went out. Six survived.

The same screening states the table's nature. The six do not cover every failure factor. They are the whole intersection of self-assessable, mappable and determinable. Factors outside the intersection keep operating. They are simply not this table's business.

A twenty-parameter version is the obvious next question, and the answer is a trade between filling cost and judgment quality. Triple the parameters and you triple the time, while correlation means the new parameters add far less information than a linear estimate would suggest. The design target is a first screen in fifteen minutes that yields an executable reading route, and that target sets the table's width. Finer granularity has its place, later in the chain: coarse positioning first, then a detailed check of the criteria in whichever chapters you hit. Granularity gets finer downstream.

## 35.2 The Six Parameters: Definitions and Three Levels

Parameter 1: coupling. How fast and how far a component failure propagates through your system.

* Low: the failure can be isolated physically or by process, and the affected surface can be counted (one production line, one customer).

* Medium: the failure spreads inside your department or along one business chain, and isolation takes a manual action (stopping a queue, switching a data centre).

* High: the failure crosses systems, crosses departments or goes straight to the outside, and the propagation path has no list. Chapter 21's test: nobody can answer "who does this outage affect".

Parameter 2: redundancy reality. How far your backup would survive a real switchover.

* High: redundancy is heterogeneous (different vendors, different principles, different people), and a switchover has been rehearsed under real pressure and succeeded.

* Medium: redundancy exists but shares its source with the primary system (same vendor, same drawings, same crew), or switchover has only been rehearsed on plan.

* Low: redundancy exists on paper and has never been verified, or the critical step was a single point to begin with.

Parameter 3: incentive direction. The angle between the appraisal and reward system and the exposure to risk.

* Aligned: the people who create the risk carry the consequence, and the consequence enters their appraisal.

* Partly misaligned: local metrics pull against overall risk, but a hedging role exists with real authority (risk control, safety supervision).

* Systemically misaligned: each layer's rational choice sums to a loss for the whole, and the person who finds the problem is the person who benefits from it (Chapter 17's test).

Parameter 4: time pressure. The window between a decision and its consequence.

* Short: the window runs in days, and the gain from skipping verification is visible now (a release date, a delivery date, a shift).

* Medium: the window runs in quarters, and compressing the process pays this period while the cost lands inside the year.

* Long: the window runs in years, and the decision-maker's tenure will most likely end before the consequence matures (the soil of Chapter 28).

Parameter 5: scale. The number of people coordinating, the number of nodes, the length of the dependency chain.

* Single point: one team, and every open question can be settled in one meeting.

* Multi-team: cross-team interface documents are needed to state who depends on whom.

* Network: outsourcing, franchising, multiple suppliers, a platform ecosystem, so a layer of dependency sits outside the organizational boundary (the territory of Chapters 24 and 33).

Parameter 6: regulatory density. The frequency, depth and penalty weight of external inspection.

* None or sparse: the outside barely looks at your failures, and the binding constraints are the market and yourself.

* Dense: frequent inspection, item-by-item checking, real penalties.

* Dense but captured: inspection is frequent, but the rules update more slowly than the business, the information source is single, and the penalty sits below the gain from violation (Chapter 31's test).

One trap sits on the regulatory density parameter: a high level does not mean safe. A dense inspection regime is where Compliant and Unsafe (Chapter 32) breaks out most often, because the proof of passing each item takes the place of the question whether the whole is safe. Be honest about your industry when you fill this in. Density on paper does not count.

There is a candidate fourth level for this parameter: the trust inertia that follows capture, where the regulated party has lived alongside the regulator long enough to form a culture in which the rule is the whole of the obligation, and compliance reads as safety. It stays out of the table because the regulated party cannot judge it from inside. An organization at that level usually does not know it, and detecting the level needs an outside view and the multiple information sources of Chapter 31. A reminder for the reader: ten years or more in one industry without a single substantive regulatory challenge may mean you are already at the fourth level.

Every parameter comes with an evidence list. Check the evidence before fixing the level, and let the evidence set the level.

Coupling: the impact list from your last cross-system failure, and whether the affected surface was countable; the age of your dependency documentation; and an oral test. Ask three senior people at random "if this component fails, who is affected" and see whether the answers agree. An organization where they do not agree moves its coupling self-assessment up one level.

Redundancy reality: the record of the last real switchover, with date and result; the vendor overlap between backup and primary; the difference list between the rehearsal script and the production environment. No real switchover in three years caps the level at medium.

Incentive direction: where risk items sit in the current appraisal form and what weight they carry; the last instance where someone paid an appraisal price for blocking a risk; any documented veto by risk control or a safety post against a production decision. If you cannot find a veto instance, treat the parameter as prefilled at systemically misaligned.

Time pressure: this quarter's delivery commitments; the last three decisions to compress verification or review, with the reasons given; an estimate of the consequence cycle, meaning how long a problem typically takes to surface. Write the decision cycle and the consequence cycle side by side and the window's length becomes obvious.

Scale: how many interface documents exist and when they were last updated; the outsourcing and supplier list; how many teams one change has to be announced to. A notification list above ten people gets treated as prefilled at network.

Regulatory density: the number and depth of external inspections over the past year, item-by-item checking or listening to a briefing; the ratio of penalty to gain from violation in the cases you know; instances where a rule update lagged a business change. Three pieces of evidence in hand and the level is clear.

The evidence lists share one property: everything comes from documents and records you already hold, so filling the table starts no new work. Failing to assemble the evidence is itself a finding. Whichever parameter has the thinnest evidence in your organization is the darkest area in your knowledge of it, and a dark area gets filled at the most dangerous level.

At the second region you roll out to, the parameters may already have moved.

## 35.4 Narrowing from Six Candidates to Two or Three

Three filters, run in order.

Filter one: evidence. Ask of each candidate whether your situation holds the specific evidence named by that mechanism's entry criteria. Chapter 15's practical drift, for instance, is tested by "formal process and actual practice have diverged over time". Where you cannot produce one concrete example, the mechanism drops to an observation item and leaves the main list.

Filter two: level (Chapter 9). Which layer can you move? A line engineer can move the technical layer and the organizational layer, so a mechanism triggered mainly at the incentive layer (12, 17, 27, 28) is a finding that has to be reported. Written as a traffic report that travels upstream, it has a route; treated as something to defend against locally, it has none.

Filter three: time window. Consequences landing inside your tenure stay on the main list. Consequences beyond it move to the ledger with a named recipient. This is not avoidance. A risk nobody picks up is handled only once it is written into a handover (Chapter 30).

After the three filters, the last step converts the main list into an action list. Each mechanism on it maps to section 5 of its chapter, How to Prevent It, where the devices are listed one by one: which device, who signs, at what rhythm. The action list format sits in the template of Chapter 38. Table to action list to template, Chapters 35 to 38 form a complete chain from finding to landing. This chapter is the first link; the chain closes in the rehearsal meeting of Chapter 39.

Two filled instances make the use concrete.

Instance one: the payments team of a SaaS company, fourteen people, just signed its second large customer and is about to take on a third. Six parameters. Coupling medium, because a payment failure spreads along the order chain but manual traffic switching can isolate it inside the business chain. Redundancy reality medium, because the standby payment channel comes from the same vendor as the primary and the switchover was rehearsed once, at launch. Incentive direction partly misaligned, because release cadence counts in the team's appraisal while the risk control post holds a real veto over changes that cause financial loss. Time pressure short, because a quarterly revenue commitment makes the gain from compressing the verification cycle visible at once. Scale multi-team, because payments depend on risk control, reconciliation and the merchant interface team. Regulatory density sparse, because the industry has no strong regulator and the binding constraints come from customer contracts. Against the table in 35.3, four rows hit: 16 (multi-team plus reporting that carries appraisal weight), 22 (medium redundancy from the same source), 24 (climbing from multi-team toward network), and the edge of 12, where the evidence that the layer above sees only numbers is thin, so it drops to an observation item. The three filters: evidence keeps 16 and 22 (last quarter a financial-loss report took three weeks to reach the team, which is the concrete example for 16; the standby channel has never switched for real, which is the example for 22), while 24 stays because the action is under way, with a note on when to refill. Level puts all three inside the technical and organizational layers the team lead can move. The time window transfers nothing. Main list: 16, 22, 24. Action list, three items: read the prevention section of the three chapters; schedule one real switchover rehearsal for the standby channel and record the result; put "delay in information reaching us" on the agenda of the next quarterly report as an explicit item.

Instance two: a surgical nursing unit in a hospital, the sheet filled by the head nurse. Coupling high, because any checking error acts directly on the patient and there is nothing to isolate it. Redundancy reality medium, because the second-line duty draws on the same pool as the shift and the roster lists them as each other's backup. Incentive direction partly misaligned, because operating-room turnover counts toward performance while the nursing department holds independent inspection authority over safety checks. Time pressure medium, because complications show up over weeks and months. Scale multi-team, because one operation is run jointly by surgery, anaesthesia and nursing. Regulatory density dense. Three rows hit: 16, 18 (a multi-party signature structure), 32 (dense regulation, and the department's safety meeting agenda is dominated by violation notices). Filters: evidence keeps 18 and 32 (the three-party check runs with the signature as the completion mark and the content check left to individual diligence, so 18 holds; six consecutive months of minutes discuss violation events and not one near miss, so 32 holds). Level demotes 16 to a reporting item, because the cross-department reporting structure is not something a head nurse can move. The time window transfers nothing. Main list: 18, 32.

Set the two instances side by side and one thing shows. Same six parameters, and the software team's main list lands on information and redundancy while the nursing unit's lands on structure and compliance. The difference in combination decides which chapters get read. The table does not hand you the answer. It takes you off random reading.

The table also works as a ninety-minute meeting. Everyone fills their own copy beforehand, fifteen minutes. In the room each parameter is called out with its basis read aloud, about sixty minutes for six parameters. Before people leave, one item only: the two parameters with the widest disagreement become actions, fifteen minutes. The discipline comes from the rehearsal meeting in Chapter 39. Argue with the basis, never with the person.

Of the three filters, the level filter is the one most often skipped, so here is a small worked case. A mid-level engineer at a platform company fills the table and the main list comes back with Chapter 28, horizon mismatch: the platform's exit clauses are missing, the consequence cycle clearly outlasts the tenure of the decision-makers, and the criteria hold one by one. The level filter then asks its question, and the engineer cannot move the exit clauses, which belong to the financing structure and the board. Chapter 28's destination is a report, written in the shape of a finding: the criteria one by one, the evidence each points to, the facts verified, and the action recommended at the decision level, in a form the layer above can read. All of Chapter 16's techniques for the upward channel apply. Copy yourself on one baseline as well: a known structural risk that has been written down counts as doing your duty, and one that has not been written down is complicity. Here the level filter's real function shows. It converts "what I cannot defend against" into "what I can report", which is why the main list only ever holds locks you actually have keys to.

## 35.6 The Six Parameters at Organizational Level

The personal version is complete on its own. The organizational version turns it into a quarterly rolling instrument. Each quarter, every owner of a key project or system fills one sheet, and the sheets aggregate upward to the governance layer, which reads trend rather than single sheets. Is coupling rising or falling? Is redundancy reality improving or degrading? Has incentive direction worsened? A parameter's quarterly trend carries far more than a single reading. A single reading locates; a trend warns.

The second organizational use is the distribution chart: a bar chart for each parameter by level across all projects, counting how many projects sit at high coupling, how many at low redundancy, how many at systemic misalignment. The extreme quadrant, the count of projects where high coupling, low redundancy and systemic misalignment hit together, is the organization's heaviest risk carrier. That carrier list sets the topic priority for the rehearsal meeting in Chapter 39.

The third use is entry positioning for new projects. At approval, the project owner fills one sheet as an attachment, standing beside the exit clauses and the list of irreversible points as the three documents of approval. Entry positioning hangs the warning on the project from day one, instead of waiting for the first anomaly to open the book.

Calibration data only accumulates value in a format. The recommended minimum is one line per record: date, object, six levels, chapters hit, revisit column. The revisit column gets two fields six months after the fill. Whether the object showed symptoms, meaning what happened, or an explicit statement that nothing did. Whether the hit held, where a symptom matching the mechanism of the hit chapter counts as a hit and a mismatch is recorded as a deviation with the mechanism that actually ran. Version control for the map follows from this. The table in 35.3 carries a version number, governance reviews hits and deviations once a quarter, and a row is revised only after three or more deviations accumulate, with the calibration source attached to the revision. The rule has one purpose: to make the table more accurate with use instead of leaving it at factory precision. Three years of calibration data carries a by-product. Your organization's own mechanism bias will show, the same two or three chapters hit repeatedly by similar objects, and that bias is the organization's failure fingerprint. The topic list for the rehearsal meeting in Chapter 39 is drawn straight from it.

Three typical fingerprints recur in high-frequency situations, listed for quick comparison.

Fingerprint A: an internet product in growth. Coupling medium to high, redundancy medium, incentive partly misaligned, time short, scale climbing, regulation sparse. Hits 16, 22, 24 and 33 at high frequency. The theme is expansion turning yesterday's redundancy into today's single point: during a scale climb, the backup that was enough last year does not cover this year's added dependency surface.

Fingerprint B: a heavily regulated operating unit (hospital, nuclear power, aviation maintenance). Coupling high, redundancy medium, incentive partly misaligned, time medium to long, scale multi-team, regulation dense or dense but captured. Hits 18 and 32 at high frequency, adding 31 where the capture evidence is sufficient. The theme is the feeling of compliance taking the place of the safety question, with the fact of passing every inspection read as evidence that the whole is safe.

Fingerprint C: long-cycle infrastructure (buildings, pipe networks, platform foundations). Coupling high, redundancy low to medium, incentive often systemically misaligned, time long, scale network, regulation dense with rules that update slowly. Hits 21, 25, 28 and 29 at high frequency. The theme is invisible maintenance meeting horizon mismatch: the consequence lands in the next person's tenure while the cut happens in this period's budget.

Fingerprints save time. A reader whose situation closely resembles one can read that fingerprint's chapters in the order of their hit frequency, then come back and complete the evidence for all six parameters as a check. A fingerprint is a starting point; evidence is the landing place. Where the two conflict, trust the evidence.

A rarer result is also real: six parameters filled, rows compared, no row hit. An empty result has three explanations, in order of probability. The likeliest is optimistic filling, a variant of error one, and the fix is to go back to the evidence lists with attention on incentive and redundancy, the two places where self-image pulls the level. The next is a genuinely clean situation: low coupling, real redundancy, aligned incentives, a sane tempo, controllable scale, effective regulation. Such combinations exist, mostly in small stable teams, and the table's job is done, with an annual refill. The one that deserves most suspicion is the third: every parameter green and the felt sense uneasy. Return to the line at the head of this chapter, where the numbers look good and the feeling is wrong. Unease usually comes from a dimension that has not been parameterized yet, the state of a key person, a process awkwardness nobody can name. Write it down as a candidate parameter for the next fill. An empty result is information, and the order for handling it: doubt the table first, doubt yourself second, trust the result last.

## 35.7 The Limits of the Table

No table can be exhaustive. Four limits are known and stated here to prevent misuse.

Limit one: the six parameters compress, and compression loses information. Dimensions outside parameters one to six (team culture, leadership style, an external shock) are not in the table. Lost information does not become unimportant; it falls outside this table's remit.

Limit two: three levels is coarse, and the world is continuous. The coarse grain leaves room for subjective judgment at the boundary, and the counter is the basis field. A level with a written basis at least lets a reviewer judge your judgment.

Limit three: the trigger regions overlap, and one combination hitting several rows is normal. Several hits does not mean all of them are running. The table gives candidates, not a diagnosis, and diagnosis goes back to each mechanism chapter and checks its criteria one by one.

Limit four: the table does not replace intuition. It is a tool, and the judgment of the person holding it is the core. Chapter 14's warning about the failure zone applies to the table as well: where the environment is irregular or feedback is delayed, your locating intuition may itself be off, and in that case the table deserves more weight than the intuition does.

The common conclusion from all four: the table is a first screen, not a final review. Its value is compressing twenty-four chapters into two or three, which gives attention a starting point. What lies past those chapters, going back to the chapter, checking the criteria, finding the evidence, is not the table's work. It is yours.

## 35.8 Five Common Filling Errors

Error one: optimistic levels. The sign is an empty basis field, or a basis that states a wish ("redundancy: high, we are planning a remote disaster-recovery site"). The design already carries a counter, since a level with no basis gets the most dangerous setting. What actually works is cross-filling: two fillers' difference in levels is more conspicuous than any reminder.

Error two: filling the target state. The organization is mid-reform and the filler writes the post-reform picture. Keep two sheets apart. A present-state sheet locates mechanisms; a target-state sheet argues the reform; the difference between their levels is exactly what the reform is buying. Mixed into one sheet, every warning misses, because you compared an organization that does not exist yet against the trigger regions.

Error three: filling once. The sheet gets treated as a one-off diagnosis and filed. Mechanisms run dynamically and parameters move: expansion raises scale, layoffs lower redundancy, a new appraisal scheme worsens incentives. Put the table on a rhythm, quarterly routine plus event triggers, where a new appraisal scheme, an organizational expansion or a serious accident reported in your industry each earns a refill. The event-triggered sheet usually carries more information than several quarterly ones, because the event has just changed your situation.

Error four: taking a hit for a diagnosis. Main list of two or three chapters in hand, the reader skips the criteria and starts installing prevention. The table gives candidates. Diagnosis goes back to section 1 of each chapter and checks the entry criteria one by one, keeping what has concrete evidence and demoting what does not. Prevention installed past the criteria is a placebo.

Error five: filling it and not showing it. The sheet goes into a drawer because the levels look bad. The table's value to one person is finding risk, and its value to the organization is trend and distribution. Both depend on being seen. A sheet nobody sees is a sheet not filled: its whole information content has improved the filler's intuition, and Chapter 14 has just said under what conditions intuition fails.

Same table, different roles, different centre of gravity. Four common roles are worth separating.

Line engineer: what you can move is the technical layer and the organizational layer. Incentive direction will most likely come out partly or systemically misaligned, and by the level filter that is not a level you can change; it is material for a report. The engineer's version of the table therefore produces two columns: what can be defended (mechanisms in the technical and organizational layers, with the prevention section attached) and what can be reported (mechanisms in the incentive layer, written as a report with concrete evidence). Keep the columns apart and the table does not turn into a complaint list.

Middle manager: you are the direct operator of four of the six parameters (incentive, time, scale, regulation), and your filling accuracy sets the quality of the organizational roll-up. The error you are most likely to commit is error two, filling the target state, because you are usually the person executing the reform. The counter is to have your direct reports cross-fill your sheet. Their level for incentive direction sits closer to the truth than your own.

Executive and board member: your seat suits two parameters, scale and regulation, whose evidence is on your desk. The other four should come from the middle-management roll-up rather than your own pen. The core output of the executive version is the distribution chart (section 35.6) and the list of extreme-quadrant carriers. A single sheet is of limited use to an executive; the distribution is your view.

External role, audit, investment due diligence or regulation: you are filling in the situation of the subject, and the self-assessment standard is discounted because you do not hold their ledger. Work by evidence availability, dropping a level where evidence is missing. Where you cannot get evidence, assume nothing optimistic, start at medium and mark it "not visible from outside". The greatest value of an external fill is comparison: set your sheet against the subject's own, parameter by parameter, and the widest gap becomes the first item on the due-diligence question list.

The shared discipline for all four roles is one line. Fill the parameters you have evidence for, and cite the other parameters from whoever filled them. The higher the role, the easier it is to fill the line's levels from imagination, and the table distorts upward along the power gradient the way information attenuates upward through the hierarchy (Chapter 16).

A concrete schedule for landing it.

Week one: each core project lead fills a present-state sheet, one cross-fill happens, and the pair produces a main list and a draft action list. The week does not aim at an organizational roll-up; it aims at every sheet having a basis field.

Quarter one: two things inside three months. Turn the "read the chapter" item on the action list into one two-hour collective close reading, with a presenter for each hit chapter and a written answer afterwards from each participant, which level they now fill differently. Run one revisit cycle as well, recording the state of the previous round's objects in the revisit column, even if the first round's conclusion is "no symptoms, hit holds".

Year one: after four quarters, governance should hold four things. A map with a version number. At least three rounds of calibration data. One distribution chart. At least one rehearsal meeting scheduled off the distribution chart (Chapter 39). With all four, the table has turned from a sheet of paper into a system. Missing any one, it is still paper.

The most common break point in the schedule is the second quarter, when the first round's novelty is gone and nobody fills the revisit column. The counter is to write the revisit into the standing agenda of the quarterly meeting, ten minutes, never skipped. Agenda status is the only reliable defence against forgetting, and it shares a base principle with the invisibility of maintenance in Chapter 29: anything without a routine carrier disappears into busyness.

## 35.9 Where the Table Sits in the Book's Toolchain

The table is the first link, and four things hang off it downstream. The thirty-minute walkthrough of Chapter 36 takes the table's output as its input: the first half of the walkthrough list, which three deaths, comes straight off the main list. A walkthrough without the table picks topics from memory, memory picks wherever the last accident happened, and that place is not necessarily where the mechanism is most active. Chapter 37's review questions are chosen by hit mechanism. Running the questions for all twenty-four chapters is unrealistic, so the design questions belonging to the hit chapters get asked first, and the question list carries chapter numbers for selection by number. Chapter 38's postmortem template uses mechanism names as archive tags: every postmortem writes "mechanism: Chapter X" in its conclusion field, archived postmortems aggregate by mechanism, and the aggregate feeds calibration of the 35.3 map, which closes the chain into a loop. Chapter 39's rehearsal topics come from the extreme quadrant of the distribution chart, since the quarterly organizational roll-up of section 35.6 produces the heavy-risk carrier list and the rehearsal schedules off it, one carrier per session. The mark of a full circuit: a real hazard found in a rehearsal, archived through a postmortem, becomes one calibration record in the map. Read the four together and the table's role is clear. It turns "read a thick book" into "read two or three chapters and do three concrete things". The rest of the toolchain each holds one segment, and together they are an organization's mechanism warning and rehearsal system.
