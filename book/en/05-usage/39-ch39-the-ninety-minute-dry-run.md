# Chapter 39 The Ninety-Minute Dry Run

> What this chapter covers: a meeting script for rehearsing failure before the fact. Who chairs, how the opening goes, what gets asked, how it is recorded, how it closes, who follows up; with a one-page agenda.
> When to turn back: when a project is approaching an irreversible point, or a team needs a kickoff or a go-live review. Drop the meeting in and use it.
> Estimated reading time: 35 minutes.

The premortem's method comes from Gary Klein's prospective hindsight: the team assumes the failure has already happened and reasons backwards to the causes. In the experiments, groups told to assume the failure had occurred listed about thirty percent more risk causes than groups doing ordinary planning. The reason is social. Assuming it happened removes the pressure, because nobody has to be the first to say that something bad might happen. Saying that it already did gives everyone permission.

The ninety minutes divide into six blocks, on the time boundaries of the agenda table in Section 39.3. Minutes 0 to 10, opening: the chair reads out the decision and the irreversible point (the result of Chapter 7's decision table), announces the rules, and sets the frame as problems only, no debate, every entry recorded without a name, no rebuttal. Chapter 19's silence rule starts running here. Minutes 10 to 25, independent writing: each member writes causes of death on sticky notes, alone, without discussion and without looking at anyone else's. Independent writing exists to defeat anchoring and conformity. Three entries minimum, more is better. Minutes 25 to 55, reading out: each person reads their deaths aloud, recorded only, no rebuttal, exhausted before any grouping. Minutes 55 to 70, grouping and collision: the individual deaths are hung on candidate mechanisms (Chapter 9's three layers, technical, organizational and incentive), and anything new is added on the spot. Minutes 70 to 85, claiming actions: each selected death gets one defensive action and one detection indicator, with an owner and a check date claimed in the room. Minutes 85 to 90, closing: the chair reads the one-pager, dissent is itemised, the meeting ends.

The division of labour with Chapter 36's thirty-minute process runs like this. The thirty minutes is a personal tool, run by one person before a decision. The premortem is a team tool, run by a group before the irreversible point. Neither substitutes for the other. The thirty minutes covers the blind spots of individual judgement, the premortem covers the blind spots of collective judgement, and the two work best in series: the individual runs the thirty minutes and produces the conclusion page, the conclusion page goes in as input to the premortem, and the premortem's output comes back to update the individual page. The series takes about two hours, and a high-irreversibility decision is worth the two hours.

Three failure forms are known. Formality: the notes get written and filed, and nobody follows the actions. The counter is to put actions into work orders (Chapter 10's work-order conversion). Power performance: the senior people speak first and the rest follow. The counter is strict enforcement of the independent writing block, with nobody allowed to open his mouth for the twenty minutes. Complaint session: the room blames the external environment and the decisions above it. The counter is that the chair follows every external attribution with one question, what can we do inside these conditions, which pulls the attribution back into the range of action.

Three tests decide whether a decision deserves a premortem. All three must pass, or the meeting is devalued by being held too often.

Test one: an irreversible point genuinely exists (Chapter 7's decision table has been passed), and the cost of withdrawal is higher than the cost of the meeting. A reversible decision can use the personal thirty minutes in Chapter 36. A reversible decision does not need a rehearsal.

Test two: at least three of the participants hold information that does not overlap. A room with homogeneous information cannot produce blind spots. The deaths written will be highly repetitive, and a repeated joint list is worth less than one person's list. The organizer judges overlap before the meeting, quickly: three roles, three kinds of experience, or at least one outside view.

Test three: the political temperature of the decision allows bad news to exist. If the project has already been publicly endorsed from the top (Chapter 13's narrative has closed the books), deaths raised at the premortem get translated automatically into "we know, but it is already decided". Fix the temperature first, or split the meeting into the anonymous written version in Section 39.7. Hold it anyway, and what comes out is form one, the credit-claiming meeting, in premortem dress.

What the three tests share is a view of the premortem as a scarce resource. Its output quality is set by conditions that exist before anyone enters the room. Most organizations do not hold too few meetings. They hold too many. Rehearse everything and you have rehearsed nothing. Two to four a year, each with its conditions fully met, beats one a month with the conditions compromised.

One more point belongs to scheduling. Hold the meeting early enough before the irreversible point to leave a remediation window, because defensive actions need execution time. Held right up against the point, actions get claimed and cannot be done, and the premortem degenerates into an exoneration procedure: we warned. The recommended window is two to six weeks before the irreversible point.

## 39.1 Positioning: Before, Not After

The premortem and the postmortem are a pair. The postmortem comes after the outcome; the premortem comes before the irreversible point. The psychology comes from the premortem method Klein proposed: the whole room assumes the project has already failed, then each person writes down why. Once the death is assumed, criticism stops being doom-saying and becomes autopsy. (Klein, 'Performing a Project Premortem', Harvard Business Review, September 2007; reprinted in IEEE Engineering Management Review, 2008; Crossref record checked.)

Ninety minutes comes from a single constraint: the meeting has to fit inside one ordinary meeting, with no extra resources. A premortem that runs past ninety minutes never gets held a second time, which is the same as never holding it. The output of the whole session is one page: a list of three to five modes of death, one defensive action per mode, one verifier and one date per action. That page has the same shape as Chapter 36's conclusion page. The premortem is the conclusion page executed as a meeting.

## 39.2 Preparation (organizer, about 60 minutes, not counted in the ninety)

Three items make up the materials pack: the conclusion page from Chapter 35's positioning table, with two or three candidate deaths already screened (if it was never done, it has to be done before the meeting, since the premortem cannot start from zero); the three most recent same-class entries from the anomaly ledger; the current version of the dependency graph or dependency list. The pack goes out 24 hours ahead, merged with the cooling-off period in Chapter 7.

The chair comes from outside the project under review. Rank is not the qualification. The authority to keep the meeting on script is. The project lead's role is respondent, not chair, and chairing your own premortem makes the defendant the judge.

The room: everyone on the project plus one external reviewer, from a sibling team, a downstream user or risk. Five to ten people. Above ten, split into two sessions.

Six steps carry the script. The detail below is written for the chair, step by step.

**Opening, minutes 0 to 10.** After the three fixed sentences, one more action: put the three pack items on the wall (the conclusion page, the three ledger entries, the dependency graph) and say plainly that deaths have to hang on these three materials, and that anything conjured from thin air will not be taken. That sentence blocks half the emotional entries. The difference between a death at a premortem and a confession in a counselling room is the material anchor.

**Silent writing, minutes 10 to 25.** The chair's only work in this block is the clock and the silence. Two practical details. Sticky notes come in three colours, one for each layer, technical, organizational and incentive, with the meaning declared before anyone writes. Forcing the colour choice is the first layer of attribution training. The notes of the highest-ranking person are collected last, laid face down on the table, and read last, which is a physical defence against anchoring. If someone starts discussing early, the chair points at the wall clock and says nothing. Keeping silence works better than correcting it.

**Round-robin reading, minutes 25 to 55.** The discipline is record only, no rebuttal, and the hard part is the order of reading and writing. Record with the left hand and do not judge with the right: after each item, one question, which layer it belongs to and which mechanism chapter it resembles. An item that cannot answer goes to the edge of the board, into a pending zone, and is not forced onto a chapter. Ten to twenty items in thirty minutes is normal density. Past thirty, someone has been writing sub-items instead of deaths, and they get merged in the room.

**Classification, minutes 55 to 70.** The tagging discipline is chapter number before intuition. Every death gets a specific chapter number. The ones that will not fit are marked off-vocabulary and kept on file, because off-vocabulary deaths are the raw material for revising Chapter 35's mapping table, and they are never thrown away. The ten general questions get sampled in this block, and only against the deaths with votes. Votes come from a quiet tally: after one round of reading, each person puts dot stickers on the three deaths he agrees with most, thirty seconds for the whole room.

**Action claim, minutes 70 to 85.** Every selected death passes three columns on the spot: action, check date, claimant. The trap is the collective claim, the one that says our group will watch it. The chair has to force out a single name, and an action with no name becomes an escalation item there and then. Escalation items carry a recipient and a deadline as well, and an item with two blank columns does not go into the minutes.

**Close, minutes 85 to 90.** The chair reads the one page aloud, then asks one last question: how will this page be verified in three months. The answer goes into the footer, with the review date and the comparison method. An output with no verification plan is, by Chapter 38's standard, a dead document.

Three details on preparing the pack. The first item may reuse an organizational conclusion instead of a newly filled page, as long as the date it was filled is marked; a page older than a year gets refilled before the meeting. The second item is chosen by similar mechanism, not similar project: a first launch with no precedent in this project takes same-mechanism records from other projects, since same class means the same class of death, not the same line of business. The third item is the one that most often fails. Most organizations have no dependency graph sitting ready, and the substitutes go in order of preference: an auto-generated dependency list, an annex to an architecture review, and only then a hand-drawn sketch. A project with none of the three has produced the premortem's finding number one before the meeting started, that its dependencies are invisible, and that finding is written up as one known death in the pack.

One distribution rule. Send conclusions, not the raw library. A thirty-page log gets read by nobody, which makes sending it the same as sending nothing. The conclusion page stays inside three pages, the raw library goes out as a link, and whoever wants depth fetches it.

## 39.3 The Script

| Time | Step | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 0-10 | Opening | The chair reads the script and the rules, and announces the assumption that the project has already failed |
| 10-25 | Independent writing | Everyone silent, each writes "how we died", three entries minimum per person |
| 25-55 | Round-robin | Each person reads his deaths in turn, record the item, not the argument, exhaust first and classify later |
| 55-70 | Classification | Hang individual deaths on candidate mechanisms, new ones added in the room (pointing back to Part Three chapter numbers) |
| 70-85 | Action claim | One defensive action per selected death, claimant and date claimed in the room |
| 85-90 | Close | The chair reads the one-pager, objections itemized, meeting ends |

The opening words are the chair's, and there are three of them, fixed. "This meeting assumes that three months from now the project has failed. We do one thing: name the causes of death." "Everything said is recorded and nothing is rebutted. Rebuttal starts after minute seventy." "At the end of every death I will ask what its earliest visible signal was." The second sentence is the floor the rest of the meeting stands on. The number one cause of a failed premortem is rebuttal arriving early: the first speaker finishes, gets explained away, and everyone behind him closes his mouth.

The independent write is the key component of Klein's method: write before speaking, so that the senior person's account cannot anchor the room. This is Chapter 13's narrative before evidence, in meeting form. The silence rule runs to the end of the block: the chair does not speak, and the highest-ranking person hands in his pen last.

Classification uses the mapping table in Section 35.3 and the question sets in Chapter 37. The chair opens with a sample from the ten general questions, which turns Section 36.2's library lookup into an oral examination, and any answer that hits a question goes into the evidence column of the one page on the spot.

Action claiming follows Chapter 36: one action per death; the action has to be markable as a date on a calendar; the claimant needs the authority to execute or the authority to escalate. A death with no action becomes an escalation item, with the recipient and the deadline written in. Only two things may leave a premortem, an action with a claimant and a report with a recipient.

One worked sheet follows, a teaching composite rather than a real project. The format can be copied.

> **Premortem one-pager** | Subject: warehouse automation, phase two go-live | Irreversible point: 11-20, 08:00, the cutover switch | Chair: L.W., Quality (not a project member)
>
> Death one: practical drift (Chapter 15). The emergency rollback procedure is written in five steps; execution in practice follows the three-step drill version. Defensive action: redo the rollback drill under 5% real traffic. Check date: 11-13. Claimant: K.Z., platform team.
>
> Death two: hidden coupling (Chapter 21). The legacy warehouse system's reconciliation job still connects directly to the new database, and after cutover the reconciliation windows overlap and lock the table. Defensive action: complete the historical integration list with a dependency scan, and delay cutover until it clears. Check date: 11-15. Claimant: D.M., architecture team.
>
> Death three: handover rupture (Chapter 30). Cutover day falls the day after a shift change, and nobody on the duty roster has been through a previous cutover in full. Defensive action: two-person duty on cutover day plus a cutover manual handover page. Check date: 11-18. Claimant: S.H., operations team.
>
> Escalation item: incentives point the wrong way (partly misaligned, the Chapter 17 group). The go-live milestone bonus structure makes the gain from compressing testing immediately visible. Recipient: general manager of the business unit. Deadline: written reply by 11-12.
>
> Footer: review date 12-20, comparison by checking the three deaths one by one against the cutover log (owner L.W.). Objection items, two: see minutes.

What the sheet shows is the interlock between columns. Every death carries a chapter number, every action a date, every escalation item a recipient. The whole page is under three hundred words and takes ten minutes to write, and its entire value is paid out on 20 December, when it is read back: how many of the three deaths hit, how many of the three actions blocked something. That review is the premortem's second meeting.

## 39.4 Records and the Close

Minutes follow the minimum standard in Section 38.4 of Chapter 38, with four columns added for this meeting: the death list with mechanism chapter numbers, the defensive actions, the verifier and date, and the objection items. Objection items are the line between a premortem and an ordinary review. An opinion rejected in the room is recorded with the reason for the rejection, and three months later those entries are the first batch of evidence for the postmortem.

The one-pager goes to everyone within 24 hours of the meeting, copied to both sides' superiors. A premortem that is not copied upward is a tea party: defensive actions need resources and calendar space, and neither arrives by a route that bypasses the level above. The copy also carries a design intent that has to be explained to the people receiving it. A superior who receives the page has exactly two correct moves: assign resources to the actions, or state a position on a particular death and leave a record. Demanding an item-by-item verdict is too much. Expecting silent acceptance is also wrong. The footer keeps one line for a reply, filled with agreement to the actions and the schedule, or a comment on individual items, five words being enough. The reply line is what makes the copy a two-way commitment: the level below discloses, the level above catches. A one-pager whose reply line is blank twice in a row goes straight to governance before the next meeting, reported by the chair. A failed copy is measured, not guessed by feel.

## 39.5 After the Meeting

Follow-up has two items. Defensive actions enter the anomaly ledger with a 30-day review point, because the reason an action did not run is itself a diagnosis: no resources, no authority, or an action that was written as a wish. And the deaths identified at the premortem enter the comparison column of the postmortem template: at project close or at failure, the team checks item by item what was rehearsed, what happened, and why the rehearsal did not stop it. That comparison is the only feedback loop this institution has for improving itself. Skip it and the premortem degrades into a rite.

The three failure forms can be detected from the feel of a single meeting, and the chair can carry three questions.

Question one: in the first five minutes, did the highest-ranking person speak? A word before the silent writing block means form one, the credit-claiming meeting, at better than even odds, because he has already set the frame of the agenda. The fix belongs to the next meeting: the chair talks to him beforehand and asks him to hand in first and speak last.

Question two: what share of the death entries name a person? Past twenty percent carrying specific names, form one has crossed with blame and the meeting has left autopsy logic. The fix is on the spot: the chair restates the recording rule, and entries with names move to oral clarification and stay out of the minutes.

Question three: how many minutes did the claim block take? Under ten, the meeting was form three, going through the motions: thirty minutes reading deaths, ten minutes claiming in a hurry, and the output has had its teeth pulled. The fix is to set the next timetable backwards, locking minute 70 as the moment claiming must start, and cutting the death list to three rather than letting claims run out of time.

One indicator runs across meetings: the hit rate of the death lists, meaning the share of deaths that actually happened at the three-month review. The healthy band is twenty to forty percent. Zero means the premortem is writing safe words. Above half means the problem at this project level is severe enough that no rehearsal was needed to see it, and the meeting should escalate to governance. A team that lands outside the band three sessions running has a problem in the composition of the room, meaning homogeneous information, rather than in the process, and should change the people under test two of Section 39.1 before holding it again.

The psychological boundary deserves stating, so the tool is not overrated. It solves one problem precisely: social pressure that suppresses bad news, which was Klein's core experimental finding, and both the independent writing and the already-dead framing are designed around that suppression. Three classes of problem it does not solve, and the organization needs other instruments for each.

Patch one, knowledge blind spots. A problem nobody in the room can write cannot be rehearsed: if no one knows a coupling exists, it will not appear on the list. Only an outside view and materials can light that area, which is why the three-item pack is not optional. The premortem's value equals the knowledge boundary of the participants plus the boundary of the materials, and meeting technique can approach that ceiling but not break it.

Patch two, incentive-layer deaths. Participants go soft on their own incentive structure, since writing that our appraisal scheme caused the compressed testing is writing the exam question for one's own boss, and incentive entries come out systematically thin. The remedy is the escalation channel the chair keeps open in Section 39.3, plus one direct question during classification: one level up, is this an incentive problem? Asking by name is more reliable than waiting for volunteers.

Patch three, insufficient diversity in the room. Five people from the same role, the same tenure and the same background produce five similar lists, and independence buys nothing. Diversity here means information trajectory, not demographics: someone who worked in another industry, someone transferred in from downstream, someone less than six months in the post, each of them carries a path nobody else in the room knows. The organizer selects on trajectory, and where the room cannot supply it, the external reviewer slot in Section 39.2 fills it.

What the three patches share: the premortem is an amplifier, and what it amplifies is the knowledge and the viewpoints carried into the room. Its output ceiling is fixed before the meeting, by materials and by people. A well-run meeting cannot rescue a compromised preparation.

## 39.8 Where Chairs Come From

Chair supply is the bottleneck on institutionalizing the premortem. Everyone has sat in meetings. People who will make the project lead keep quiet for twenty-five minutes on script have to be grown. Three steps.

Step one, observation. A new chair sits in on two sessions with a counting task: how many times a rebuttal arrived early, how many minutes the claim block used, how many entries carried names. The task is designed to make him see the gap between script and room, and the gap list becomes his first textbook.

Step two, co-chairing. In real meetings the new chair gets an experienced co-chair. The split: the chair holds the process, the co-chair records items and keeps the clock. When the chair slips, letting a rebuttal through or missing the closing question, the co-chair does one thing during the meeting, which is pass a note, and one thing after, a ten-minute review. Chair errors get reviewed within forty-eight hours. Left longer, they become convention.

Step three, rotation and rating. Chairs rotate through the organization on the same logic as Chapter 36's verifier rotation. After each meeting the participants score three dimensions: process held, rebuttals intercepted, claims and close. A chair with stable scores across three rounds enters the organization's chair pool, and the pool stays at five or more. An organization with one good chair loses its premortems the day that person transfers.

The selection advice runs against intuition: the best chairs are often technical leads from other teams, not full-time safety or quality staff. A full-time functionary chairing tends to turn the meeting into an inspection; a peer technical lead keeps it a discussion among peers. Both kinds belong in the pool, at a suggested ratio of two to three.

Venue and equipment need practical specifications, because a small failure here drags down a large block.

Venue: a wall you can stick things to is a requirement, whether a whiteboard, glass, or a large sheet of paper taped up. Death entries have to hang, physically or digitally. Notes lying flat on a table have no public visibility and classification cannot run. Remote sessions use a shared whiteboard, with the rule unchanged: once an item is posted, deletion leaves a trace, struck through rather than removed, which prevents disputes about the record after the meeting.

Materials: three colours of sticky notes, each colour enough for fifteen per person; dot stickers for voting, three per person; a timer at the chair's hand, page turned when it rings. Check the day before. A chair who leaves the room to find sticky notes comes back to a meeting that has changed flavour.

Seating: no head of table, the chair sits at the side. The respondent, meaning the project lead, is placed deliberately furthest from the wall, so the path of reading items and hanging them crosses his field of view. The arrangement reminds the room physically that this meeting is about the project and also about him, and neither of those needs to be handled gently.

Remote sessions add three rules: cameras on, since reading faces is part of the work; whiteboard write access opened to everyone before the meeting, so each person posts his own in the silent block; and stricter timing, because online meetings lose time discipline by default, with the chair announcing the clock once per block.

Three challenges come up, and the chair should have an answer ready for each.

"This death cannot happen, we already have a defence." Do not argue. Record it with a note attached, respondent says a defence exists, and test the evidence for that defence in the classification block, using question two of the ten general questions, the second-to-last confirmation point.

"This meeting is talking the project down." Go back to the second opening sentence, death assumed and nothing rebutted, and add one line: a death not named today will have to be named in three months, with interest.

"I am the project lead, I say how this meeting runs." The chair should be holding an authorisation obtained before the meeting, the chairing authority in Section 39.2, and now uses it. Authority is not debated in the room. The line is: we run on script, and objections go to my superior after the meeting.

## 39.9 Data Flow and the Toolchain

Four destinations carry the premortem's data inside the book's toolchain, listed here so the data does not die in the minutes.

Destination one, defensive actions into the anomaly ledger, as in Section 39.5, with the 30-day review point watched by the steward. Destination two, the chapter-number distribution of the death list into the calibration data of Chapter 35's mapping table: when premortems on one class of situation keep hitting the same two or three chapters, that is the failure fingerprint of the class, and Section 35.6 describes the organizational use. Destination three, off-vocabulary deaths and recurring variants into Chapter 37's supplement channel for the question bank, under rule one, that increments come from postmortems, with the premortem as the second increment source. A death that will not fit the vocabulary, if it is real, is worth a new question, and the question bank's boundary expands with each premortem. Destination four, the three-month hit comparison into the comparison column of Chapter 38's postmortem: the premortem list is the first thing a postmortem cites.

All four destinations sit under one steward, the same one who holds the question bank and the ledger, reconciled once a quarter. Completeness has a simple test: pull a random one-pager from a year ago and try to find, within ten minutes, the execution status of its actions, its review conclusion and its citation in a postmortem. If all three come up, the toolchain is alive. If not, the premortem was a meeting, however lively, and not a system.

## 39.7 Three Variants

Micro, thirty minutes, three to five people. For medium irreversible points, an architecture change or an important release. Compression: silent writing and round-robin reading merge into write and post, post and read; classification is cut, with the micro version tagging mechanisms directly and running no collision; the claim block keeps its full fifteen minutes. Output is half a page: three deaths, three actions, three check dates. The difference from Chapter 36's thirty-minute process is the number of people. There is no case where three to five people each run the personal process and then combine the pages to better effect than a micro session. If they can sit in one room, hold the meeting.

Twin, two ninety-minute sessions a week apart. For mergers, outsourcing switches, platform migrations, situations with a wide dependency surface. The first session runs standard and produces the death list. The week between is the organizer's, filling in materials against that list: dependency scans, supplier financial checks, historical incident searches. The second session is a death hearing, not a repeat: each death passes through the new evidence and is confirmed, upgraded or withdrawn, with a reason required for withdrawal. The logic is investigation. The first session produces hypotheses, the interval collects evidence, the second produces conclusions. A large situation rehearsed in one sitting produces an output that is half true by necessity. Split it in two with one pass for evidence and the quality doubles.

Anonymous written version, one week, asynchronous. For situations with high political temperature or teams that cannot meet across time zones. The organizer sends an anonymous form, three deaths, each tagged to a layer. After the deadline the chair classifies and tags chapter numbers independently, and the output goes out as a premortem report, deaths unsigned, classification reasons stated. What is lost is the collision block, meaning the associations that verbal exchange sparks. The remedy is a second round: after the report goes out, one round of anonymous additions is opened, for entries written after reading other people's deaths, and it usually recovers two or three more. The anonymous version produces less than a same-room version in quality, and far more than a same-room version where nobody dares say the true thing. When the temperature is not ready, anonymity is the only form that collects real deaths.

The three variants share one page format, one follow-up rule with actions into the ledger and a 30-day review, and one hit-rate statistic. Variants upgrade. A micro session whose deaths tag to more than three chapter numbers upgrades to standard; a standard session that cannot check its dependency surface upgrades to twin. The upgrade test is a single number: if more than thirty percent of the last output's deaths could not claim an action, the situation is larger than the meeting format, and the format upgrades.

The 30-day review point has a fifteen-minute micro format of its own. It is not a self-check email. It is three people standing at the ledger for fifteen minutes: was the action executed, checked against the output; if not, was the cause resources, authority, or an action written as a wish, one of the three, written into the ledger; and a wishful action gets rewritten into an executable one, following item two of Chapter 36's cross-check list. The commonest finding at thirty days is the third: actions claimed at the premortem turn out to have been wishes when the time comes to land them. That finding feeds the next claim block, where the chair spends two minutes on historical cases of wishful actions, and the quality of claiming improves visibly. One task remains: the first pass at the hit grade, happened, partly or not, which the three-month formal grading revises later.

## 39.6 The Three Failure Forms

Form one, the credit-claiming meeting. Symptoms: the death list is all "customers too demanding" and "the market changed", and the defensive actions are all "strengthen communication". The disease is a chair who is not independent and rebuttal that arrives early. The fix is a new chair and a reread of the second opening sentence.

Form two, the anxiety meeting. Symptoms: the death list runs without end and not one entry claims an action. The disease is treating the premortem as confession. The fix is the rule that an action which cannot be claimed becomes an escalation item, which forces convergence, and the meeting ends at ninety minutes.

Form three, going through the motions. Symptoms: the one-pager is identical to the last one. The disease is that the premortem has no interface with the ledger or the postmortem, meaning the two items in Section 39.5 were never executed. The fix is to put the hit rate of the previous premortem's deaths on the first page of this meeting.

The premortem is not a one-off rite but a machine that needs maintenance, and its maintenance record is the comparison of successive one-pagers. Without the comparison, the third session slides to form three by necessity.

## Appendix: A One-Page Agenda

```
Premortem · Agenda (90 minutes)
Subject: ____   Irreversible point: ____   Chair: ____ (not a project member)
Sent 24 hours before: positioning table conclusion page / last 3 ledger entries / dependency graph
0-10    Opening: the three script sentences, read aloud
10-25   Silent writing of deaths (at least 3 per person)
25-55   Round-robin reading, record only, no rebuttal
55-70   Tag mechanisms (Section 35.3 table) + sample from the ten general questions
70-85   Action claim (one per death: action / check date / claimant)
85-90   Close: read the one-pager; itemise objections
24 hours after: one-pager to all staff and both superiors; actions into the ledger
```

Voting and priority need their own note, because this step decides the quality of the one page. The default rule is three dot stickers per person, voted on the deaths each person agrees with most, all three allowed on one death. After the ranking the chair does one thing: check the distribution across layers. If the top three all sit in one layer, usually the technical layer, because technical deaths are the easiest to write and the safest to name, he forces one from each of the other two layers into the top three. This is a targeted correction of the room's comfort zone, since organizational and incentive deaths are hard to write and will be absent without force. The two forced nominations are marked on the page as the chair's nomination and carry the same weight as the voted entries. The list stays at three to five entries, and beyond five the merging is done by action, since one action that defends several deaths is written once. Chapter 36 gave the principle: three actions equal zero actions, and five actions equal two and a half.

Hit-rate counting is specified down to the operation. On the review date, the one in the footer, the review owner pulls the actual cutover or close-out records and grades each death on the list in three bands: happened, meaning the process described actually appeared; partly happened, meaning a variant of the mechanism appeared with different details; did not happen. The bands are counted separately and the hit rate counts only happened. The grades go into the annotation column of the one page, which is not rewritten, only annotated in a different pen colour, and the annotated page is filed into the ledger. In the annual tally two numbers stand side by side: the organization-wide hit rate, and the count of happened entries that an action blocked. The first measures the premortem's eye and the second measures the defence's execution, and reading them separately is what keeps either from contaminating the other.

An annual calendar for the organization, as a model. Quarterly routine: one standard session, its topic taken from the extreme-quadrant carriers on Chapter 35's parameter distribution chart (Section 35.6), plus however many micro sessions the quarter produces, decided by each team with the organization counting pages only. The quarterly standard session sits in the second week of the quarter's first month, clear of quarter-end delivery pressure, with time to prepare the pack.

Event-triggered: after a peer's major accident bulletin, add a session for projects carrying a similar mechanism on site, with the third pack item replaced by the bulletin itself. The value of the trigger is a psychological window. An external accident makes bad news fully legitimate for a while, and deaths that cannot be said in ordinary weeks can be said in that window. The window expires: not held within two weeks of the bulletin, and the temperature falls back.

Milestone-fixed: one month before go-live, one month before a closing, one month before an outsourcing switch, three points written into the project template as an institution, by adding one line to the initiation checklist, premortem scheduled. The point of the template is to move the meeting from remembered to unmissable. Institutional memory does not run on people's memory.

Annual summary: hit rates across all pages, chair pool ratings, distribution of variants used, one page to governance. The most important line in that report is the absence count, listing the large irreversible points that have no premortem record. The absence list needs reading more than the hit list does, since the risk of a session that never happened is uncovered in full.

The calendar's total cost can be counted. Four standard sessions a year at six hours each including preparation, plus micro sessions and triggered ones, comes to about eighty person-hours a year for an organization of a hundred people. Against the organizational cost of one real accident, which is the order-of-magnitude table in Chapter 1, the arithmetic does not need doing a second time.

## 39.10 The Close

The ninety minutes, accounted for at the end: the premortem spends one meeting's time to buy the chance of converting "we never thought of it" into "we thought of it and did not stop it", before the irreversible point arrives. The gap between those two states is an order of magnitude, because the first only becomes visible after the point is passed, and the second still has an action with a name on it. The book's tools close the loop at this chapter: Chapter 35 positioning, which says what to defend against; Chapter 36 the personal walkthrough, which says how one person defends; Chapter 37 the question pass, which measures the cognitive stock; Chapter 38 the postmortem, which books the tuition already paid; this chapter's premortem, which saves the tuition not yet paid. The five share one discipline: translate vague unease into statements with sources and actions with dates. The premortem is the only meeting among the five, and the only one that needs other people to cooperate, which is why it is the easiest to criticize as a fuss and why it has the lowest cost per session and the highest output per session of the set. The reason to hold one never needed more evidence, since Chapter 7's list of irreversible points is the whole of it: every real irreversible point added to that list deserves one more page.

Three points on using the agenda. The agenda goes out with the pack 24 hours ahead, and participants arrive holding a filled conclusion page from the positioning table, because a participant who skipped the personal thirty minutes will spend the first ten minutes of silent writing trying to recall project details. The time boundaries in the agenda are hard: the chair's timer answers to the agenda and not to the mood, an overrunning block cuts content in the room, cutting items and keeping claims, and no block is extended. The post-meeting action on the last line, the one-pager out within 24 hours, is done by the chair personally, since a delegated send quietly shortens the copy list.

One suggestion for the first session: start with a small and real micro version, on a medium irreversible point a team actually faces, and ten minutes after the meeting have everyone score it on three dimensions, freedom to speak, quality of entries, hardness of claims, then fix the low scores before the next one. The first meeting aims at walking the full process, not at meeting the standard. An organization that has walked it once faces half the resistance at the second session, because the doubt in the room has changed from what use is this meeting to what about the one we failed to stop last time. The second doubt is the healthy one: it means the output has entered the mental ledger.

Five objections follow, which the sponsor will meet in full, usually the person who read this book and wants to bring the process in.

"The project team knows its own risks best, so an outside chair is formalism." The team knows the part of the risk that has already been discussed. The premortem wants the part that has not been discussed, and independent writing and an outside chair are both doors onto that part.

"There is not that much to rehearse, our project is small." Small projects take the micro version, thirty minutes. The test is withdrawal cost, and plenty of small projects carry a withdrawal cost that has nothing to do with size.

"We can write it down, but nobody will act, the actions never land." A death that cannot claim an action becomes an escalation item, and escalation items have recipients and deadlines. If escalation also draws no response, that fact deserves escalation on its own, by Chapter 19's test, and the premortem has merely measured a deafness the organization already had.

"Meeting time costs too much." A wrong comparison will always look expensive. The comparison is the historical accident cost of the same class of irreversible point, and Section 38.7's organizational profile holds the numbers ready.

"A premortem makes people nervous." Look at which kind does: the hazard sealed in the corridor after the meeting, or the hazard written on a page with a claimant's name. Nerves come from unease that has nowhere to go, and the page is where it goes.

One ordering suggestion for the sponsor, after the five answers. The first step in promoting premortems has always been a team willing to try and one real decision, not a policy document. A policy brought to a meeting with no sample behind it draws an objection list much longer than this chapter's. With one fully walked micro sample in hand, a one-pager, three deaths, three actions and a review conclusion thirty days later, the policy discussion changes from whether to hold these meetings to how to hold them well, and once the question changes most of the resistance goes with it. The book's five usage chapters follow the same order. The positioning table and the thirty-minute process run without approval. The question pass borrows an existing review meeting. The postmortem rewrite starts with the next postmortem and costs no approval. The premortem comes last, because it needs people and authority. Moving from tools that need no approval to tools that need approval is itself a ladder of rising resistance, climbed in order, and every step of that ladder has a section of this chapter as a handrail. An organization that has climbed all five no longer holds Part Three's twenty-four chapters as a book. It has its own ledger, its own card library, its own hit rate, and an external book has grown into an internal system.

## The Five Self-Checks

1. Of the three items in the premortem pack for your nearest irreversible decision, how many do you hold right now?
2. If that meeting were held tomorrow, who chairs it, and would he dare make the project lead keep quiet for twenty-five minutes?
3. At the last review meeting you attended, were the objections recorded or explained away?
4. The last premortem one-pager: did it go to both sides' superiors?
5. If you have organized premortems, where is the comparison table of successive one-pagers? With no comparison, which session will you start backfilling from?
