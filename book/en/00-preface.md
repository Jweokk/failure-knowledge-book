# Preface: The Reader's Contract

## What this book is

Everything this book asks of a working manual on organizational failure fits in one sentence: after reading it, you can say, before you decide, which ways of dying you most resemble.

Three things follow from that sentence. Failure scattered across hundreds of pages of investigation reports becomes numbered case cards, twelve fields each, with the field definitions in Chapter 5. The cases are gathered into twenty-four mechanisms, one chapter each, with trigger criteria, generating conditions, weak-signal lists and countermeasures. Then comes a method of use: a situation table, a hundred-question review checklist, and a script for a ninety-minute rehearsal.

Every fact in the book comes from a primary document: investigation reports, court judgments, regulatory penalties, recall notices, audit reports, financial statements and prospectuses, and the parties' own published postmortems. Each case carries a full citation and link at the end, and the numbering corresponds one to one with the master case table in Appendix B. The book conducts no new investigations. Investigation is a separate craft, practiced for decades by other people, among them the writers at the US National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB). What this book does is circulation: pulling the reusable parts out of those documents, putting an index on them, and arranging for them to come to mind at the moment a decision is made.

## The reader's contract

Four commitments, each of which every chapter has to honor. A chapter that fails one is a defective copy.

1. Three lines at the start of each chapter: what it covers, when to come back to it, how long it takes to read.
2. Each chapter ends with five self-checks rather than a summary. All five ask you to answer about work you currently hold. If you cannot answer them, the mechanism is running in you now, and you are not reading about someone else.
3. Cases stop at the decision point. Judge first, then turn the page. One marker throughout:

   **Stop point: judge first, then turn the page.**

   1. What information is in front of the people involved at this moment?
   2. If you were them, do you sign?
   3. What do you bet happens in the next three months?

   **Outcome.**

   The stop has a purpose. Postmortems are written after the result is known, and a reader of a postmortem holds the answer while hunting for the process, so everything looks obvious in hindsight. The stop withholds the answer, forces a choice where the information is incomplete, and then sets your choice beside theirs. Where your choice went wrong and where theirs went wrong are two separate accounts.
4. No moral instruction. The book writes structure, not "should". Where a structural problem appeared, which signal came first, what change would alter the incentive or the information flow: that is what gets written. Whether the people involved were stupid or arrogant gets no verdict, because such verdicts cannot be verified and they get in the way of seeing structure.

## No assumptions about who you are

No industry, no rank. An aviation maintenance release case and a hospital disinfection case sit in the same chapter because the mechanism is the same, and when the mechanism is the same you can find the isomorphic instance in your own industry. Every term is explained the first time it appears, all terms are registered in the glossary in Chinese and English side by side, and no term drifts between chapters.

If a case detail is opaque, skip the detail and read the structure. Every case carries a decision point and a minimum counterfactual, and neither depends on industry knowledge.

## Evidence grades

Each case carries a grade from A to E. A is the full report of an independent investigation body. B is an official regulator bulletin or communiqué. C is a judgment or penalty document. D is the parties' own published postmortem. E is press coverage, usable as a lead only, never as a source of fact. A case's minimum counterfactual and escalation point can be supported only by A-grade material and by part of B and C; cases below that threshold enter the appendix matrix and stay out of the body. The grades are defined and their use governed in Chapter 10 and Appendix C.

One rule belongs at the start: every number in the body of this book needs a four-part provenance, meaning who counted it, when, on what sample, under what definition. A number that cannot supply all four gets written as mechanism description instead. Most paragraphs you read without a number are there because the discipline held.

## What this book does not cover

Three things are out of scope. Psychological consolation after failure, of which the market has plenty. Blame and compensation, which belong to lawyers. Prediction of which company will fold: the book classifies ways of dying, and the divination is left out.

One class of failure is deliberately confined to Part 6: exploratory failure. Without it there are no new drugs, no new routes, no new technology, and handling it together with preventable operational failure would amount to teaching people to refuse every attempt. Part 6 gives the test that separates the two.

## One reading route

If you are short of time, read three chapters: Chapter 2, on how your sample of cases was filtered; Chapter 35, the situation table; Chapter 37, the review question list. If you have a project to review, add the two mechanism chapters that match your situation. If you write postmortems, read Chapters 5 and 38. Treat the rest as tools to consult.

Chapters stand on their own, and you can start anywhere. The one requirement: when you reach a stop point, actually stop, answer the three questions, then read the outcome. A book read with the stops skipped is half a book.

## Three shortest paths

Three shortest paths, restated here to close the preface. Scenario one, your organization has just had an incident: go to Chapter 38 for the postmortem template, fill it in, then follow the mechanism chapters the postmortem raises back into Part 3 for close reading. Scenario two, you face a large decision: fill the table in Chapter 35, run the walkthrough in Chapter 36, rehearse in Chapter 39, three steps inside one day. Scenario three, you want to learn the subject properly: read straight from Chapter 1, write your answers at each stop-point card from Chapter 5 onward, and by the end of Part 5 install the five instruments in your own organization. All three routes end in the same place, the ledger. The ledger that remains after you finish the book is what the book actually delivers.

And a note for writers who cite this book: cite the number and the source ("av-034, NTSB AAR-02/01"). A retelling that drops the number loses checkability after two generations of citation, and checkability is the whole floor that separates this book from a collection of anecdotes.

May these numbers outlive the book in your hands.
