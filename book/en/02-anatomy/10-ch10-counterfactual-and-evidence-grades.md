## 10.1 The discipline of the minimum counterfactual

Definition, expanding field 11 of Chapter 5: the one action whose change would have avoided the event or greatly reduced its consequences.

One word in that definition needs slow reading: greatly. A counterfactual does not have to erase every consequence. Turning 110 deaths into 110 injuries is a qualified counterfactual. What it must satisfy is proportionality: the scale of the intervention has to match the scale of the consequence. Set the counterfactual for a 97-death accident at a reminder issued in a pre-shift meeting, and the reminder cannot stop a consequence of that size. The test for proportionality is magnitude coverage: does the intervention carry enough leverage to move this consequence? A counterfactual without that leverage is a toy. The way to identify a toy is to run it for real and ask whether the consequence would actually have shrunk to the magnitude claimed.

The least-cost rehearsal is a variant of that exercise. Assume the counterfactual action is executed once, and answer three questions: what does it cost, who objects, and does the process allow it. The three answers score the counterfactual's realism. A low-realism counterfactual can still be qualified, but it is only theoretically qualified. Theoretical counterfactuals mark the position; realistic ones can be executed. Keep both in the working draft, and put only the one that passes realism into the signature line.

Reduction is measured by what can be verified. State the magnitude: half the consequence, or nine tenths of it. Declaring the magnitude gives the assessment a scale. An unverifiable claim that the consequences would have been much better does not count.

Three disciplines follow, and all three have to pass. Write one counterfactual only. Test it for dependence on luck. Test it for overreach.

Why only one, argued from the author's side rather than the reader's (Chapter 5 made the reader's argument): the impulse to write several comes from a fear of omission, the worry that a better candidate is being left out. The working draft answers that fear. Put every candidate in the draft, attach the draft behind the card, publish nothing from it. The card carries one conclusion, and every candidate leaves a trace. Separating draft from conclusion is routine in academic review, and postmortem writing can borrow it as is. The serious form of the discipline: two counterfactuals sitting side by side on the table mean the backcast is not finished. Compare their intervention positions and their evidence strength, choose one, and move the loser into the draft. A reference book owes its reader exactly one conclusion.

There is a game-theoretic reason as well. When several counterfactuals stand side by side, the reader takes the cheapest as the lesson, and the cheapest is often not the most effective. The choice of lesson slides from the author to the reader's laziness, and the result is that one case yields different lessons in different hands. Randomised lessons amount to no lesson at all. A single-counterfactual discipline returns the choice to the author, whose comparison sits in the draft while one conclusion is delivered. The two layers, delivery and draft, get rigour and readability at once.

## 10.2 The five-step backward method

**Step one: reverse the failure chain.** Start from the consequence and list the nodes backwards: the consequence, the point of no return (Chapter 7), the last decision point, the earlier structural conditions. Go down to the organizational and incentive layers; the nine-cell table of Chapter 9 can be used directly for the sorting. Every node carries a date and an actor. "Management was lax" is not a node. "The 14 March review meeting put this item at position 7 on the agenda and spent 4 minutes on it" is.

Timeline software or paper will do, and the tool is not what matters. What matters is that each node is written on a card. One node, one card. A backcast is trial and error, and a wrong order gets redone; carding drives the cost of re-sorting to near zero. The physical act (writing the cards, laying out the order, drawing the lines) looks crude and beats most thinking software. The book draws one practical conclusion from this: do the postmortem and the backcast on paper.

A team version is worth adding. Tape the node cards to a wall and let the whole group sort them. The sorting disagreements mark where the group's understanding diverges, so mark the disputed cards in a different colour. The colour wall is the advanced form of the review meeting: the meeting stops being a report-reading session and becomes a sorting session, and its output is a failure chain calibrated across the group. A shared organizational picture of a failure survives handover better than any individual's picture (Chapter 30).

That picture also needs a documentary exit. Photograph the sorted wall and archive the photograph alongside a digital version of the final order, both into the knowledge base and the knowledge pack (Chapter 30). The archived wall is the permanent draft of that failure chain. A newcomer rebuilding the chain three years later compares against it, and the deviation becomes measurable. The trail of deviations is the record of how the organization's understanding evolved.

The starting level deserves its own warning. Choose which consequence to backcast from: the death, the injury, or the near miss. A different start gives a different chain. A major consequence gives a longer chain; a near-miss chain stops at the defence gate. Choose by the purpose of the exercise: to study defences, start from a near miss; to study penetration, start from an accident. Purpose comes before backcast, and this is the first appearance of Chapter 35's positioning table inside the method.

One more node criterion: a node has to be an action, not a state. A meeting being held is an action; an item's position on the agenda is an action. Lax management is a state, and so is a poor culture. Actions can be swapped, and states cannot be swapped directly, since nobody can swap in a different culture. States change through the actions that generate them, so the work is to find the decision that produced the state. States are regular guests on the incentive layer of the nine-cell table, and writing down which action generated a given state completes the reduction from state back to action.

**Step two: test each node.** For every node ask whether a different action there would have changed the consequence. Mark the ones that would as effective, the rest as ineffective. Use only what was visible at the time (the discipline of Chapter 6). For an action to count as a qualified intervention point, its necessity has to hold inside the actor's own information environment.

The information environment itself is a boundary case worth naming. Sometimes the actor could not see something because the system did not display it. In that situation the qualified intervention point moves up a layer, to the information system: making the information visible is the counterfactual, and making the actor cleverer is not. The logic matches the nine-cell table, where redesigning an information environment is an organizational-layer intervention. Both levels can hold a qualified counterfactual at once, each labelled with its layer, following Chapter 9's labelling discipline.

**Step three: strike the luck types.** Any candidate that depends on coincidence goes out. "If the inspector had looked once more that day." "If the person on site had happened to be a senior engineer." The identifying feature of a luck-type counterfactual is that the action it demands has no position in the system. Whether it happens depends on staffing and on coincidence.

**Step four: strike the overreach types.** A candidate that requires the actor to break procedure, defy a superior, or exercise authority he did not hold gets removed, or moved up a layer. "The shop-floor operator should have refused to release the batch" is an overreach counterfactual unless the procedure actually grants him an independent veto. Where the veto does not exist, the qualified intervention point sits in the authorisation design, and the node moves up to the meeting that wrote the procedure. Overreach is the most common defective item in postmortems, and its function is to dress a structural problem as a courage problem.

The overreach check needs a companion step. A node that has been moved up must go through the luck test again. If the repair at the authorisation layer depends on an enlightened leader, on some executive happening to care, the candidate is a luck type once more. Luck does not discriminate by layer, and it can enter a candidate at any layer. The mnemonic is one action, one question: does this action have a position in the system? If it does, carry on; if it does not, move up or strike it.

**Step five: take the latest one and sign it.** Among the qualified candidates that remain, take the one latest in time and write its three elements: date, location (whose ground it sits on), action. The signature format reads: "Minimum counterfactual: on (date), on the (carrier), (actor) performs (action)." If the three elements cannot be filled in, the backcast is not finished, and the answer is to go back to step one.

The signature has a quantitative use. Collect a year's qualified counterfactuals and classify each by layer, position and action type. The reading is a distribution of the organization's improvement opportunities: which layer produces the most counterfactuals. That distribution tells next year's improvement money where to go, since the layer with the most counterfactuals is the layer the budget belongs to. This path from tally to budget is the interface between the chapter's method and the budget template of Chapter 39.

It has an organizational use too: conversion into a work order. A qualified counterfactual generates a work-order draft, with the fields mapping one to one, date to deadline, location to owner, action to task. Conversion is what turns a lesson into a standing to-do item. A counterfactual that is not converted dies when the review meeting ends. The trigger for conversion is a pass at review, and the review asks the five-step questions, so passing the five steps makes the work order legitimate on its own.

## 10.3 Three disqualifying counterfactuals

| Type | Example | Defect |
|---|---|---|
| All-purpose | "strengthen management, improve the system, raise awareness" | No action, no actor, no date; cannot be executed and cannot be refuted |
| Luck | "if the inspector had happened to look one more time" | The action demanded has no position in the system |
| Overreach | "the operator should have held out against the pressure and refused release" | Requires authority the person did not hold; the qualified intervention point sits in the authorisation design |

All-purpose is the most common. The test is to compress the sentence into one verb and one object. If it will not compress, it fails.

A hybrid form needs recording: the disguised specific. "Management should have recognised the fragility of the supply chain earlier" looks as though it carries an action and an actor. Take it apart. Management is a collective noun, so there is no named actor; "recognised earlier" is a state, so there is no action. A disguised specific is more dangerous than an obvious void because it passes the two-second intuition check. Only the five steps, run item by item, bring it back into view. The mnemonic for the disguised version: name a person, land a verb, give a date; missing any one of the three sends it back.

## 10.4 Evidence grades A to E

Every case in the book carries one of five grades, and the grade decides what that case is entitled to support.

**Grade A: the full report of an independent investigating body.** Independent of the party investigated and of the prosecuting function, published in full, with a factual section and an analytical section. Supports the decision sequence, the options ruled out, and the minimum counterfactual. This is the grade of the cases in the main text.

**Grade B: official notices, communiqués and regulatory announcements**. Authoritative, without a complete evidence chain. Supports the magnitude of the consequence and conclusive findings. Does not support the detail of a decision point. B-grade cases go into the appendix matrix; a B case that enters the main text must be labelled "reconstructed". A process report, such as the JTSB's 経過報告, is graded A− to match Appendix C.

**Grade C: judgments, administrative penalty decisions, arbitration awards.** The facts have gone through adversarial testing, but the selection of facts serves the legal issue. Supports amounts, responsible parties, and the timeline of acts. Does not support technical processes that never entered the dispute.

One directional hint for using C-grade material: prefer the findings that went against your own side. A fact confirmed by the court against the party that contested it has been tested hardest, so an adverse finding is the harder finding. When a postmortem cites C-grade documents, leading with the facts the party admitted is a technique for raising credibility.

**Grade D: the party's own public postmortem** (software industry postmortems, company statements, participants' memoirs). First-hand detail, inherently self-interested. Supports the shape of an organization's internal process. Supports a finding of fact only after cross-verification against A, B and C material.

Cross-verification of D-grade material has an efficiency trick: an order of work. Verify first the details that appear nowhere else, since only the unique ones need testing. Where the material overlaps a public report, the report has already vouched for that part, and re-verifying it wastes effort. Verify the unique, skip the overlap. This order cuts the cross-verification workload by 70%.

**Grade E: media reporting.** Lead generation only. Use it to find a case, then go and find A to D material on that case. The main text may never rest on E-grade material alone.

A search tip belongs here as well: media reporting has a time structure. The incident report is fast and wrong often. The investigative piece is slow and corrects more. The anniversary retrospective is slowest and adds the long view. Reading all three layers in sequence is the complete move in E-grade lead mining; stop at the first layer and the direction you take is often the wrong one. The reading list for those layers sits in's search manual, which names the deep outlets for each domain and the search strings for anniversary pieces.

Two rules govern movement between grades. Combination upgrade: a core fact corroborated by B and C together can support a main-text case labelled "reconstructed", while a single B-grade document cannot. Downgrade: a dead link with no archive drops the case to pending verification, and a quoted passage that does not match the original is removed outright. the link check and the number-to-source check carry these out.

Corroboration has one further detail: what gets corroborated is the fact, not the conclusion. Two B-grade documents recording the same fact identically establish corroboration. Two documents agreeing on a conclusion while describing the facts differently do not, because agreement on a conclusion can be a loop of retelling. This is Chapter 2's circulation skew in its evidentiary form. A cites B, B cites C, and three sources turn out to be one. The test that breaks the loop is to find the earliest source: is the first of the three an independent interview, or a retelling of the others? The mnemonic: trace it back, find the earliest, and ask whether that source saw the thing itself.

Two further states exist beyond up and down.

A frozen case: evidence grade undetermined, investigation running, litigation unresolved. Mark it frozen. A frozen card enters neither the main text nor the matrix; it waits in the knowledge base, with the reason written down and the thaw condition stated, which is publication of the final report. On thaw it is graded afresh. Freezing prevents two errors, concluding from an out-of-date process report and forgetting to come back once the final report appears.

A partial downgrade: within one card the facts carry different grades, the core fact at A, circumstantial material at C. The card does not drop as a whole. What drops is the support range of one fact, and the labelling switches to per-fact grades, so that a user of the card knows precisely which line is hard and which is soft. A coarse whole-card grade misleads on a complex case, and per-fact labelling is the mark of a mature one.

Disagreement about a grade can be arbitrated. Grading has a subjective element, and two people can grade the same document differently. The rule is to take the lower of the two, since the margin of safety in that rule belongs to the reader, who uses the material at the lower grade and loses nothing. Record the dispute in the case's errata line. The dispute is itself material.

## 10.5 Labelling "reconstructed"

Cases on medium evidence are still worth writing, on one condition: the inference is marked. Most organizations' internal material is B, C or D by nature, a front-line review being D, an internal investigation B, legal paperwork C. A grade system gives those materials a grammar of mutual recognition for the first time, so that an internal review can be used correctly, neither overstated nor thrown away. Teaching that grammar takes a day, and afterwards the organization's stock of old reviews turns from a pile of paper into a graded knowledge base.

The labelling convention: narrative text carries verifiable factual sentences only, and an inference opens with "Reconstructed" and states its basis.

Inference has a tone discipline of its own. Keep the modals restrained, and never write "obviously", "undoubtedly" or "of course" inside an inference. An inference is an inference, and an author who writes it as an assertion is using tone to make up for evidence. Tone runs in inverse proportion to evidence, which is a hard rule of postmortem writing: the stronger a sentence sounds, the more the reader should ask for its evidence number.

The format, by example:

"Minutes of the 14 March review meeting show the risk listed as agenda item 7 (factual sentence, grade C: meeting minutes). Reconstructed: from the agenda order and the meeting's length, the risk received less than 5 minutes of discussion and no resolution item was recorded (inference)."

The discipline for a reconstructed sentence: a reader must be able to re-check the inference along the basis given. An inference with no re-check path is not written.

Density needs self-control as well, since a case reconstructed on every page reads as a page of footnotes. When reconstruction exceeds 40% of the body text, the evidence is not yet sufficient to write the piece, and the material goes back to the matrix. The quantitative standard is a ratio of factual sentences to inferences of at least three to one. Below that ratio, the title must state that this is a reconstruction, not a report of fact.

Grades are not for life. New evidence moves a case up or down: publication of the investigation report lifts a B case to A, and a quoted original that gets overturned drops the case or removes it. The institutional carrier for that maintenance is the case library's annual review, which reads each card's evidence status in turn, asking whether a new report exists and whether the links still resolve. The output is a list of grade changes, published to readers, so that what a reader holds is always the current grade. Appendix B's case table keeps a grade column for every case. A reader's trust should follow the grade.

## 10.6 Numbers back to source, and citation convention

Every number in the main text carries the four-part record set out in Chapter 5: who counted it, when, on what sample, under what definition. Two execution rules are added here.

Rule one: the set check. Before a chapter is delivered, extract every number in the body and take the difference against the set of numbers in the source cards that chapter cites. An empty difference passes.

The difference can be audited in the other direction as well. Numbers in the cards that the text does not use are not a problem; numbers used in the text that the cards do not contain are. A reverse difference shows that the source cards are richer than the chapter, which is a good sign, since it means the author made selections rather than copies. Write the reason for each omission into the card's note, so the selection stays traceable. The practical meaning of the rule: no approximate numbers written on the fly. Every number in the body comes from a source card, or it is deleted.

The cost and the return of that discipline are worth stating. The cost is slower writing, since every number goes back to its card for a check. The return is that nobody has to check you. A reader can trust each number because the system guarantees them, and the trust compounds at the point of citation, where another author cites your number without re-verifying it, because you already did. Being citable is the long-run return on numeric discipline.

Rule two: a quotation keeps the language of the original, with a translation in brackets. Institution names, document titles and regulation names follow the glossary's bilingual table, and no chapter drifts from it.

The bibliographic form is fixed: (institution, document number or title, year, link at the Appendix B entry). Page numbers are completed at the checking stage. Where a page cannot be obtained, widen the cited range, writing "the report's executive summary" rather than "page 41 of the report". That is how a style handles uncertainty honestly.

The honesty principle extends to telling the reader the grade. A conclusion resting on A-grade evidence may be written as a flat statement. One resting on B-grade evidence is written as a probable statement. A reconstructed sentence always carries its marker. Grade disclosure lets the reader read at the right level of confidence, and an uncertain sentence never appears in a certain tone. The disclosure goes into the postmortem template of Chapter 38, where the template governs the format and the discipline governs the substance.

One more application: what to quote. Quote only sentences you have checked word for word. A reported quotation is marked as reported ("according to a paraphrase"). The distinction between paraphrase and direct quotation is the foundation of citation ethics. On that foundation sits one more check: every directly quoted sentence must be findable on a page. A direct quotation that cannot be found on a page is downgraded to a paraphrase. The check costs a minute a sentence and buys the book's zero-tolerance commitment, which is to quote one sentence fewer rather than one sentence that cannot be verified.

## The five self-checks

1. Write the minimum counterfactual for your organization's most recent loss, then run the five steps to see whether it is a luck type or an overreach type.
2. Do your project's decision records support grade A, or only grade D? Where is the gap?
3. Of the industry cases you have cited, how many survive the grade test in 10.4? How many conclusions still stand after the downgrade?
4. When did an all-purpose counterfactual last appear in one of your postmortems, and how did it pass review at the time?
5. Set a citation rule for your team: material below which grade may be cited as fact in your meeting room?

The graduation exercise sits outside the five. Write a complete case card for your organization's most recent loss: twelve fields, five-step backcast, grade labels, reconstructed sentences, the whole sequence. The work takes about half a day, and the acceptance standard is a colleague who does not know the event being able to re-check it end to end from your card and its links alone. Pass that, and you hold every component of the book's method. Writing a card is the last tool this book hands over, and every later part uses it again, the domain pages organising cases by card, the usage chapters expanding templates from cards, the boundary chapters checking against cards.

Your first card will not be good, and it is not meant to be. Its job is to expose the distance between where you are and the standard. A list of that distance is more useful than a perfect card, because the list is your study plan for the next stage. Take the finished card and check it against the field table of Chapter 5, cell by cell: which cell is empty, which cell has a story impersonating a fact, which cell's counterfactual is all-purpose. Write the result as a one-page memo. Run the memo, and your second card passes about half of it. All of Part II exists to get you to a passing second card, a fluent fifth, and a tenth you can teach from.

Before the tools are put away, run the evidence disciplines of the five chapters into one route. A reader facing a new piece of failure material passes five gates. Gate one: what grade is it (this chapter). Gate two: has it cleared the recording threshold (Chapter 2). Gate three: are the visible-at-the-time and the reconstructed-afterward kept apart (Chapter 6). Gate four: how many layers did its failure chain break (Chapter 9). Gate five: is its minimum counterfactual qualified (this chapter's five steps). Once the five are passed, the material's use is settled: cite it, refer to it, keep it as a lead, or discard it. The route fits on one sheet of paper, and that sheet is Part II's final compression.

Not every reading needs all five. Skimming the news needs gates one and two. Citing needs all five. The gates are used at levels, the way Chapter 23's releases are tiered: the use decides the depth. Material mentioned in passing needs none of them; material written into the basis of a decision passes all five.
