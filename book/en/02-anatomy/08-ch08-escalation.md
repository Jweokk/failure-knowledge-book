# Chapter 8: Escalation

> What this chapter covers: the seven rungs between "something has gone wrong" and "there is no way back", the symptom, the test, and the cover story at each rung, plus a way to find out which rung you are standing on.
> When to turn back: when a project has already gone wrong and you are deciding whether to stop or keep funding it; when reviewing a proposal to push harder.
> Reading time: 40 minutes.

This chapter and Chapter 27 (Sunk Cost and Escalation) divide the work between them, and the line is worth drawing first. Chapter 27 is a mechanism chapter and answers why: why people and organizations keep pouring resources into a path that has already failed, the psychological ledger of sunk cost, the binding of a decision-maker to a project, the institutional pressure that makes exit read as an admission of error. This chapter is anatomy and answers how. Escalation is not one decision. It is a series of rungs, and each rung has its own test and its own vocabulary of cover. What the chapter tracks, rung by rung, is the top-up: a commitment enlarged, then dated to a meeting or a signature. That date is the point of escalation.

A second boundary runs against Chapter 13 (Narrative before Evidence). The rungs are an internal process, the way an organization locks itself in step by step. Narrative is the external presentation, the way it tells the world what is happening. The two meet at rung four: a larger public commitment is both a rung and a narrative, so cases at that junction, such as the Challenger story in the walkthroughs (8.4), need both chapters read together. The internal rung decides which way the external story runs; the external story then reinforces the internal rung, because words already spoken become the reason there is no way back. That two-way reinforcement is the most complete dissection an escalation case offers, and one chapter alone leaves half the mechanics out.

The ladder metaphor needs one more piece: the elevator between rungs. Some organizations skip. A small team need not climb rung by rung, and an organization run by one strong person can go from rung one to rung six without touching the middle. Skipping saves the cost of a single voice, and the saving is the motive: the intermediate rungs go with their ceremonies. The identification is a gap in the vocabulary. Rung-one language is followed directly by rung-six language, with rungs three and four absent, and the gap is the evidence. A skipping organization is also harder to walk back. A skipped ladder has no intermediate platform, so it either turns at rung one or falls at rung six, and the intervention window is narrower rather than wider. Hence the countermeasure: where the single-voice signature is obvious, outside parties should set the warning line earlier.

One misuse has to be blocked before the model is handed over. The ladder is not an instrument for accusing colleagues. Pasting a rung number onto a person ("that is rung-two thinking") turns anatomy into a weapon. Anatomy describes processes and structures, not personalities; the ladder describes a sequence of organizational behavior, and applying it to an individual converts it into a trial of motives. Hence the first rule of use. The ladder is for self-testing, for diagnosing an organization, and for analyzing published material. Judging the motives of a named person lies outside this book's tools, always.

The other half of the usable model is what to do once you have located yourself. Every rung has a step-down action, the cheapest one that still works at that level. Rungs one and two: register and restate, so the explanation is deposited and the original accounting restored. Rungs three and four: independent review, and a freeze on commitments. Rungs five and six: re-examine the accounting basis, and freeze new investment. Rung seven has one action only: go outside, to an external adjudicator or a regulator. Stepping down is cheaper than descending a staircase, since you need not reach the bottom in one move. Two readers by design, the person inside (a self-rescue manual) and the governing layer (an intervention manual).

## 8.1 The Ladder at a Glance

Seven rungs, ordered by severity. The ordering principle takes one sentence: the higher the rung, the more expensive the currency of withdrawal. Chapter 7's three currencies project onto the ladder neatly. Rungs one to three are paid in paper and meetings, in announcements and registers, both reversible. Rung four begins paying credibility. Rungs five and six pay real money. Past rung seven even information, the raw material of every later decision, has been rebuilt. The price of the currency is the order of the rungs.

The order has a second reading, by people. Rungs one and two are mostly technical handling by the front line and middle management. From rung four upward senior management has to appear in person, because the signing authority for credibility sits with them. That hands an outside scout a useful detail: the density of senior executives personally delivering set phrases in public material is a high-precision indicator of ladder position.

Each rung also pays for the next in commitments. Explaining the anomaly away classifies the fact as an exception. Shrinking it books the cost at zero. Finding a scapegoat spares everyone inside from repenting. Promising more ties the public image to the failure. Changing the measure stops the ledger showing deterioration. Enlarging investment rewrites the cost of exit. Sealing information leaves nobody inside able to object. An outside observer sees "no way back" only after all seven, while inside nobody has looked for a way back since rung three.

Two uses, for two audiences. Outside observers, whether investors, regulators or journalists, use the ladder as reconnaissance: count how often each of the seven vocabularies appears in the target's public material. An organization carrying all the rung-three-and-above phrases is a high-risk target, and the cost of looking is one read-through of the public file. Insiders use it as a self-test. You cannot stop senior management climbing, but you can judge your own rung, and after rung three the cost of speaking plainly rises steeply. The purpose is not martyrdom. It is deciding where your information should go, whether the direct reporting channel of Chapter 16 or the external channel of Chapter 19.

Both uses rest on one precondition: visibility. The linguistic signature of each rung has to be written out in full, which is what the cover-story lines in 8.2 are for. Learn those sentences by heart and the radar is installed.

## 8.2 Rung by Rung

### Rung One: Explain It Away

**Symptom.** The anomaly is assigned a one-off cause. Weather, an individual case, one new employee's mistake, the coincidence of several very unlikely events. The attribution never enters the register and produces no process change.

**Test.** When the same class of anomaly appears a second time, is the explanation identical to the first? If it is, rung one is running.

The automated version is explanation comparison. The attribution field for anomalies sharing a keyword is displayed side by side, and a human judges the similarity; no algorithm is needed, the eye is enough. A quarterly rhythm is suggested, with workload running linearly with the number of anomalies, so a busy quarter costs half an hour more. The output is an explanation-drift report: each year's explanations of the same anomaly in parallel, where the drift in wording is the running record of rung one. It doubles as knowledge capture, since a collapsed explanation becomes a counterexample, and the counterexample library is live teaching material for new staff. That is the hidden dividend of an anomaly register.

A detail belongs in the countermeasure against resetting to zero. The first-attribution column records an explanation; the same column on a later, similar entry records a test. When a repeat appears, the register pulls the earlier explanation up for comparison, triggered by a matching keyword rather than by anyone's memory. Three outcomes get three handlings: the explanation holds, so the attribution is confirmed and archived; it collapses, so the attribution is escalated and analysis triggered; it cannot be tested, so the attribution is marked open and held. Those branches go into the register's field logic, with a comparison-result field of holds, collapses, or open. The point is to make the register think: it stops being a warehouse and becomes a testing machine for attribution quality.

**Cover story.** "This one was an exception." "Everyone in the industry runs it this way." "It just happened to coincide."

### Rung Two: Shrink It

**Symptom.** The scope of the impact assessment narrows after the fact. An event first reported as three hours of downtime becomes, in the management pack, "brief latency perceived by a small number of users". Costs outside the casualty count, such as handling hours, customer churn and premium increases, never reach the ledger.

**Test.** Put the two impact descriptions of the same event side by side. Has the scope moved? Is there an impact register opened at the original scope, or only one written at the final scope? No register means shrinkage is running.

The anchor row has an external version. The first report to a regulator is a legal anchor: the initial filing under a statutory reporting duty is protected by law, and any later change of scope has to be justified in writing to a regulator who reviews the reason. That makes retrospective shrinkage show up. If the internal report says the impact was contained while the initial filing says three hours of downtime, the gap between the two documents is a ready-made line of inquiry. Consistency between the internal register and external filings therefore has two motives, legal and governance, and the annual self-check on it belongs in the internal audit plan.

One discipline should be fixed hard. The initial scope, once registered, is the anchor of the ledger, and any later change may only be added as a new row citing the anchor row. Change the scope, never the history. The target is retrospective shrinkage: three hours of downtime at the time, "a brief fluctuation" in the quarterly report. Enforcement is row-level locking, where a registered row cannot be edited and corrections are new rows. That is mature in financial systems, where it is the irreversible journal entry; porting it to an impact register is a configuration job.

**Cover story.** "The impact is contained." "Actual losses are far below the book figure." "The vast majority of users noticed nothing."

### Rung Three: Find a Scapegoat

**Symptom.** Responsibility is placed where it cannot answer back. A departed employee, an outside supplier, the previous management team, the macroeconomy, user misuse. The attribution has one property in common: every serving member of the organization is safe.

The precondition deserves naming first: the candidate pool. Whoever has already left, whoever is most junior, the outsourced, the deceased, anyone unable to defend themselves. The pool's shared property is that the cost of resisting is infinite. Its contents are a reading of the culture: an organization whose first thought after a failure is who to blame shows that systems analysis never had a place in its accountability culture. The countermeasure is proceduralized accountability, in which accident analysis is driven by a template whose question list carries the names of stages and never the names of people. That leaves a scapegoat no grammar to stand in.

**Test.** Does the attribution contain no serving, named party at all? A scapegoat is the cheap substitute for accountability rather than its opposite: one head buys the system an exemption from inspection.

Who gets chosen follows a pattern worth a footnote. Outsourcing and agency labor are the high-yield pool: the relationship is a contract rather than employment, and the legal procedure for disposing of them is the simplest. The scapegoating rate of contract workers is a product of labor structure and accountability culture together. The public observers here are labor inspectors and the press. The construction shows up in official statements as "the temporary worker", "the outsourced contractor", "the agency staff", and the appearance of those words is the fingerprint, which is why inspectors can put them on the list of flagged terms for reviewing a public notice.

A second fingerprint is grammatical. Is the subject of the attribution sentence a singular person or a plural system? "An employee violated procedure" is a suspect sentence. "The procedure did not cover this case" is a systems sentence. The deciding point is where the remedy lands. A remedy that punishes a person leaves the scapegoat standing. A remedy that revises a procedure serves accountability and improvement at once. The two readings are not mutually exclusive, and the test is proportion: only the person handled and no system action taken, and confidence is very high. An outside observer can then rewrite the scapegoat sentence into a systems sentence, and that rewriting right, held by journalists and researchers, is the public tool against a scapegoating culture.

**Cover story.** "The employee concerned has been dealt with under company policy." "We were let down mainly by our supplier." "This is a legacy issue."

### Rung Four: Promise More

**Symptom.** An existing failure is met with a larger target. A bigger feature set announced after a delay, a higher revenue target announced after widening losses, "a higher safety standard" announced after an accident. The public commitment is defensive: occupy the narrative first, so that "we have failed" has no place left to stand in the language.

Defensive commitment has a more concealed form: borrowing someone else's mouth, in a vision speech at an industry conference or a long-range plan in a media interview. Commitments through third-party channels never enter the formal disclosure process and escape internal review. The identifying point is the separation between the channel and the formal one. The vision appears only in the interview, never in the annual report, and the separation is the signal. The consequences match a formal commitment, since the market prices the stock on what it hears, but the company keeps a right of denial: that was only an outlook offered in an interview. That right is the clearest feature of the type.

Two market indicators turn defensive commitment into a score rather than a feeling. The ratio of commitment to resource comes first: a jump in the ratio, with the target doubling while the budget stays flat, is high confidence. The data are public, since a listed company's targets and budgets both sit in its filings, and for a private organization you read the supplementary budget requests. The interval between announcement and exposure comes second, where a major new commitment published within two weeks of the exposure is probably defensive. Together they move identification from impression to grading: a ratio jump plus a coinciding window is two points, and a total over threshold flags red.

**Test.** Where does the resource for the new commitment come from? If the resources behind the raised target come from cutting testing, cutting validation or cutting redundancy, which is ground held by Chapter 20 (the slack of success) and Chapter 25 (margin erosion), then this rung has started structurally.

Resources are not only money, either. Headcount, hours and management attention are resources, and the common form of rung four is pressing the same people into two jobs at once, closing out the old project while sprinting at the new target. Borrowing from one line to pay another is invisible on the budget sheet, which records only money; it shows in rosters and timesheets. The tracking tool is a quarterly comparison of hours distribution, where the drift in a key team's share of hours is the invisible progress bar of rung four. Its readers are the PMO and the governing layer, because the war the budget cannot see is visible in the roster.

**Cover story.** "Turning a challenge into an opportunity." "Double down and burn the boats." "Hold ourselves to a higher standard."

### Rung Five: Change the Measure

**Symptom.** The indicator is swapped, the basis adjusted, the baseline reset. This is a change of ruler rather than a falsification. The indicator measuring the quantity that deteriorated most visibly is quietly replaced by one that performs better.

**Test.** Was the statistical basis changed in the same period, without independent review? Can the reason for the reset baseline be independently verified? What separates rung five from Chapter 12 (goal displacement) is timing. Chapter 12 describes an indicator optimized over years until it no longer measures what it names. Rung five is an emergency ruler change at the moment failure surfaces, and the discriminator is the coincidence in time.

The coincidence can be quantified. Measure the interval between the ruler-change date and the bad-news date: look back from the announcement for the nearest bad news, a loss warning, an accident notice or a regulatory inquiry, and record a change within thirty days as an emergency suspect. Suspects go into the register described in Chapter 12 with one added column: days since the nearest bad news. The annual statistics on that column are a thermometer for the organization's ruler-changing culture. A high share of emergency suspects means ruler changes are stress responses rather than science, and stress-driven changes also carry a high later correction rate, which is the subsequent evidence of stress.

**Cover story.** "To measure it more scientifically." "To align with industry practice." "Adjusting the statistical method."

### Rung Six: Enlarge the Commitment

**Symptom.** Resources are added to a path already shown to fail. More budget, more people, more schedule, more leverage. The reasoning no longer depends on new evidence; the argument is the size of what has been spent. We have put in this much, so stopping now loses all of it. This is the home ground of Chapter 27, which supplies the classic experimental basis of the sunk-cost effect, Staw's 1976 design, along with the institutional conditions. (Staw, "Knee-deep in the big muddy", Organizational Behavior and Human Performance 16: 27–44, 1976; the Crossref record has been verified.)

**Test.** In the minutes of the incremental decision meeting, what share of the arguments is "we have already invested"? Once is rhetoric. Where it becomes the main argument, that is rung six.

A measurement basis for that share: the ratio of already-invested arguments to new-return arguments in one set of minutes, where a ratio above three to one marks the documentary form of rung six. What makes the measure executable is that both classes are countable, and annotating one set of minutes is half an hour of reading. The training material is this chapter's list of cover stories, which maps phrases to argument types. The output is an escalation-concentration curve for budget meetings, quarter by quarter. The budget season in which concentration rises is the season in which the ladder is being climbed.

**Cover story.** "Stop now and everything we have put in is wasted." "One more doubling and we turn it around." "Our competitors are escalating too."

### Rung Seven: Seal Off Information

**Symptom.** The circle of the informed contracts, the external line is unified, the channels for challenge stop working. Classification levels rise, minutes move from written to verbal, the person who raised an objection is transferred, which is Chapter 19 (why the truth-teller goes first) cashing out, and data stop reaching the board or the regulator in full.

**Test.** Do access tightening, a demand for one line, and the movement of dissenters coincide in time with the exposure of failure? Coincidence means rung seven.

In a digital workplace the coincidence test has a new fingerprint: the change log of the permission system. Bulk adjustments to access rights, bulk upgrades of classification, temporary rerouting of the approval chain. The log records every tightening faithfully, and timeline analysis against the exposure date is basic digital forensics; for a management audit it takes one query. Its advantage is that it asks nobody's cooperation. In the paper era, investigating a seal took interviews and courage; in the log era the seal leaves its own trace. The precondition is that the log itself is not sealed, so its access control must be independent of the business system under audit. If the auditor can reach the log, the fingerprint means something. Past this rung the organization holds no route by which "turn back" can be raised, and the problem worsens until outside parties see it.

One variable sets the speed of that worsening: cash flow. How fast a rung-seven organization deteriorates is finally decided by cash, since a problem can be carried as long as cash flow holds, and cash flow is the fuel depot of delay. The reading is available from outside: payables stretching, abnormal inventory turnover, the rolling frequency of short-term borrowing. Creditors and suppliers watch all three daily, and their abnormal combination, stretched payables plus rolling borrowings plus inventory piling up, is the financial portrait of a rung-seven organization. That portrait is a direct input to a lender's credit decision, and the money that keeps such an organization alive is a prepayment on the next bill.

Outside observers have an intervention point too. A rung-seven organization still answers hard external signals: a regulatory inquiry, a qualified audit opinion, a core supplier shortening credit terms, an unusual rhythm of executive departures. What rung seven seals is internal dissent; statutory and contractual pressure still has to be taken, usually by reassurance and delay. The checklist is therefore which external actions penetrate the reassurance layer: a qualified audit opinion, a regulator's on-site inspection, a major customer's contractual right to audit. Those three carry legal or contractual force, and the layer cannot hold them. The list is valuable in the hands of creditors, large customers and regulators, the external braking system of a rung-seven organization. Where the brake bites is this chapter's offering to readers in finance and procurement.

**Cover story.** "To prevent the release of unverified information." "One message, to avoid panic." "Exceptional measures for an exceptional period."

## 8.3 The Mechanics: Why Nobody Stops Halfway

Before the seventh rung there are many positions where the climb could stop, and it rarely does. Three layers explain the failure to stop.

Layer one: every rung delivers a real local gain, and the list deserves to be complete. Rung one buys output without stopping the line. Rung two buys a presentable report. Rung three buys a temporarily steady morale. Rung four buys the share price and the financing. Rung five buys a passed appraisal. Rung six buys a diluted unit cost. Rung seven buys surface quiet inside. The gains are real, and a defense built on denying them gets nowhere. The design principle is substitution: give the organization a way to obtain the same gain without escalating. Rung two's gain, for instance, can be bought with disciplined disclosure of the impact register, which also buys a credible report and a reassured regulator. Substitution rather than confrontation is the core technique, finding a clean source for the benefit. The accounts of escalation are global and intertemporal, while decisions are local and current, which is the ground Chapter 28 (horizon mismatch) covers.

Layer two: irreversibility rises rung by rung, and the early warning indicator is the lead time of reversible actions. At rung one the explanation is written after the fact, the next day. By rung four it is pre-written before the meeting, with the commitment text finalized ahead of the event's conclusion. That inversion, conclusion before evidence, is the precursor of an irreversible jump. Detect it by comparing document timestamps, where a commitment text finalized earlier than the record confirming failure is an alarm. The response is an audit, since an inverted sequence means the decision process has been turned upside down and every document it produced needs re-reading. In Chapter 7's three currencies, the actions of rungs one to three (explaining, booking, attributing) are almost entirely reversible, undoable with an announcement and an edit to the register. Rung four pays credibility, rung five pays the credibility of the ledger, rung six pays cash and contractual obligation, and past rung seven even information has been rebuilt. The further down, the dearer the way back: that is the physical shape of a slide.

Layer three: each rung shrinks the option space of the next. A quantifiable expression exists: topic survival rate, the frequency with which challenge-the-status-quo items appear on meeting agendas and how many rounds they survive. In a rung-seven organization's agenda they are extinct. The method is an annual sample: pull the year's agendas, count the challenge items, and follow what happened to each, whether discussed, shelved or vanished. The year-on-year comparison is a trend in openness, and an organization on a downward trend has rung seven at the door. The signal belongs to the governing layer, since topic survival is one of the few cultural indicators visible without a survey, needing only the agenda archive. Rung three's scapegoat deletes "the system has a problem" from the agenda. Rung five's ruler change hides the real data from rung six's incremental decision. Rung seven's seal removes the input that would have triggered a stop-loss after rung six. The rungs are not seven parallel behaviors on a menu. They are a one-way street, each later rung built on the foundation of an earlier one.

The one-way street can be walked backwards inside a specific window. A triggering event that changes level, a new chief executive, regulatory intervention, pressure from a major customer, a financial alarm, can sometimes knock an organization from rung five back to rung two, with the new ruler scrapped and the impact restated. An external shock resets the internal cost of the cover story. For outside parties the lesson is timing: the first three months after a shock are the best window for demanding a restatement, because the cost of self-justification is highest and the cost of compliance lowest. The window closes fast, with a new vocabulary rebuilt inside a quarter.

## 8.4 Two Walkthroughs

Walkthrough one: locating Challenger on the ladder, drawing on the Rogers Commission report (1986) and Vaughan's study (1996). The O-ring erosion found after earlier flights was explained as "normal within acceptable limits", which is rung one running flight after flight. The cold-temperature risk on launch day was pressed in the evening teleconference of January 27, 1986, and that pressure has the same structure as rungs one and two. Contractor management reversed itself after the meeting was paused and came out for launch, which is the meeting form of rung four, a commitment set against a doubt. (The official text of the Rogers report was read and verified on November 1, 2026. The original-source counterpart of the rung-one phrase appears in Volume 1, Chapter 6, where Thiokol's conclusion after flight 51-C described the condition as not ideal, though acceptable, and the launch constraint established in July 1985 was waived by the project manager flight after flight. The rung-four meeting form appears in the Volume 1, Chapter 5 record of the afternoon caucus. Vaughan (1996) remains available only on paper, pending interlibrary loan.) The point of the walkthrough is to aim an abstract rung at a specific meeting, so that each rung lands on a date, a meeting name and a signatory.

Walkthrough two: the judgment made in flight on Columbia. At the launch on January 16, 2003, foam shed from the external tank and struck the left wing. In the project assessments before the accident, foam shedding was classified as "in-family", meaning the same class as shedding already observed on earlier flights and therefore not a new safety concern. The Columbia Accident Investigation Board gave that classification a dedicated review: the standard itself absorbed "a larger piece of foam" into an existing category, and the new information, the location and energy of the strike, was never entitled to trigger new analysis. (CAIB Vol. 1, pp. 122–123, verified.) This is rungs one and two working together, explaining it away as a familiar phenomenon and shrinking it by grading against historical experience, while the information visible at the time did contain an option that would have forced reassessment: requesting external imagery. That request was cancelled partway along the decision chain, and the CAIB report carries a section on it. (CAIB Vol. 1, p. 39, verified.) The conclusion lands on structure again. The absence of a rule for updating the classification standard let rung one run automatically.

Both walkthroughs share one teaching point: locatability. An abstract escalation concept, once landed on a date, a meeting name and a signatory, becomes a concrete object for a corrective action list. The output is coordinates, not a verdict. The suggested format is a ladder-location table, the seven rungs as rows and the case's evidence in the columns: which document, which meeting, which sentence as spoken. A row that can be filled in is a rung that has already happened. The table has a second use in postmortem training: give trainees a historical case and have them fill it in, and the filling is how the tests get internalized. The check on internalization is how fast a trainee locates a new case.

## 8.5 Devices for Stepping Down

Four devices, ordered by installation cost, cheapest first.

Device one: the anomaly register. Three columns per entry: initial impact scope, attribution, and whether the same class recurred. Its function is to remove rung one's hiding place, since an explanation has to be deposited, and when the same anomaly returns, the earlier explanation becomes the object under test.

A threshold has to be designed into its maintenance: the bar for registering an anomaly must be low, with the front line defining what counts. A high bar, admitting only anomalies whose consequences exceed some limit, leaves rung one a permanent hiding place, because small-consequence anomalies are never registered and explaining them away costs nothing. The price of a low bar is noise, and noise is handled by quarterly folding, which merges anomalies of one pattern into a single line so the register stays readable. The ratio between threshold and folding is the core operating parameter: low enough for the front line to register without burden, folded enough that management is not drowned.

Device two: a register of accounting changes. Any adjustment to a statistical basis, a baseline or a measurement method is registered separately, stating the proposer, the reason and the effect, and it needs an independent reviewer's signature. Under this register, rung five's change of ruler turns from a covert operation into an explicit act.

The step after an explicit act is an explicit trail: an annual compilation of the change register, one year's ruler changes in one view, which ruler, proposed by whom, what the reviewer said. The compilation should be visible to the whole governing layer, since the list is their first-hand material for judging whether reporting is credible. A governing layer that reads the reports but never the list is reading a world someone else has already adjusted. The second reader is the audit, because basis drift is a high-value lead and the register cuts the evidence-gathering cost by an order of magnitude.

Device three: exit conditions up front. At approval, write down which signals start an exit review, who adjudicates, and that at the review the investment already made no longer counts as an argument. Chapter 27 gives the full design. The after-the-fact defense of rungs four and six fails against an ex-ante clause.

Timing of the signature matters for the clause's force: it has to be signed at approval. Exit conditions added after a project runs into trouble have no teeth, because by then the drafter is already standing on the ladder. Being ex-ante is psychological as well as political, since nobody knows whether their own project will fail, and that makes the clause fair to everyone. A fair clause signed at the start, cited in a crisis three years later, leaves no party able to call it unjust.

Device four: rewards for termination. Reward, as policy, the person who stops a project, so that whoever halts a sunk project receives the same level of recognition as whoever rescues one.

One technical point sits in how the award is judged: post-hoc verification of the stop. What became of the stopped project, and how does the reason it died compare with the reason for stopping it. Where the comparison supports the decision, the award stands. Where it overturns the decision, the case is recorded anyway, because a stop that was a misjudgment is also learning material. Termination rewards do not only reward the right calls; they record every call, and the completeness keeps the institution from deforming into a correctly-guessing contest. The PMO compiles the aftermath of all stopped projects each year into the review material. This is the reverse-incentive design that answers Chapter 27's mechanism. Without it, the people who run the first three devices are systematically eliminated by the performance system (Chapter 19).

Publicity is an execution detail. Recognition has to be organization-wide and public: an all-hands meeting, an internal newsletter, a promotion announcement. Private recognition carries no cultural effect. The worry is that the stopper gets labelled, and nobody dares let them run a project again. The hedge is to classify the stop: stopped because the market moved versus stopped because execution failed, the first an award for judgment, the second an award for correction, with both framings in the citation. The long-run effect is desensitization. As stopping becomes ordinary, the organization's cost of failure falls, and the end point is a stop that is no longer news. On that day the termination reward has done its work and can sit quietly inside the system.

A self-test to close the section. Take the project you are responsible for now and answer three questions. Did the most recent anomaly get an attribution in the register? Is the scope of the latest impact assessment consistent with the previous one? If all investment went to zero tomorrow, is your first reaction to check the data or to check who leaked? If you answer the third with the second, you are at rung six at least.

The third question deserves expansion into a graded set: it is the detector for rung seven, the second for rung five, the first for rung one. The complete five run like this: did the attribution reach the register (rung one), is the accounting scope consistent (rung five), are the signatures complete (rung four), has anyone been moved (rung three), has information been sealed (rung seven). Five questions together locate the rung. The organizational use is an annual cultural check, answered anonymously by everyone, where the distribution of positive answers by rung is the organization's escalation climate map and the year-on-year comparison is the trend. The reader is the governing layer. The map is more honest than any cultural slogan, because it measures behavior rather than statements.

One note on reading order. Read this chapter before Chapter 27. The ladder gives the skeleton: locate the rung with the tests, then go to Chapter 27 for why the stopping is so hard. Locating before explaining matches the natural rhythm of handling a crisis, which is to stop the bleeding before looking for the cause. The pair is finished when you can say two things about any escalation case without pausing: which rung it stands on now, and why it cannot stop. Those two sentences are everything the two chapters have to give.

## Self-Check: Five Questions

1. Which of the seven rungs does the most recent anomaly on your project belong to? Test it against the criteria in 8.2 one by one, not by feel.
2. When did your organization last change its performance metric? Was the change registered? Who reviewed it?
