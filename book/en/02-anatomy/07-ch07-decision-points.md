# Chapter 7 Decision Points and Points of No Return

> What this chapter covers: how to recognise the two most valuable positions on a failure chain, the decision point where the track can still be switched and the point of no return where it cannot, with a reversibility test table you can apply directly.
> When to turn back: when setting checkpoints into a process, when defining signature authority, or when judging whether there is still time to brake.
> A fourth use, past the three openings of the chapter: training a newcomer. These two concepts are the first lesson of induction in any industry. A newcomer who lacks them treats every moment as equally important and spreads attention like pepper from a shaker. The teaching kit is this chapter's table plus three cases from the newcomer's own industry. Fill three tables and the newcomer has the feel for where the points sit in that trade.
> The same kit scales to an industry association. A published checklist of points of no return, drafted once from this chapter's table plus cases contributed by member firms, gives every member's release form a common skeleton. It is the highest-yield service an association can offer: one drafting, a whole sector served.
> Estimated reading time: 40 minutes.

Not every moment on a failure chain deserves your attention.

Attention is the scarcest resource in a postmortem. The session runs two hours, and where those two hours land decides what the session produces. The allocation rule is the two tools of this chapter: mark the decision points first, because a fork earns the time; mark the points of no return next, because a cliff edge earns the time; run every remaining advance moment in a single line each.

Put a number on the rule and you have decision-point density. A competent postmortem carries between five and ten marked decision points. Fewer than three means the failure chain was drawn too coarse. More than fifteen means advance moments have been promoted to forks. Calibrate against good published work: the count of findings in a formal investigation report is a usable yardstick for where the density should sit.

Most moments are the event advancing. At a few of them, someone stands at a fork with the untaken roads still in hand. This chapter supplies two tools for finding those moments: recognising the fork (the decision point), and recognising, past the fork, the step from which no road leads back (the point of no return). Both tools come with one test table.

The relationship between the two tools is worth drawing on a timeline. Decision points cluster before the point of no return. A failure chain usually carries several decision points and one or a few points of no return. Decision points are opportunities; points of no return are boundaries.

The timeline has a management consequence. The last decision point before the point of no return is the most valuable position on the chain, and the minimum counterfactual of Chapter 5 usually lands there. Staffing that position is a governance question: who stands there, and does that person hold enough authority and enough information to use what they see. Chapter 18's single-owner list carries the answer.

The timeline also reads backwards. Count decision points backward from the point of no return: first back, second back, third back. The smaller the backward index, the lower the intervention cost and the more indirect the effect. Choosing among the backward indices is the whole art of the postmortem.

## 7.1 The Decision Point: Three Necessary Conditions

A moment that satisfies three conditions at once is a decision point.

Condition one: at least two real options exist. Real means the option carries a concrete action, a concrete cost, and could have been chosen at the time. "Be more careful" is not an option. "Delay until the next day, when daytime temperature rises" is one, and it was the option rejected in the Challenger case (Rogers Commission report, 1986).

Condition two: the person with authority to choose sits within reach. In the room, on the other end of the line, at the next node of the approval flow.

Options laid on the wrong table have a standard shape. The one who could actually veto is not present, and the ones present believe they hold no veto.

An option set can also fail before anyone enters the room. The menu was fixed in advance. The real choice happened in informal contact the week before, and the meeting is a coronation. A ritualised meeting satisfies the three conditions of condition one on paper and none of them in substance.

Detection runs on the agenda timeline. Look at how mature each item was when it reached the table. If the pre-read already carries a conclusion and its endorsements, the decision point sits before the meeting, and the search has to move backward.

Moving backward is done by reconstructing the shadow meeting: the email, the phone call, the corridor. The table in the shadow meeting is the real table. Read the shadow record beside the formal minutes. Reading two meetings together is advanced postmortem work, and it is the only way to recover a decision that was made in a room with no minutes.

Shadow material is usually absent from the file. Words in a corridor leave no trace, and that is the decision-point version of the recording gap described in Chapter 6. Move a decision point into the untraced zone and the postmortem runs out of food. Chapter 18's diffusion of responsibility has this collapse as its core symptom: everyone present, nobody holding the authority to stop.

Prevention sits at the level of the criterion, and it is cheap. Run a power pre-check when the process is designed. Annotate each decision point with three answers: who may choose here, who generates the options, who confirms a veto. Write the three answers into the process document. The cost is three extra lines at design time. What it prevents is a collapse of authority during operation, and that collapse is far more expensive to repair. Learning after an accident that nobody at a given position held the authority to stop is the most expensive way to learn it.

Review the annotations annually, folded into the process review. Every major process change updates the authority annotation in the same pass. A stale annotation is as dangerous as a stale process.

Condition three: the consequence of the choice appears later. Time sits between the decision and its consequence, and that interval is both a window for correction and a bed for illusion.

Mid-course checkpoints are the governance tool for the interval. Place one or two mandatory review points between decision and consequence, and ask a single question at each: how far does the current actual diverge from what we expected. This converts the window from passive to active. Divergence is measurable before the consequence lands, and a measurable divergence triggers a correction rather than a blame round. The institutional footing for a correction culture sits exactly here.

Density scales with the consequence cycle: one checkpoint per third of the cycle. A decision whose consequence matures in three years gets review points at nine months and at eighteen months.

Route the output into the decision record. It is the counter-document to the probability draft of Chapter 6: the expectation then, the actual now. The comparison of two drafts is the learning itself.

Deferred appearance supports two readings. "We chose correctly, which is why nothing happened," and "our turn has not come yet." Telling them apart waits for Chapter 11 on the normalization of deviance. For now, hold one line: a deferred decision is free in the mind and never free on the ledger.

The psychological accounting has a forward-facing version. Deferral does more than make the current decision feel free; it teaches the current decider a wrong way to read feedback. Nothing bad happens in the short term, the decider reads nothing-bad as positive feedback, and the misreading accumulates into the confidence illusion of Chapter 14.

The correction device is the post-decision audit. Thirty days after a major decision, review once: what did we expect, what actually happened, how do we explain the difference.

Cost, half an hour. What it buys is speed in the feedback loop. Without it, feedback waits for the consequence to cash, and a decision with a three-year consequence cycle carries a three-year learning cycle.

Give the audit a slot in the decision record template: the three lines of Chapter 38 plus a fourth, the audit date. One line of text turns every major decision into a learning loop.

Moments satisfying all three conditions appear more than once on a failure chain, which is why the list matters more than any single point on it.

The list also feeds the book's stop points. Every decision point on it is raw material for a stop point. A stop point is not a literary flourish; it is a decision point made teachable, the real fork on a real chain carried to the reader intact.

One operation converts list to stop point: lay the information that was visible at the time in front of the fork. With the information assembled, the fork becomes the reader's problem.

The craft sits in the proportion. Information too complete and the problem is trivial. Too fragmentary and the problem is unfair. Calibrate by testing the distribution of reader judgments. A problem everyone answers the same way carries no value, and a problem that splits the room is a good one.

The principle is stated here so the reader knows a stop point is not decoration. Behind every one of them sits a list of decision points dug out of a real failure chain.

The discipline for writing and for postmortems is to take the latest one. The latest decision point on the chain is the anchor of the minimum counterfactual (field 11 of Chapter 5; Chapter 10 gives the full backward-walking method). The latest decision point carries the lowest intervention cost, sits closest to the consequence, and holds the most complete evidence.

The latest-one rule has a boundary case. At the latest decision point, every option is bad. Postponement is bad; degradation is bad. A dead corner is not a good anchor for a minimum counterfactual, because an anchor should be a position that held one good option not dependent on luck. Handle a dead corner by moving forward: find where a good option appeared, and anchor there. Record the dead corner as evidence of the organisation's helplessness.

Keep both marks. The anchor is marked for the counterfactual, the dead corner for structural repair. A dead corner shows that the organisation needs a mechanism for generating new options, not better technique at choosing among the options it has.

A second boundary case: the latest decision point sometimes carries the least authority. The person who could actually stop the thing left the chain at an earlier position, so the latest position where intervention remained feasible sits with someone who holds no authority. Annotate both levels: the latest feasible position for training, the earliest position holding authority for institutional change.

Add a column to the decision point list for the authority grade of the decider at that position, one of full, partial, none. Statistics on that column expose the organisation's authorization disease. If the last feasible position at most key decision points concentrates in the hands of people with no authority, the authorization structure needs rebuilding, and the column is the proof.

One more distinction has to hold: a decision point and a point of accountability are different objects. A postmortem chases decision points, meaning where the track could be switched. Courts and disciplinary processes chase points of accountability, meaning who owes a legal duty for the consequence.

The two sit on different scales. Decision points are positions on the failure chain, distributed across the whole advance of the event. Points of accountability are legal anchors: a signature, a job title, a duty of care. They are far sparser. The sparsity follows from what law will connect, which is foreseeability and control. Law does not assign fault to a link you could neither foresee nor control.

The gap between sparse and dense is the division of labour between legal learning and engineering learning. Law teaches people to discharge duty at sparse anchors. Engineering teaches people to switch tracks at dense forks. An organisation needs both, and merging them into a single meeting loses both.

Coincidence, never necessity. The latest feasible switch point may sit with someone who holds no authority, while those holding authority narrowed the options to zero at several earlier positions. Take the accountability answer as the postmortem answer and the organisation learns "sign carefully." It does not learn who narrowed the options, in which meeting, on what grounds.

Resisting that gravity has an organisational form: an independent right to convene the postmortem. Convening does not pass through the accountability line. Safety or quality convenes directly, and the accountability line assembles its own material separately. Separating the two lines is standard in industries with mature investigation systems (Device 2 and Device 4 of Chapter 3), and it works inside a single firm.

The resistance comes from the efficiency argument: two meetings for one accident is expensive. The cost comparison is the output quality of the two meetings. A merged meeting produces a finding of responsibility. A separated meeting produces an improvement list. The two documents do not sit on the same shelf.

The answer to the efficiency argument sits in the cost table. The half-day saved by merging buys no effective improvement item at all.

A technical form of resistance: the conclusion layer of the postmortem and the conclusion layer of the legal document cite each other and never substitute for each other. The postmortem writes structural findings; the judgment's factual section is cited as an evidence source. Different readers, different purposes, different standards, and a document that mixes them serves neither reader.

The organisational form is two texts on two signature lines. Safety signs the postmortem, answering the improvement question. Legal signs the legal document, answering the responsibility question. The lines meet at the fact layer, which exists once, and diverge at the analysis layer, where the purposes part: the postmortem asks how to prevent, the court asks who answers.

Cost, one more document. Return, both kinds of learning hold and prevention learning does not contaminate responsibility learning.

## 7.2 The Three Currencies of Reversibility

Irreversibility is a spectrum, never a switch. Decompose "undoing an action" into the capacity to pay in three currencies.

Currency one is money. After the action, can money buy it back? Rebuild a part, switch a supplier, recall a batch. Money works while the object is unused, unconsumed, not yet grafted onto something else. A bad supplier on the approved list comes off for a switching fee. Concrete poured into a wall does not come back for any sum.

Currency two is time. After the action, can waiting undo it? Postpone a release, a departure, a construction window. Time works while the window stands open. Before it closes, postponement is a costless undo. After it closes, the same action jumps from the reversible column into the irreversible one with nothing in between.

Currency three is credibility. After the action, can it be withdrawn in public? A correction notice, a recall statement, an apology with compensation. Credibility works while nobody has yet taken an irreversible action of their own on the strength of your statement. Correcting a notice nobody followed costs little. Correcting a standard the downstream has executed for six months costs in proportion to the breadth of that execution.

What none of the three currencies can buy back forms the fourth category: the signature and the go-live. The release engineer signs the airworthiness release, the software team presses deploy, the aircraft finishes its takeoff roll, the reactor synchronises to the grid, the drug enters the formulary, the announcement goes out. The shared structure: a statement has been made about the world ("this thing is good"), the world has begun to act on the statement (passengers buy tickets, clients call the endpoint, physicians prescribe), and withdrawal no longer depends on your capacity to pay. It depends on what others have already sunk.

Split the fourth category in two tiers. The signature tier makes a single-point statement: one release, one approval. The go-live tier makes a systemic statement: a product launch, a regulation taking effect, a public announcement. The withdrawal cost structures differ. Withdrawing a signature faces the validity of a document. Withdrawing a go-live faces an ecosystem's adaptation, and that cost scales at network level with what others have built on the statement.

The tiering has a budget consequence. At the signature tier, a second confirmation slot can be a person. At the go-live tier it has to be a system, because human confirmation does not hold against launch momentum. Grey release and the canary are the engineered second confirmation slot at that tier.

That engineering is the software industry's best contribution to this chapter's table. For any go-live tier action, in any industry, one question is worth asking: where is my canary.

One-line test, expanding field 9 of Chapter 5: money can change it, time can change it, a public correction can change it; a signature and a go-live cannot.

The test carries a time dimension, because reversibility decays. Right after the action, money still works: the part is uninstalled, the stock is on hand. Three months on, money no longer saves it: the part sits in the airframe, the stock has dispersed. Reversibility has a half-life.

The management consequence is to pull the undo decision early. Every day of delay after the error is discovered burns reversibility. The escalation described in Chapter 27 burns a psychological account; delay burns physical reversibility.

Quantify the half-life by annotating each action with a reversible window: the installation cycle of the part, the turnover days of the stock. Supply chain and production systems already hold that data.

The annotation costs one comparison table, and it upgrades the test from a static instrument to a dynamic one. The test says whether the action can still be undone. The window table says for how much longer.

One misreading has to be blocked. Irreversibility is a spectrum, and the three currencies are usually paid in part. Money recovers one portion, time another. An assessment of partial reversibility must name which portion is reversible and which is not, and a half-reversible action is defended on its irreversible half.

The mechanism behind the misreading is the normalization of Chapter 11. Reading half-reversible as reversible is step one of normalizing, because the language game about margin begins with a vague currency.

The action for the reader: for any action assessed as partially reversible, write the irreversible half into the notes column of the table. If you cannot name that half concretely, the assessment is not finished.

## 7.3 The Reversibility Test Table

Section 7.2 becomes a form you can hand to someone.

A digitised version embeds the test in the workflow system. The action node forces a result to be entered, the four answers go into the system, and an irreversible class action triggers additional steps automatically: second confirmation, cooling period, higher authority.

The benefit is that the judgment stops depending on the individual's diligence, which drifts. By the thirtieth release of the year, the judgment has gone from deliberate to reflex.

The cost is mechanisation. The system asks fixed questions, and a new form of action may not fit a fixed test. Keep an exception channel, a manual judgment path, and let anything routed through it leave a trace. The trace is the safety valve on a digital test system.

For the action you are assessing, answer row by row.

| Row | Question | "Yes" means | "No" means |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Does undoing this cost within the normal budget? | Money-reversible | Move to row 2 |
| 2 | Does an unclosed window allow postponement or a redo? | Time-reversible | Move to row 3 |
| 3 | Can the external commitment be withdrawn, with nobody yet acting on it? | Credibility-reversible | Move to row 4 |
| 4 | Is the bearer of the undo cost identified, and does that bearer consent to carry it? | Actionable | Irreversible, even if rows 1 to 3 answered "yes" |
| 5 | Does a second confirmation point stand between the action and its effect? | One intervention slot remains | You are at the point of no return |

Row 4 carries a generational dimension. If the bearer of the undo cost appears years later, because the action belongs to this period and the cost to a successor or to the public, then that bearer's consent cannot be obtained at all. The row answers "no" by construction, and intergenerational costs are graded strictly. The reason is the billing logic of Chapter 28: an irreversible cost that crosses periods must be signed for across periods, and with no signature it is treated as irreversible. The exception is statutory authority, where law explicitly lets a decider of this period bind a future one. The boundary of that authority is written in the statute, and an intergenerational action that exceeds the boundary is graded strictly as well.

Reading the table: the count of "yes" answers in rows 1 to 3 gives the reversibility grade. All three present is reversible; two or more missing is irreversible. Row 4 carries a single-vote veto. Row 5 tells you whether one last intervention slot remains.

The grey zone needs a rule too. Two yes answers and one ambiguous answer: grade the ambiguous row strictly, which means counting it as a no. The reason is what the verdict is for. The verdict exists to set defences, and the two errors are not symmetric. Treating a half-reversible action as irreversible costs an extra confirmation. Treating an irreversible action as half-reversible costs a disaster. Strictness is the rational side of that asymmetry.

The exception to strictness is paralysis. Grade everything strictly and the organisation stops doing anything. Use a quota: a fixed number of lenient verdicts per year, with the list of lenient verdicts published. The quota lets strictness and action coexist, and publication keeps the leniency from becoming invisible.

The table has three uses, one per kind of person. The decider fills it in before acting; the moment row 5 answers "no" is the point of no return, and it goes into the decision record. The process designer places a second confirmation point ahead of every point of no return and sets that point's minimum information standard, which is the visible-at-the-time discipline of Chapter 6. The postmortem writer marks the point of no return on the failure chain and examines what happened at the last second-confirmation slot before it.

Examining that slot has a time dimension. The slot is designed to sit before the action, and in practice it may be moved after it: release first, signature later. A signature collected afterward is a pseudo-confirmation.

Detect it by comparing timestamps. If the confirmation record is timestamped later than the action, the confirmation is false.

When pseudo-confirmation is systematic, when a spot audit finds a backdating rate above twenty percent, the slot has ceased to exist in substance. Count a slot that exists only on paper as absent, which is the downgrade clause of Chapter 22: paper confirmations do not count toward the defence inventory.

Governing pseudo-confirmation requires a system lock. The confirmation action must be locked in the system before the action can complete, so a release cannot be generated at all until the lock has fired.

## 7.4 Points of No Return in the Cases

The 737-9 door plug (av-030, NTSB AIR-25-04) puts the point of no return on the final assembly line, at the moment the door closes and the aircraft passes into delivery and service. Before that position, one records check on work that opened a critical area, run by hand or forced by system, would have stopped everything downstream. After it, the risk entered the life ledger of the 176 people on board: 169 uninjured and 7 with minor injuries, figures taken from AIR-25-04. The currency of withdrawal switched from person-hours to lives.

The design question is therefore concrete. In the factory's release chain, was the information "this critical area was opened" entitled to trigger a mandatory re-check? At the time it triggered nothing, and that failure is the generating condition taken up in Chapter 29 (the invisibility of maintenance) and Chapter 15 (practical drift).

Alaska Airlines 261 (av-034, AAR-02/01) contributes a finer type: the irreversibility of an institutional decision, realised one installment at a time.

The approval to extend the check intervals, company application and FAA approval together, was itself reversible. The intervals could have been set back. From the day the approval took effect, every flight released under the new intervals was a micro point of no return: an aircraft flying with components no longer checked to the original standard. The failure did not occur on the day of approval. It cashed on one of dozens of successive releases.

A second sample of the installment type sits in Chapter 12's first case, the quarterly reversals. Each quarter's reversal was reversible in itself, since the accounts could be adjusted back. Five consecutive quarters of reversal locked the disclosure basis into a new normal, and a decision that was reversible once became structure through repetition.

Read the two cases together and the identification list completes itself. The decision can be withdrawn individually. Repetition requires no additional approval. The conditions of each execution match the last. No feedback loop carries the consequence back. An institutional decision that hits all four is an installment machine, and the moment to notice it is the second item: where repetition needs no approval, the machine has started turning.

Counting is how you recognise this type. The more exposures an institutional decision accumulates, the less negligible the cumulative probability of a single deviation, which is the arithmetic footing of the normalization described in Chapter 11.

Give the count an institutional form: an exposure ledger, with every realisation of an installment-type irreversibility recorded, its date, its object, and the state of deviation at the time.

The ledger has two readings: the cumulative count, which says how close you are to the accident probability in the statistical sense, and the rate of increase, which says whether exposure through this institutional decision is accelerating. Chapter 25's margin logic sets the alarm line: where exposure count multiplied by single-event probability approaches one, that is the place to stop.

The ledger belongs to the person signing the release, who adds one line as they sign. The cost of recording matches the cost of releasing.

The template is one line with four columns: date, object released, summary of deviation state, signature. Keep the template deliberately crude. Crudeness is a survival strategy, because a complex ledger has a very high probability of being abandoned within three months.

Electronically, fold the ledger into the release system, so the release action generates its own row at zero extra cost. A merged ledger produces something for free: an automatic count of releases, which gives the installment count a live reading for the first time.

Put that live reading on the signature screen. A number the signer sees at every release is a number the signer will consider.

The Haneda runway collision (av-037, JTSB Interim Report No. 2, 2025) puts the point of no return at the moment a combination forms: the Japan Coast Guard aircraft on the runway and the A350 holding a landing clearance.

This case's decision-point density is worth counting with this chapter's instrument. The decision points are the Coast Guard aircraft's confirmation before the stop line, the controller's issuance of the clearance, the A350 crew's acceptance of the clearance, and the tower's monitoring of runway occupancy. Four points strung on one chain, each of which held a chance to rewrite the ending.

The joint-failure structure, where a different action at any one point breaks the chain, is the series-dependency form of Chapter 33: decision points in series are as fatal as equipment in series.

The postmortem reading of a joint failure is to file the four failure modes separately, each point with its own visible-at-the-time and its own option set: four small cards rather than one large one, since a large card dilutes four responsibilities into a single sentence.

Past the point of no return, the option space left to the two aircraft was set by speed, distance and lighting. All the value of the postmortem sits before it: the confirmation at the stop line, the readback and comprehension of the control instruction, the cockpit's visual search for runway occupancy. The final report, unreleased as of this book's writing, should concentrate its hypothesis space on the same segment. Material at the interim-report stage carries grade A-minus, an independent investigation body's document that is not the final one, and the minus sign stays when citing it (on the use of evidence grades, Chapter 10).

This chapter is one of the load-bearing columns of the anatomy part. Chapter 6 supplied the discipline of the record; this chapter supplies the language of position. Every instrument in the chapters that follow installs onto these two words: a ledger mounts at a decision point, a checklist hangs in front of a point of no return, a drill is scheduled at a second confirmation slot. Position language is the grammar of the anatomy part, and a reader who holds it can find the standing place for every device in the four chapters that follow. The auditor of this chapter is the annual output-ledger review of Chapter 20, where the exposure ledger and the catch ledger are audited side by side: one counts how much you exposed, the other counts how much your defences caught.

## 7.5 Designing Your Own Points of No Return

Identification is the smaller half of this chapter. The output should be design moves. Four of them.

Move one: place a second confirmation slot ahead of every point of no return, and set an information threshold for the slot.

Aviation's release order is the most mature template. The checks required before signing are listed item by item on the form, and a signature declares that every item on the form has been verified. Every go-live, loan disbursement, product launch and public announcement in your organisation deserves an equivalent.

A general template for the equivalent takes five columns: the action, the irreversibility type (the verdict from the four currencies), the checklist (each item verifiable), the confirmer (named, with a named alternate), and the visible-at-the-time summary (three to five key items).

Grade the form by the risk of the action. A low-risk action merges the five columns into three. A high-risk action fills all five and attaches a timestamp.

Electronic versions should enforce the fields: no completion, no release button. It is the same mechanism as the release validation of Chapter 29.

Review the template annually against that year's accidents and near misses. Which columns proved redundant in real events, and which were missing. The review is how a release form evolves with the organisation that uses it.

Move two: give irreversible decisions a cooling-off period.

Judging whether an action is reversible costs far less than judging whether it is correct, and the gap widens for the third currency. Public announcements and public commitments wait one night, without exception.

Credibility has a property the other currencies lack: other people cash it in. Once an announcement is out, the cost of withdrawing it depends on how many people acted on it, and the more irreversible their actions (a move, an investment, a surgery scheduled), the more expensive the withdrawal.

Assess that population before the announcement. Who will do something irreversible because of this notice? Build a forecast list, which turns credibility assessment from a feeling into arithmetic.

Make the forecast compulsory. Add one step to the signing flow for credibility-class announcements: an impact forecast table, with an empty cell meaning the document goes back.

The forecast table also serves another chapter. It is the same table as the commitment tiers of Chapter 13: grading an external commitment and forecasting its credibility impact are one piece of homework, and one piece of homework can feed two chapters.

What the overnight rule filters out is not error. It is impulse.

Set the cooling period by currency tier: overnight at the signature tier, a week at the go-live tier, and at the credibility tier by impact, with longer cooling for wider impact.

Tiered cooling meets institutional friction from the business calendar, since the business side reads a cooling period as an efficiency tax. The justification for the tax is the cost of one withdrawal. A single withdrawal avoided by one night pays a year of the tax.

Enforce cooling in the system: build a time lock into the signing button for announcement-class documents, reusing the mechanism of Device 4 in Chapter 13 on the credibility currency. The virtue of a system lock is that cooling stops depending on memory, and impulse is exactly what memory fails to hold.

Move three: write the point of no return into the decision record.

Three lines: what moment the point of no return was, where the last second confirmation slot before it stood, and what information was visible at the time (the two columns of Chapter 6).

Three years on, this record is the only raw material a postmortem can still use.

Test the material for usability with a simulated postmortem. Take a decision record from three years ago, look at nothing else, and try to rebuild the table as it stood then from the record alone. The completeness of the reconstruction is the report card of the record-keeping system.

Run the simulation as an annual sample: three historical decision records a year, rebuilt as an exercise. The output is an improvement list for the record-keeping system, naming which class of missing information stalled the reconstruction.

Half a day a year buys the record system a real stress test. A record system with no stress test degrades quietly into form: the fields filled, the information gone.

Move four: the signer is the terminal of responsibility, and a chain of signers is the absence of responsibility.

This move carries a protection for the signer that has to be written alongside it. Information rights come with the halt right. If one person is accountable at a point of no return, that person needs access to everything before it and unilateral authority to stop the action. A signature post with mismatched authority and duty is a timer set inside the organisation.

The institutional form is a rights list for the signature post: the scope of information access, the halt procedure, and the immunity clause that applies after a halt. Three items, written into the job description.

Detect a missing item with one question to a candidate for the post: why did the last signer sign? A post that answers vaguely will produce the next vague signer.

Then write it into contracts, processes and job descriptions: who signs for this action's point of no return, and whether that signer holds unilateral authority to halt. Chapter 18 shows the case structure in which seven signers equal zero signers.

## Self-Check: Five Questions

1. Write down three points of no return in your project over the coming thirty days, and grade each one with the table in section 7.3.
2. What was your organisation's most recent case of money-reversibility that drifted into irreversibility? Was the drift done by the process or by a person?
3. Of the signatures you have signed, how many carried fields you did not verify item by item? Can you carry that ratio?
4. At the last second confirmation slot in your process, does the check examine the record or the physical thing? Do the two fail in the same way?
5. Does your organisation carry installment-type irreversibility, a reversible institutional decision accumulating exposure through every routine release? Can you put a number on the installments?

Beyond the five questions, a pocket version of this chapter: the three questions of the reversibility table, sized for a phone. Can I afford to undo it (money)? Is there still time (time)? Can I take it back in public (credibility)?

Each question takes a yes or a no, and the combination gives four states: fully reversible, half reversible, critical, irreversible. The action a state calls for is one sentence spoken to yourself: which of the four is this.

A week of practice installs the habit. Ask the three questions of one action a day, and at the end of the week the table has been internalised.

The test of internalisation is a falling misjudgment rate. Compare past verdicts against what later happened, and keep the statistic for yourself. Above thirty percent, go back to the table and learn it again.
