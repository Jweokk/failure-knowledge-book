# Chapter 1: Why Failure Knowledge Is the Most Expensive, and the Least Organized

> What this chapter covers: the price of failure knowledge and the supply of it, plus the distance between having heard of a way to die and knowing one.
> When to come back: when you need to persuade someone, yourself included, to spend hours and budget on organizing failure; when a postmortem lands on your desk and you have to judge whether it deserves reading.
> Reading time: about 30 minutes.

## 1.1 Eliminative Knowledge

An organization makes the same kind of decision every day. Fund this project or not, release this batch or hold it, sign this build or send it back, fill this shift or run short. Each decision takes one item out of a set and abandons the rest. Knowledge has exactly one job in that act: it makes the set smaller.

Two kinds of evidence shrink a set. A success case says this path works, and validates one point. A failure case says this region contains a path that kills, and removes an area. For the same amount of information, the second removes more.

Organizations do not decide once. They decide daily, on structurally similar questions, which is what makes negative samples worth counting. The expected value of one failure record equals the number of wrong paths it removes, multiplied by the expected loss on each of them. Losses run in a long tail: nearly every error produces damage the organization absorbs, and a small number erase it. The large losses cluster on a handful of paths. Those are the paths the investigation agencies write about, year after year.

Failure knowledge therefore wins on two counts at once. It removes more area, and the area it removes carries the largest losses. Together the two support a claim about single records: a failure record carries more information than a success record.

The claim has a premise, and the premise is this chapter's question. Knowledge, here, means organized failure records. Failure that has not been organized does not qualify as knowledge at all. The rest of this section sets the threshold.

The eliminative arithmetic has a boundary, and it should be drawn before the argument goes further. A negative sample removes a region only if the region already has a name. One failure record eliminates a whole area when the area sits inside a pattern library; outside one, the same record eliminates a single point, that company's practice in that year, and the value decays to nothing as circumstances change. This explains why a good failure story told across a dinner table changes no decisions at the table. It is a point, and there is no library to multiply it. It also explains the order of this book: twenty-four chapters build the library before a single page spends itself on cases. The situation table in Chapter 35 is the interface between that library and your own circumstances.

There is a second boundary. Elimination value multiplies by expected loss, and expected losses in the tail depend on data. Rare large losses are exactly the ones nobody estimates well. Appendix C therefore fixes the evidence grades and the reconstruction method, and any figure that cannot be estimated is left blank instead of being handed to a decision-maker with false precision. The two boundaries make the chapter's first promise: every eliminations figure discussed here is backed by a pattern library and by an evidence grade.

One further compression of the opening is worth making. If a decision is an act of elimination, then the unit of knowledge value is the area it cuts away, not the quality of the telling. On that view the twelve-field card is a measuring instrument. The fields are graduations, a card is a reading, a mechanism is the conversion table. The instrument view has a consequence for prose: this book does not chase narrative tension, because the reader should feel tension at the decision point and nowhere else. Its negative example is the narrativizing tradition in failure writing, where an accident is told as a mystery. Readers walk away remembering the protagonist and the reversal, and lose the conditions and the structure. That habit is the largest single drag on the circulation of failure knowledge, and it owns about half the cost counted in Section 1.5.

## 1.2 Is "I Know a Company That Died That Way" Knowledge?

The failure knowledge circulating at dinner and in meeting rooms usually sounds like this. I know a company that died exactly that way. So-and-so went hard at channel expansion and collapsed overnight. The person saying it often did watch a company die, and the information is not false. As knowledge it has three holes.

The first hole is in the fact layer. The sentence cannot be checked. Which company, which year, on the authority of what document, was there an investigation afterwards: all missing. The listener can believe it or not. Knowledge's first requirement is verifiability, meaning a third party can test the statement against source material. Without that layer the statement drops to rumor, and rumor has no part in decisions. It only sets a mood.

The second hole is in the condition layer. The sentence has no boundaries. It says nothing about the company's size at the time, its regulatory density, its cash structure, its market stage, so you cannot tell whether this way of dying applies to your project. Every failure conclusion has a range of validity, and knowledge used outside its range manufactures accidents on its own. Taking "internet companies must burn cash for scale" into a cash-flow business, or "manufacturing must be asset-heavy" into a contract-manufacturing model, are the same error twice. Failure knowledge with the condition layer missing sounds deeper the more damage it does, because it invites the listener to carry the conclusion straight across.

The third hole is in the structure layer. The sentence cannot be retrieved, accumulated or cited. Three months later you cannot reconstruct the full version; when a colleague needs it you cannot produce a source; it will not merge with similar records into a regularity. Knowledge enters organizational decisions only through a system: searchable, comparable, citable, contestable. All four verbs presume structure.

Set against that a failure record done seriously. A formal NTSB aviation accident report has four fixed parts: factual information, analysis, conclusions, and safety recommendations. The factual part carries a complete timeline, and every statement in it traces back through the investigation docket to physical evidence, recorded audio and interview transcripts. The conclusions part lists each finding and marks whether it is contributory or an observation. Safety recommendations are numbered one by one, and the recipient must answer in writing within 90 days. A reader may disagree with the analysis, but every step of checking has a route. That is what fielded means: fact, condition and structure, all three present.

A contrast makes the return on fielding concrete. For one and the same accident, the narrative version, in the register of a press release, has an information half-life measured in months: news is buried by news, and a source rots with its link. The fielded version, in the register of a formal report, has a half-life measured in decades: the document number enters an archive system, and paper and digital copies are held in parallel. The gap between the two half-lives is the compound interest of fielding. Only knowledge that can still be cited in ten years is entitled to enter an organization's long-term decisions, and equipment selection, process design and staff development are all ten-year decisions. One practical instruction for decision-makers: before any piece of failure knowledge enters your decision library, ask for its half-life. Anything with a shorter half-life than your decision cycle is background, not evidence.

Chapter 5 compresses this fielding into a twelve-field case card. Here only the standard matters: before discussing failure, check whether the material passes the three gates of this section.

The three gates become a short set of questions. Handed any failure narrative, ask three in order. Can I follow the source to the original text, and does that text carry a time, a place, an institution and a document number? Did the narrator state the scale, the regulation, the cash position and the market stage of that moment, or jump straight from the story to the lesson? Can the record be searched, compared and cited, which means does it carry a number that lives inside a system? An answer that fails sends the item down a grade. Downgrading is not deletion, it changes the use: a rumor is a lead, you follow the lead to primary material, and you upgrade one record at a time.

The gates have another side. Material that passes them has not thereby become a conclusion. A primary report supplies facts and conditions, and the lesson layer still requires the reader to walk the pattern library. The gates sort material by grade. They do not think on the reader's behalf.

One objection comes up often enough to answer here: does all this make dinner-table stories worthless? No. They serve two purposes. A story is a topic lead, telling you where a primary document worth finding might exist. It is also a thermometer for organizational culture, since the way a team retells failure exposes its habits of knowledge. What needs governing is the use of rumor as evidence, meaning a decision document with no source attached, not the story at the table. The gates route each narrative to the position that matches its evidence grade. Chapter 10 is the formal version of the three gates. Section 1.2 is the everyday one.

## 1.3 The Word "Replicate" Has Misled Everyone

The claim travels widely: success can be replicated, failure cannot. It points attention in the wrong direction. The accurate version runs like this. Success cannot be replicated. Failure can be avoided. Neither of them is replication.

People who learn from failure meet a third casualty of the replication myth. They get asked, in effect, whether failure cases are not all special, and whether their own project is not different. The objection is half right, and the halves need separating. The wrong half treats applicability as all or nothing; the value of the condition layer is precisely that it hands you a comparability checklist, and scoring that list item by item turns applicability into a measurement rather than a stance. The right half is a reminder that migration carries a tax, which the rest of this section and the domain pages in Part 4 pay. Once separated, the objection converts into a tool sentence: you say it is different, so name the condition that is different. A clause-level rebuttal returns the conversation to the field layer, where disagreement can be checked, instead of the position layer, where it can only be staked out. The sentence travels into review meetings, project approvals and postmortems, and it is the three gates in portable form.

Replicating a success requires the conditions to replay. The list runs: timing, team composition, competitive picture, regulatory window, funding environment, and a quantity of luck the people involved cannot articulate. Business school cases compress success into a three-part story, vision, execution, result, and what the compression removes is exactly what replication needs. Following success cases therefore tends to raise the failure rate rather than lower it, and when you fail nobody writes your postmortem. That is the survivorship bias of Chapter 2.

The reusable part of failure knowledge is far more stable. A lethal path usually puts broader requirements on initial conditions than a success path does, and the reason is structural. Each link in a failure chain acts as a filter, so organizations that pass every filter and arrive at the same ending can start from wildly different states. Goal displacement appears with nearly identical structure in hospital waiting-time targets, school progression-rate targets and platform content targets. Success runs the other way. Every success path rests on a narrow set of conditions.

So failure knowledge exists naturally as patterns, not as cases. Patterns migrate across industries; cases are reference material. This is why Part 3 is built as twenty-four mechanism chapters with four cases each, drawn from four different industries: the definition of a mechanism requires it to hold across industries. Something that appears in only one industry is not a mechanism. It is an industry characteristic, and it belongs in the domain pages of Part 4.

Migrating a pattern carries a tax called adaptation cost. When a mechanism in the shape it takes in industry A enters industry B, every parameter has to be re-labeled. The decay rate in Chapter 16 is not the same number in an organization with two reporting layers as in one with six. There are two ways to pay. You can label the parameters yourself while reading cases from the home industry, which costs time and carries error, or you can read a book that has already labeled them, which is what Part 4 and the mechanism-by-domain matrix in Appendix A supply. The labeling has to be honest about its limits. Each cell of that matrix answers presence only, meaning a reliable case exists for the mechanism in that domain. It says nothing about strength, which varies by organization and which this book does not pretend to deliver as an industry manual. The right use is three steps. Check the cell to confirm the mechanism is present, go to the mechanism chapter for the criteria, then plug in your own parameters. Those three steps pay most of the tax.

What remains sits in semantics. A mechanism's name carries the smell of its home industry when it crosses over: goal displacement is called compliance-target assessment in healthcare and exam-focused teaching in education, and the smell persuades readers they are looking at a different mechanism. The deodorant is the criteria sentence in each mechanism chapter. All three criteria per chapter are deliberately written in industry-neutral language, because criteria are a mechanism's fingerprint and fingerprints do not smell of an industry. When you migrate across industries, hold on to the fingerprint. If it matches, the industry nouns can be swapped freely. If it does not, a familiar name does not make it the same mechanism. That principle is the user manual for the Appendix A matrix, and it marks the line between this book and industry lore: lore collects smells, this book collects fingerprints.

## 1.4 Who Produces Failure Knowledge

If failure records were worth this much, the market should have produced them already. The producer list explains why it has not.

Statutory investigation bodies come first. Aviation has the NTSB, the UK AAIB, France's BEA and Japan's JTSB. Chemical and process industries have the US CSB. Nuclear has the IAEA incident reporting system. Maritime has the UK MAIB. Their output is steady, free and citable, which makes them the largest legitimate supply of failure knowledge. It arrives as legal documents: several hundred pages, dense with technical terminology, written for hearings and litigation, with very little of it written for reading.

Insurance and reinsurance companies hold the largest failure datasets anywhere, spanning industries and years, with loss amounts attached. Those datasets are assets for pricing cover, and they are closed. You cannot buy them on an open market. You pay for them inside your premium.

Judicial and regulatory documents make up the third group: judgments, penalty decisions, recall notices, public and steadily growing, with China Judgments Online having once offered the largest disclosure surface in the world. Their weakness is fragmentation. One document covers one slice of one dispute, and nobody maintains a data structure across documents.

Academia comes fourth. Vaughan's normalization of deviance, Perrow's normal accidents, Snook's practical drift, Dekker's drift into failure: Part 3 takes its mechanism names from this line of work. But academic theory arrives wrapped in cases, the cases wrapped in second-hand retellings, and the citation chain back to the source frequently ends at a link that will not open. The theory is reusable. The cases cannot be checked.

The organizations involved come last, and produce least, for the hardest reasons. Writing failure in detail carries legal risk, hands intelligence to competitors, and costs employees and founders their reputations. All three point toward silence. Chapter 4 takes each of them apart.

Lay the five side by side and a mismatch appears. The largest producer of failure knowledge is a body of documents that its largest potential consumer group happens to be unable to read. Investigation agencies have written for decades. Business schools scarcely cite them. Corporate training does not use them. Decision-makers do not recall them at the moment of choosing. Production is not the shortage. Circulation is.

The reverse face of the circulation gap is a misalignment of producer incentives. An investigation agency's best output is procedural completeness, which survives litigation, rather than a format that travels well. An insurer's best output is pricing precision, not industry education. A party to the event has one best output, which is not writing. Three optima add up to a structural blank, since every side is rationally declining to do the circulating. A structural blank admits only structural fixes: an independent identity for whoever circulates, belonging to none of the parties, which is where this book places itself; a stable unit of account for what gets circulated, which is the twelve-field card; and a checkable base, which is the links in Appendix B. None of the three depends on anyone's goodwill changing. They ask one thing: that somebody stand between upstream production and downstream consumption and translate between the two formats. The scarcity of translators is the field's central fact, and imitation is what this book would most like to provoke.

The entry cost of imitating it can be estimated for you. A first edition of one industry's card library, at this book's specification, is fifty cards, which is one knowledgeable part-timer for a year at a card a week. Fifty cards cover an industry's high-frequency ways of dying, following Pareto: most losses concentrate in a few patterns, and that coverage is enough to run the situation table in Chapter 35. The other end of the budget is maintenance, thirty percent refreshed each year plus ten cards from new reports. Maintenance cost is the real threshold, because the excitement of building is universal and the patience to renew is scarce. Chapter 39 therefore puts maintenance in its own line in the budget template and ties it to output, where each new card is matched against the decisions it blocked that year. Paying for maintenance out of output is the only way a card library reaches its third year.

## 1.5 Circulation Stops at the Last Mile

Take a real NTSB formal report and watch where circulation breaks. It may be a three-hundred-page PDF in English, sitting on the fourth level of a database, written in airworthiness terminology, with three numbering systems running in parallel: accident number, recommendation number, docket number. The density is high. Every page carries timestamped communications, instrument readings, signed documents. For an engineer about to make a release decision, that report holds everything he needs except three things. He does not know the file exists. He does not know which file matches his situation. He cannot finish three hundred pages.

Three unknowns and one impossibility make up the whole of circulation cost: discovery, matching, reading. Once those costs pass a certain level, the effective supply of a public good falls to zero, however free it is on paper.

In the world as it stands, the circulation cost of success narratives has been driven down industrially. Media, biographies, podcasts, business school texts, trending feeds: somebody pays at every stage. Nobody pays for the circulation of failure records. An investigation agency publishes and stops there. Citing the report earns no academic citation, retelling it earns no traffic, and using it requires understanding it first. This is market failure in its standard shape, applied to an obscure commodity.

The obscurity has a secondary disaster worth naming. The blank in circulation gets filled by poor supply: bestseller narratives built on survivorship bias, success literature repackaged backwards, social media posts that turn an accident into a tragedy of character. The crowding-out is real, and it works on appetite. An organization that reads three bad books on failure concludes that the field holds nothing serious, and its budget for the subject is spent before a serious title appears. This book's answer is not to criticize the neighbors, since criticism cannot be checked, but to demonstrate seriousness in public: twelve fields, four-part provenance, links, stated gaps. Each of those is verification waiting to happen. Verifiable seriousness obeys a harsh rule. It spreads ten times slower than the other kind, and every copy it leaves behind can be checked. Slow is the right speed here, because the object is a decision, not traffic.

Look at one piece of failure knowledge that has completed circulation. ValuJet 592, case av-035 in Appendix B. The NTSB formal report, AAR-97/06, spends one page pinning the physical origin: a maintenance contractor loaded chemical oxygen generators that had not been expended into the cargo hold as ordinary spares. It spends several hundred pages establishing the condition layer: the gap in dangerous-goods rules for the category "maintenance residue", the handover habits of the contractor system, the regulator's handling of earlier fires of the same kind. The pattern, the invisibility of maintenance, Chapter 29, turns up again in warehousing, in chemical processing, in software configuration management. The card's value is that it pins how a maintenance by-product becomes a dangerous good to a number you can cite. Circulation engineering is the work of repeating that operation across more reports.

The supply side also admits an incentive design. Price the identity of a primary-material reader, because reading a formal report is a skill: airworthiness terminology, the investigation framework, and a pattern library, three layers of training. The other side of pricing is certification. If an industry has a few dozen recognized report readers, examined by interpreting a new case and judged by peers, then the translation capacity behind circulation has a human base. Certification costs the building of an exam system and returns compounding capacity, since each certified reader can produce several dozen qualified cards a year. The format in this book, twelve fields, evidence grades, the three stop-point questions, can serve directly as the syllabus: public syllabus, transparent standard, verifiable work, and the profession grows on its own.

Repeating the operation across more reports requires deciding what the product is. Three forms are available, with a division of labor. Form one is the card library, which is the body of this book: twelve fields, numbers, links, built for retrieval and citation. Form two is the bulletin stream, a monthly digest of new reports in a field, which keeps an organization in contact with the material; contact is the precondition of use, and most organizations abstain not from unwillingness but from not knowing there is new stock. Form three is the morning case, ten minutes once a week on a single card, stopping at the decision point, letting the team judge, then releasing the outcome, which presses circulation into the room where decisions happen. The three differ in cost and return: the library is dearest and accumulates, the bulletin is cheapest and breaks easily, the morning case is closest to the floor and depends on a host who keeps at it. An organization picks what its budget allows. What this book can supply directly is form one, 158 cards in Appendix B, and the raw material for form two in the retrieval handbooks of the domain pages. Scripts for form three are ready in Chapters 35 and 39.

## 1.6 Where This Book Sits

This book does not investigate. It does circulation engineering, in three moves. Compression: several hundred pages become a twelve-field case card that keeps the fact layer, the condition layer and the structure layer. Matching: cases hang from twenty-four mechanisms, so a reader starting from their own position, with the table in Chapter 35, can pull the list of ways of dying that applies. Delayed conclusion: the case stops at the decision point, the reader judges, and only then does the outcome arrive with the minimum counterfactual.

The third move is where this book separates from retelling. Retelling hands out conclusions: so-and-so failed through arrogance, so-and-so succeeded through humility. A conclusion of that kind cannot be reviewed and cannot enter a decision. What this book hands over is a slice of process. On a given date, in a given room, on a given document, these were the facts available, these were the options, and this is what the people did. The judgment stays with the reader, and the structural analysis is already written. That is the specific remedy for the attribution bias of Chapter 2.

Circulation engineering has edges. Compression loses information, so every case keeps its citation and its link and the reader can walk the number back to the original report. Compression can also introduce error, so every number needs a four-part source, and whatever cannot supply one is written as mechanism description. What this book submits is a traceable middle layer. Its value depends on how hard it is to check, not on how well it is written.

The last layer of checking is an admission that the book can be wrong. Any card in the appendices may differ from its source, since compression always loses, transcription always risks, and links always rot. The institutional reply to that is errata: somewhere to report the discrepancy, a record of the report, and that record published with the same weight as the text it corrects. A correction is credit rather than disgrace, because a long errata list means the material has been checked often. A system that welcomes correction accumulates trust faster than a declaration of zero defects, and that accumulated trust is what lets a body of knowledge like this one be finished at all.

The loop closes with a channel running the other way, for checking the book itself. The self-audit machinery is in the standards directory, and three entry points are open to any reader. Appendix B links each card, so the original text opens from the page and you can test the citation against the body yourself. Every number carries its four-part provenance, who measured it, when, on what sample, under what definition, and anything that cannot answer all four is marked pending or blocked. The gaps are stated plainly: Chapters 26, 28 and 34 each record what was not collected, why, and where collection should go next. With those three entry points, the right posture toward this book is neither belief nor disbelief. Pull three cards and check them. Only a book that can be checked deserves a place in a decision process, which is Section 1.2's standard applied to this book.

## Five Self-Checks

1. Did your organization's last major loss leave a primary document anyone can retrieve? Ask three colleagues, write the three answers down, and compare them.
2. The last time you retold a case of failure, which source document had you read it in? If you cannot name the document, which category in Section 1.2 does that retelling fall into?
3. For the success cases your team consults before deciding, can you list the three narrow conditions that case required? If you cannot, what does that tell you?
4. Of the three circulation costs, discovery, matching and reading, which is highest in your organization? Is anyone working to lower it?
5. Given a budget for organizing your industry's failure knowledge, what would you build first? How does that answer connect to the situation table in Chapter 35?

Use the five together, once. The first checks inventory, whether the organization has any stock of failure knowledge at all. The second checks citation, whether the team's language carries sources. The third checks conditions, the real cost of the success cases you measure yourself against. The fourth checks circulation, which of the three costs is jammed. The fifth checks budget, what you would build if the money were real. Write the answers on one page, and that page is a map of where your organization stands in relation to failure knowledge. The next step from the map, what to build, how, and who maintains it, is the subject of Part 5, Chapters 35 to 39. The division of labor between this chapter and Part 5 follows from that. The meta-question chapters exist to convince you the work is worth doing, and the usage part exists to make it doable. The bridge between them is the situation table in Chapter 35, which pulls the cards you should read straight out of your own circumstances. If you plan to jump from here to Part 5, remember the bridge is there: between the argument and the tool sits an afternoon in which you already started.

That afternoon can begin with the smallest possible item. Take the most instructive thing that happened to your team last year and write it as one page through the three gates of Section 1.2: three lines of fact, three lines of condition, three lines of structure, meaning source, number, retrievability. The page will most likely fail the gates, because most internal postmortems are missing the condition layer and the structure layer. Failing is a baseline rather than a failure. The distance between that baseline and a card in this book is the distance between your organization and a world that records failure seriously. Part 5 holds the plan for closing it. This chapter promises only one thing: the distance can be quantified, in how many lines are missing, whether the source is missing, whether the number is missing. A quantified distance is a distance someone can shorten.
