    Chapter 34: Path Lock-in (David; Arthur)

    What this chapter covers: how an early accidental choice grows into a structure you cannot leave; the classic argument from David and Arthur, the criteria, two field sites with the evidence gap stated, and how to run a from-zero derivation. When to turn back: at a selection review, in a standards fight, and any time "we can't afford to switch" has become the premise of the discussion. Reading time: 45 minutes.

    Path lock-in is the only mechanism in this book that produces no accident. There are no casualties, no penalty and no investigation report. The cost is the sum of every alternative that did not happen. Paul David used the century-long history of the QWERTY keyboard to show how a layout designed to stop typebars jamming went on to rule electronic keyboards a hundred years after jamming itself had disappeared (David, "Clio and the Economics of QWERTY", American Economic Review 75(2): 332–337, 1985). Brian Arthur proved the generality of the pattern with a mathematical model: increasing returns let the early leader's advantage compound with time (Arthur, "Competing Technologies, Increasing Returns, and Lock-In by Historical Events", Economic Journal 99(394): 116–131, 1989, Arthur's bibliographic record verified in Crossref). Lock-in is not the same as error. The locked path usually performs well. What has gone is the capacity to re-evaluate it.

    The QWERTY story is worth telling in full, because it shows every part of a lock. Typewriter mechanics in the 1870s imposed a physical constraint: adjacent typebars jammed when struck in fast succession, and the Sholes layout spread the common letters apart on purpose. The constraint disappeared decades later as the mechanism improved. By then the layout had grown its own locking parts. A typist's muscle memory was an investment in human capital. The typing curriculum was an institutional investment. The keyboard maker's tooling was a fixed investment. The electronic keyboard inherited the entire compatibility requirement. The parts list maps onto this chapter's generating conditions one for one: learning cost, complementary investment, network effects, institutional fit. The lesson sits at the end of the story. No single part was a mistake, and the lock needed nobody to make an error. It needed time and inertia. That is where this chapter differs in temperament from the rest of the book. Other mechanisms have a perpetrator. Lock-in has only a calendar.

    The taxonomy of network effects is worth completing, because naming the type is the first step in judging how hard a lock will be to break. Direct effects: the more users, the more useful, as in communication protocols and social platforms. Indirect effects: the more complements, the more useful, as in application ecosystems and accessory markets. Data effects: the more use, the more accurate, as in algorithms and maps. Social effects: using it is the career-safe default, as in a job seeker's skill choice or a firm's me-too procurement. Difficulty rises across the four. A direct effect yields to a standard converter, an indirect effect to a compatibility layer, a data effect to migration tools. The social effect is hardest, because what it holds is the risk calculation in people's heads rather than the system. Device 4, the alternative archive, records them differently for that reason. The first three take technical parameters and price. The fourth takes the adopter list, meaning who uses what, and the adopter list is the only measure of a social effect.

    34.1 What It Is

    Definition: path lock-in means an early choice, often accidental, that reinforces itself over time through network effects, learning costs, complementary investment and institutional fit, until the feasible space for alternatives contracts and closes.

    Three start criteria. Any one of them means the mechanism is already running.

    ① Alternatives no longer appear in the selection discussion. They were not rejected. Nobody raised them.

    ② Migration cost only rises and is never re-estimated. Every "we can't afford to switch" cites a figure from years ago.

    ③ "We have always used this" has been promoted from a statement of fact to a statement of reasons.

    The three differ in how loudly they announce themselves. Criterion ① is silent, since a missing agenda item is invisible by construction. Criterion ② is written, and leaves a trail of estimate dates. Criterion ③ is spoken, usually in a meeting where nobody writes it down. Of the three, ② is the only one that can be audited from a filing cabinet.

    34.2 How It Grows

    Three generating conditions.

    Condition one: increasing returns. The more it is used, the better it works, through ecosystem, plug-ins, the talent pool and compatible parts, so the leader's advantage compounds with time. This is Arthur's core argument.

    Condition two: complementary investment hardens. Training, tools, contracts and downstream interfaces built around the early choice each act as a hidden tax on migration. Individually none is large. Added together they are a cost wall.

    Condition three: the evaluation capacity atrophies. Years without a selection exercise let the muscle for it waste, meaning the market scan and the alternative file. When the capability is finally needed, the organization can no longer assess, and leans further toward not switching.

    The three feed each other. What gets locked is the organization's assessment function, not the system.

    The atrophy has a ledger of its own, and the ledger explains why nobody notices. The true cost of lock-in is opportunity cost, the return the alternative would have produced, and opportunity cost is not booked under any accounting standard, because accounting records what happened. An unbooked opportunity cost leaves the locked balance sheet permanently presentable: no default, no impairment, no penalty. A presentable statement then argues for staying, since a clean sheet offers no grounds for treatment. The deeper purpose of Device 2, migration cost re-estimated, is to write opportunity cost down as a number for the first time. The re-estimate covers what migration would cost and also what staying forgoes each year, drawn from the annual improvement list of the alternatives, with Device 4's file as the data source. Set the one-off migration cost beside the annual opportunity cost of staying and the decision is complete for the first time. A complete decision does not have to choose migration, and lock-in often remains the better answer. What a complete decision ends is the state of no decision. Most organizations did not choose to stay. They never did the arithmetic.

    There is a follow-on action for organizations that have done it: book the returns of lock-in as well, which is where the rationality argument in 34.6 lands. Those returns stay out of the accounts too, the stability dividend of a settled ecosystem, the efficiency of deep optimization, the inspection-free cost of compatibility. Write them into an annual list of what staying saved this year, set that list beside the opportunity cost list, and lock-in becomes an account with income and expenditure instead of an atmosphere. The difference shows on the review date. An account with books goes through an income statement once a year: benefit still there, renew; benefit gone, discuss migration. An account without books survives on feeling, and the life of the feeling is the life of the organization.

    34.3 Field Sites

    Case 1 (Public): A Railway Built for 360 and Opened at 320

    Case ID: po-004.

    When and where: October 2023 to June 2026. London, at the Department for Transport, and Birmingham, at HS2 Limited; the sites are Old Oak Common, Curzon Street and Euston, with tunnelling, viaduct and earthworks on the route between London and Birmingham. The report records decisions to the month, not to the day.

    What and who: the Department for Transport (DfT), the project's funder and sponsor; HS2 Limited, a non-departmental public body of the DfT, responsible for design, construction and delivery of the railway into use (p.16); Euston Delivery Company Limited, set up by the DfT in April 2026 to take over the London terminus and the related works (p.12). Phase One civils are delivered by four joint ventures, each holding a section (p.29). Responsibility is recorded by post: the Secretary of State for Transport, the Permanent Secretary at the DfT, the chief executive of HS2 Limited.

    What they were doing: from January 2025 the DfT and HS2 Limited were running the third reset, with four workstreams in parallel (cost and schedule; supply chain and commercial; organizational capability; governance) and a planned close in spring 2027. The reset carries a budget of £153 million, of which £101 million had been spent by the end of March 2026 (p.18). Of the 124 recommendations accepted from external reviews, 45 were complete by February 2026, 36 per cent, with the rest scheduled to close with the reset (p.21). Construction did not pause. The Colne Valley viaduct was finished, and tunnelling towards Euston began in early 2026 (p.18). The cost estimate was rebuilt from the ground: the project split into 21 sub-projects, each checked for scope, schedule, cost and risk, then run through scenario tests to produce a range (p.25). Contracts were in negotiation. Talks with the four civils joint ventures, begun in spring 2024, had failed; in April 2025 the approach changed to settling historic accounts first and amending future terms after that, with a target of autumn 2026, about six months later than planned (pp.29–30). In January 2026 the Secretary of State asked HS2 Limited to assess what a lower speed would save (p.25). In March 2026 the DfT appointed a delivery agent for the disposal of Phase Two land: 560 properties, of which about 350 fall under the Crichel Down rules and must first be offered back to former owners (p.47). At Euston, £3.8 billion had been spent, of which £1.3 billion on land and compensation and £1.5 billion on tunnels and the approach lines, with £4.1 billion more required under the current design (p.44). On Phase Two, land and property had cost £643 million, covering 1,256 properties across 18 square kilometres, and several purchases were still being completed in March 2026 (p.46).

    The decision point:

    ① October 2023: the government cancels the whole of Phase Two, the 2a and 2b western and eastern legs together (p.17, figure 1, note 2). The report says only "the government" and records neither a signed document nor a signatory. The cut turned Phase One from the first section of a line to the north into a spur that ends at Birmingham: trains must descend to the existing West Coast Main Line north of Birmingham (p.31), and that line's platform dimensions were fixed long before HS2 existed. The report's section records the cancellation alone, with no review that day of the train contract, the platform design or signalling compatibility; the next record on train formation comes two and a half years later (p.31).

    ② May 2026: the DfT agrees three scope changes. Maximum speed falls from 360 to 320 km/h, automatic train operation is deleted, and signalling drops to a version already in use elsewhere (pp.25–26). In the same month the Permanent Secretary publishes the Accounting Officer Assessment, with a benefit-cost ratio of 1.5 to 6.4 (p.26). The report names neither the meeting that took the scope decision nor the signatory. The shape of the decision needs to be read closely. The civils were completed to the original specification: tunnel cross-section, track spacing and structures all built as designed for 360 km/h. Everything deleted sits at the system level (p.25). What could be deleted was deleted, and what could not be deleted was kept. The report gives one further reason worth quoting: testing 360 km/h itself takes time and money, and the test might prove the speed infeasible (p.26). Speed therefore moved from an engineering question to an operating parameter. The decision carries a real benefit too, in a lighter test burden and an opening at least a year earlier (p.26). The benefit is genuine, and it lands entirely in the layer that could be deleted.

    ③ 2026: the final decision on train formation and fleet size is deferred to 2028 (p.31). This is not the absence of a decision. It moves the decision to a year when the platforms have already been poured. Civils at Curzon Street and Old Oak Common are advancing, and their costs have doubled, from £600 million to £1.3 billion and from £2 billion to £4.3 billion (p.31). The report's reason for the deferral is practical: decide the formation in 2028 and the supply chain can still adjust (p.31). The platform is not in the supply chain. It is in the civils.

    ④ April 2026: Euston is separated from the main line and handed to a newly created delivery company, which the DfT calls a "single accountable body" (p.12). At that point the company was not yet formally operating and its chief executive was still acting (p.12). The interface between station and line turned from coordination inside one organization into a contract between two.

    What was visible then: before May 2026, the following was on the table. The 2020 cost estimate of £35 billion to £45 billion at 2019 prices (p.22). The NAO report of July 2024 and the Stewart Review of June 2025, the latter finding the governance structure not fit for purpose (p.35). The DfT's April 2024 notice to HS2 Limited that it was in breach of its delivery obligations (p.20, figure 3, note 2). The four civils joint venture contracts and their change orders, with cost verification backlogs averaging 21 months and covering close to £5 billion (p.30). The train contract signed in 2021, with formation ordered as two 200-metre units (p.31). The recorded dimensions of the existing West Coast Main Line platforms (p.31). The list of 1,256 properties and 18 square kilometres of land already bought (p.46). The location map of 550 boreholes awaiting backfill (p.48). And the new estimate from May 2026 of £87.7 billion to £102.7 billion, against which HS2 Limited says it is aiming for £93.2 billion (p.22). The process that produced that estimate was on the table as well: 21 sub-projects verified one by one, spot-checked by an independent expert panel that included the Infrastructure and Projects Authority, with the DfT itself writing that the figures still carry high uncertainty (p.25).

    What was learned afterwards: cancelling Phase One would cost £33 billion to £58 billion (p.27), the same order of magnitude as completing it and more than four times the 2023 estimate (p.28); had today's cost been known in 2020, the benefit-cost ratio would be 0.3 to 0.4 (p.27); full opening falls between May 2040 and December 2043 (p.23); the new trains are slower than the incumbent Pendolino on some sections (p.31); a major decision passes five serial approval gates and can take up to nine months (p.36).

    Stop here. Decide before you turn the page.

    You are in that seat now, with the materials above in front of you.

    1. What would you do? Write the action, not "pay more attention".
    2. Which item here are you most likely to miss, and why does it look unimportant?
    3. If you may set one checkpoint, when is it and what does it check?

    What happened: Phase One continues. The cost range is £87.7 billion to £102.7 billion, with a target of £93.2 billion; £46.8 billion had been spent by the end of March 2026, of which £2.6 billion went on the cancelled Phase Two (p.22). Passenger service from Old Oak Common to Birmingham begins between May 2036 and October 2039; the full Euston line opens between May 2040 and December 2043 (p.23). The lower speed and the deleted automatic operation are expected to save £1 billion to £2.5 billion, at a cost of about £1.3 billion in long-term benefits (p.26). Back to the three questions. The most common answer to the first is "stop and recalculate" or "re-tender". In this case, stopping is priced at £33 billion to £58 billion (p.27), and the report records no assessment of re-tendering at all, only the sentence that the remaining time on the contracts is reducing and the opportunity to avoid risk and save cost is receding quickly (p.30). The cost of re-tendering is a tender document nobody has written. The price of stopping includes site restoration and termination compensation (p.26, p.27). The item most easily missed in the second is the 2021 train contract: signed before Phase Two was cancelled, formation ordered as two 200-metre units, and the platforms it will stop at broke ground three years later (p.31). It lives in the contract file, not on the construction programme, so every review sees progress and never sees it. The checkpoint in the third lands on the week of October 2023: when Phase Two was cancelled, did anyone change the train contract at the same time.

    Options ruled out: cancel the whole project, which was costed, with the restoration and termination fees folded into the numerator as avoided cost, pushing the benefit-cost ratio up to 1.5 to 6.4 (p.26, p.28), and rejected on price. Run the line at 360 km/h to the end, which requires testing whether trains and track can run safely at that speed, a test that costs time and money and may return a negative (p.26). Change the trains to single 200-metre units, which gives less capacity than the incumbent West Coast Main Line trains and cuts project benefits with it (p.31). Re-tender the civils, for which the report records no assessment and leaves only the sentence about contract time running out (p.30). Keep Euston inside the main-line reset, which was not done: it was separated into an independent delivery body in April 2026, with no assessment of a combined option recorded (p.12). Replace signalling and train control together, where the deletion of automatic operation left signalling at a version used elsewhere, and the report records no in-house or new-version option (p.26).

    Early signals: ① the 2020 estimate of £35 billion to £45 billion against a 2024 funding envelope of £44.6 billion, with both the DfT and HS2 Limited accepting that delivery inside the envelope was not possible (p.22); the number at approval and the judgement at execution parted company in year four. Overridden because the reset was under way. ② October 2023, Phase Two cancelled and the 2021 train contract left as it was, formation unchanged (p.31); the contract fixes car length, platform length follows from it, and the platforms had not started. Overridden because the contract was signed and the platforms not yet built. ③ spring 2024, the renegotiation fails: HS2 Limited did not understand costs already incurred, had no reliable total cost estimate, and the transfer of risk to the joint ventures was not accepted (p.29). Overridden because it was handled as a commercial problem. ④ a cost verification backlog averaging 21 months and close to £5 billion, with single contract verification periods running from 6 to 60 months (p.30). Overridden because accounts can be caught up later. ⑤ both stations doubling in cost, which the report attributes to immature design, underestimated complexity, site constraints and re-planning (p.31). Overridden because it read as a delivery efficiency problem.

    The point of escalation: the start of civils. HS2 Limited's own March 2025 assessment uses the words "construction works having commenced too soon", and the same passage records that the contracts "have not delivered the expected performance" (p.18). The report names neither the start date nor the approval document. From the day work began, alignment, tunnel cross-section, track spacing, longitudinal gradient and platform length left the drawing and entered concrete. Those five can afterwards only be accommodated, never re-chosen, and the space of alternatives contracts from how the whole network connects to how this one work site gets modified. Chapter 27 handles every later decision to continue. This chapter handles the start itself. The money it spent was not the largest item in the project. What it spent was the option set.

    Failure chain: technical layer: the civils were completed to the 360 km/h specification and are operated at 320; automatic operation was deleted and signalling dropped to a version used elsewhere (pp.25–26); the platforms are designed for two 200-metre units, the existing line's platforms cannot take them, and a single unit carries less than the incumbent trains (p.31); the new trains are slower than the Pendolino on some sections (p.31). All four point the same way: the changeable part was made smaller and the unchangeable part was finished. The report's phrase for the civils is that they were completed to the original specification supporting 360 km/h trains (p.25), so the cross-section, track spacing and structural margin reserved for that speed stay in the concrete and go unused in service. Organizational layer: there was no reliable total cost estimate in 2024, so the renegotiation had no floor price (p.29); the verification backlog ran 21 months and close to £5 billion (p.30); the independent panel recommended filling the systems integration capability gap early, because that function holds every interface in the project (p.33); responsibility is split between the DfT, HS2 Limited and the new delivery company, and the station-to-line interface became a contract (p.12); major decisions pass five serial approval gates and can take up to nine months (p.36). Incentive layer: the price of cancelling is the same order as the price of finishing, so continuing is the cheaper side (p.28); the remaining term on the civils contracts keeps shrinking and the bargaining window is closing (p.30); exit also carries land restoration, with part of the 1,256 properties to be disposed of and 550 boreholes to be backfilled between December 2027 and March 2028 (pp.46–48). The break sits between the organizational and the incentive layers: the organizational layer lost the capacity to reprice, so nobody was left who could change the price structure in the incentive layer.

    Minimum counterfactual: in the week of October 2023, when the cancellation of Phase Two was announced, send a change notice to the 2021 train contract, changing "two 200-metre units" to "formation to be determined, platforms designed in parallel for single and double units". One action, taken before platform construction starts at the two stations, costing one contract variation and two sets of platform drawings. It changes nothing about the cancellation of Phase Two and requires nobody to forecast demand. It takes the formation decision back out of the concrete. Per the report, the formation decision is made in 2028 while the platform design is fixed in 2026 (p.31). The window for that change notice is a few months: once the platforms are poured, parallel design collapses into a choice between two.

    Outcome and sources: Phase One continues, with a cost ceiling of £102.7 billion; Phase Two is cancelled, leaving 1,256 properties and 550 boreholes awaiting backfill on the books. One NAO recommendation faces this chapter's interface directly: when the trains are specified, they should be placed in the future operation of the whole network, with seat capacity assured (p.15). Primary source: Comptroller and Auditor General, High Speed Two reset, Session 2026-27, HC 52, National Audit Office, published 29 June 2026 (signed 22 June 2026), 56 pages, ISBN 978-1-78604-684-0; page numbers cited here are PDF pages. The link is in Appendix B under po-004.

    Case one's lock has a further anatomy in the time dimension. The window shrinks with each project stage: at route selection the space of alternatives is network-wide, at land acquisition it contracts to the corridor, after the start of works to the work site, and after opening to operations alone. The engineering reason for the shrinkage is the materialization of input, since money turned into concrete is no longer money. The governance inference follows: in a large project the substitution assessment has to be moved ahead of materialization, and the from-zero derivation at feasibility is the only cheap window, because asking the from-zero question after opening gets a political answer. Front-loaded window management is therefore the core technique of large-project governance. Every stage review should label the substitution space of that stage, whether network, corridor or work site, and the width of that space is the length gauge for the review's agenda: at a stage where only the work site can still move, the review should no longer be discussing the route. The label costs one line in the review template. What it prevents is the mistimed debate, since arguing about the route at a stage when only a work site can be adjusted consumes all the patience an organization has.

    The product history in case two (mn-006) carries a consumer-goods detail worth writing out. Two locks hold case two in place. The user's lock is the spare-part buying habit and the memory of how a new carafe fits the pot. The firm's lock is tooling, suppliers and brand consistency. Each reinforces the other, and fifty-two years of two-way reinforcement turned the object of a material substitution assessment from one material into an ecosystem: changing the material means changing the spare-part supply chain, which means changing what users believe the product is. Consumer goods usually break a lock by running a second line rather than by substituting: old and new material side by side, and the market votes with its feet. A parallel line costs double inventory across two supply chains, and its obstacle is channel objection, since retailers will not clear shelf space for a trial line. The true cost of running both, trial-line inventory and shelf cost against the expected loss from the hidden risk, almost never appears on an internal statement, because the two ledgers belong to procurement and to brand and no single sheet puts them together. Device 2's pricing table is used across departments here: putting the two ledgers on one sheet is the step that comes before deciding whether to change the material.

    Case three (av-029) gains a regulatory-document view. The two pages of a safety bulletin are the regulator's standard action on a design inheritance, since it cannot mandate a modification and can only alert the industry, and the effect of the alert depends on operators' filing habits, meaning whether an old bulletin gets pulled when a new aircraft of the same configuration is certified. Civil aviation has mature carriers for that retrieval, in the mandatory search of airworthiness directives and service bulletins. The ultralight field, with low regulatory density and dispersed operators, lacks the equivalent, so transmission of the design inheritance degrades to person to person, old hands teaching new ones, and person-to-person transmission is Chapter 30's rupture curve. Design inheritance in a low-density industry can only be governed by industry self-organization: club safety and technical sessions, type owners' groups. The community is the first safety net in a low-density industry, and its activity level is the gauge of that industry's real safety level.

    With the lessons of three cases laid side by side, this chapter can offer a quick identification check, the six signals in compressed form: a sentence about not being able to afford it, quoting an old number; an agenda item that has gone missing, the alternative dropped from the agenda; a phrase that has been promoted, always having used this now serving as a reason; a note of irritation, the from-zero derivation dismissed as boring; a silent archive, with no alternative file; a batch of characterized defects, defects retold as custom. The use of the six is a pocket audit. At the next selection meeting, the next procurement review, the next technical discussion, run the six sentences past your ear, and two hits are enough to start this chapter's annual derivation. The threshold is zero, since listening is free, and that is the minimum viable defence this chapter can offer.

    Case 2 (Manufacturing): Fifty-two Years and a Plastic Spoon

    Case ID: mn-006.

    When and where: 1974 to 29 October 2026. The United States market, with the importer Bodum USA, Inc. of New York; manufacture in Portugal and Thailand. The recall notice was published by the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) on 29 October 2026, number 27-055.

    What and who: Bodum USA, Inc., the importer of record, responsible for sales in the United States and for this recall; the CPSC as publisher of the notice; and the holders of 5.9 million French press coffee makers as the recalled population. The notice does not name the brand's design or engineering department, does not name the glass supplier for the carafe, and does not say whether the two production sites are company plants or contractors.

    What they were doing: a product line that had run for fifty-two years was in production and on sale. The notice gives the sales window as 1974 to October 2026 and lists the channels in one run: Target, Walmart and home goods stores nationwide, plus Bodum.com, Amazon and other online sellers, at about $15 to $65 (CPSC, Sold At). That list is a fifty-two-year total and the notice does not break it down by year. There are eight models: Bistro, Brazil, Caffettiera, Chambord, Crema, Java, Jesper, Kenya. Capacities run to two only, 8-cup and 12-cup, and the common feature is the replaceable glass carafe (CPSC, Description). The notice records three inscriptions: the brand name on the frame, the brand name on the inner lid, and a warning etched on the glass carafe itself (ibid.). One carafe interface covers the whole $15 to $65 price band (an inference: the notice gives no pricing basis). The company issued a statement the same day, describing itself as family-owned for 72 years, with a formula of high quality at an affordable price, and calling the recall voluntary (Bodum statement, as quoted by Daily Coffee News). On the risk side the notice records nothing at all: no design change record, no material assessment, no structural revision, no failure analysis.

    The decision point:

    ① 1974: the product enters the market and the carafe appears as a replaceable part. The freeze date, the review record, the reason for the material choice and the signatories are not in the notice. What can be verified is the result: fifty-two years later the eight models on sale still carry the words replaceable glass carafe. The consequence of that naming is inferable. The carafe became a consumable, a consumable breaking is a spare-part sale on the books rather than a product failure, and the locus of responsibility for failure moved from the factory to the kitchen. The replaceable part has a second function: switching cost here is paid per event rather than once. Every new carafe is another vote for the design, and no single vote is large enough to trigger a re-selection. One more point on the counting. Breakage happens in kitchens, so the count depends on users reporting it; 70 is the number of reports received, not the number of incidents, and the notice gives neither a reporting basis nor a denominator.

    ② The range extends to eight models. The notice gives no launch year for each model. What can be confirmed is the method: eight names attached to one carafe interface, with two capacities only and variation in body shape, frame and inner lid, never in the interface. A fixed interface shares tooling and spare parts, and the price is that coverage of a single-point defect equals the whole product line, with 5.9 million as one number for eight models together. The recalled population is written by interface too, "models with replaceable glass carafes", so the unit of identification is the carafe and not the model.

    ③ 29 October 2026: the remedy is finalized. The CPSC notice's Remedy field carries one word, Repair. The remedy consists of two objects, a plastic stirring spoon and a warning label (CPSC, Remedy). The company statement says that products shipped from then on will include the spoon and the label (original: "Going forward, these products will be sold with the plastic stirring spoon and an additional warning label"). The notice records no participants in the remedy review, no price comparison of material options, and no reason why the carafe or the machine was not changed. A fifty-two-year material question reached the table on that day, and the answer was two objects priced in cents. The remedy does not narrow the recall: 5.9 million units, not one fewer. What it changes is how those 5.9 million get used. It has one further property. It is a permanent product change rather than a one-off recall action, and the change lands on the accessory rather than on the carafe.

    What was visible then: before the remedy was fixed, the following was on the table. Seventy reports of breakage in use, 48 of them with burns (CPSC, Incidents/Injuries), all occurring during use, with no record of breakage while the pot stood idle. The hazard description puts a metal spoon into the causal chain: the carafe can be damaged or cracked by metal spoons and other implements and may then break suddenly during use (CPSC, Hazard). The warning text was already etched on the carafe (CPSC, Description), and etching happens as the carafe is formed, so those words are already in the tooling and any change of material or wording means re-etching. Eight models share one interface (ibid.). The sales window runs 1974 to 2026 at $15 to $65 (CPSC, Sold At). Two manufacturing sites (CPSC, Manufactured In) mean two lines must produce interchangeable carafes, so the tolerances have to be constrained by a single drawing (an inference: the notice gives no drawing). The notice gives no per-model sales figures, so a unit cost for the recall cannot be calculated. What the label has to warn about is written specifically: do not use a carafe that is cracked or damaged, and do not stir with a metal spoon (CPSC, Remedy). The boundary of use for this product was rewritten in this recall, and the carrier was a sticker. What was learned afterwards: the time distribution of the 70 reports, the severity grading of the 48 burns, the per-model sales split, the aftermarket volume of replacement carafes, none of which appears in the notice. In this case that column is empty, empty enough that it has to be said out loud.

    Stop here. Decide before you turn the page.

    You are in that seat now, with the materials above in front of you.

    1. What would you do? Write the action, not "pay more attention".
    2. Which item here are you most likely to miss, and why does it look unimportant?
    3. If you may set one checkpoint, when is it and what does it check?

    What happened: 5.9 million units recalled, number 27-055. Seventy breakages, 48 of them with burns, seven in ten (this book's arithmetic on the notice's fields). The injury type is single: hot liquid escaping at the moment of breakage. Two more divisions on the same fields give about 113,000 units a year across fifty-two years and 5.9 million, and about 1.2 reports per 100,000 units on 70 reports against 5.9 million (ibid.). The recall covers stock sold between 1974 and October 2026, so fifty-two years of product are handled by a single notice, and the scope fits in one sentence because the whole line has one interface. Reviewing the three questions: the most common answer to the first is "recall and replace the carafe", which is not among this case's options, since the two objects were a spoon and a label, and the cost of that action cannot be calculated from the notice because it was never done. The item most easily missed in the second is the warning already on the carafe, which proves that reminding the user has been the chosen route for decades while reports still accumulated to 70. The checkpoint in the third belongs to the crossing of material and method of use rather than to the user's hands. Ask whether the carafe can take a metal spoon, rather than whether users will stir with one.

    Options ruled out: change the material, to borosilicate or tempered glass, which the notice does not assess at all, and whose inferable cost is recutting the tooling and rebuilding the spare-part line at both production sites in step. Change the carafe, meaning recall every carafe, which the remedy does not include, and the logistics and breakage of 5.9 million glass carafes would be a new account on its own. Refund or replace the machine, where the Remedy field says Repair and the company statement lists only a spoon and a label, and whole-unit recovery would be out of proportion to a $15 to $65 price point (an inference). Change the structure, thickening the wall or altering the rim, which the notice does not record, and altering the rim would mean altering the frame and the inner lid, which means altering eight models. Remove the metal spoon from the use case, which was adopted, and it is the only one that was.

    Early signals: ① the warning text etched on the carafe (CPSC, Description). The damage mechanism was already known, and the treatment was to put it into the tooling, so every unit leaves the plant carrying the same reminder. Overridden because writing it down was taken as warning. ② the hazard description treating a metal spoon as a normal use action (CPSC, Hazard). Stirring is a necessary step with a French press, and implement and vessel have always been paired this way on this line. Overridden because a usage problem was not treated as a product problem. ③ eight models sharing one interface, so coverage of a single-point defect equals the whole line. Overridden because interface uniformity was a cost advantage. ④ the fifty-two-year sales window (CPSC, Sold At). Any year could have put the material on the table, and no year left a record. Overridden because selling well was taken as validation. ⑤ 70 reports, 48 of them burns, with one injury type repeating. Overridden as low incidence, in the company's own words: the rate of reported incidents involving carafe breakage during use is extremely low.

    The point of escalation: products shipped after 29 October 2026 include the plastic spoon and the warning label (Bodum statement). The spoon is this case's escalation point. From that day the combination of glass carafe and metal spoon moves in the file from pending assessment to mitigated, and the whole line acquires closure eligibility. The cost is one injection moulding run and one label print. What it buys is a treatment record that can be cited, and anyone opening the page afterwards sees that remedial action was taken. The spoon has a second effect: shipping the remedy with the product writes the recall into the standard configuration, so every later shipment repeats the sentence that this problem has been dealt with. The word the company attaches to the spoon is Bodum-designed (original: "a free Bodum-designed plastic stirring spoon"), which makes it a new part in the product line rather than an emergency item.

    Failure chain: technical layer: the glass carafe cracks under point load, the crack propagates in use until the carafe breaks, and hot liquid escapes. Glass is brittle, the crack sits in the wall where the user cannot see it, and invisible damage leaves care in use as the only enforceable line. The hazard wording uses "including", which makes the metal spoon one known path of damage and leaves the others unlisted. Of three variables, glass, point load and heat, the remedy touched only the middle one, and touched it by changing the tool. Material grade, wall thickness and thermal tolerance are not in the notice. Organizational layer: eight models share one interface, so the defect covers the line horizontally; the constraints on use are etched on the product with enforcement handed to the user; the recall's category is Repair, putting material and structure outside the remedy; the unit of identification is the interface, so any material change would need a validation covering eight models at once, and the notice records no such validation system; the notice records no design review and no failure analysis. Incentive layer: 5.9 million units in the field, $15 to $65 price points, two production sites and fifty-two years of channel relationships. Changing the material means recutting tooling and rebuilding the spare-part line; shipping a spoon means one moulding run. The break sits between the organizational and incentive layers: the cost structure of the remedy determines that it can only land on behaviour, never on material.

    Minimum counterfactual: before the remedy was fixed on 29 October 2026, put carafe resistance to point load from a metal spoon onto the remedy options list, quoted in the same table as shipping a spoon. One action, costing one point-load test and one supplier quotation. It commits to no material change and asks only that both options sit in one table. It depends on no after-the-fact information either, since the test uses the very action already written into the hazard description. The effect is on the agenda: the table gains a column that wants a number, and whether or not the column can be filled, its presence turns shipping a spoon from the only option into one option. The notice records no such item, and nothing in the verifiable content of the remedy shows a trace of one.

    Outcome and sources: on 29 October 2026 the CPSC announced voluntary recall 27-055: 5.9 million units, 70 breakages, 48 burns, remedy repair, being one plastic stirring spoon and one warning label; manufactured in Portugal and Thailand; importer Bodum USA, Inc. of New York. The notice does not say whether the recall runs in batches, whether it covers every carafe in the field, or how replacement carafes will be supplied. This document is a recall notice, not an investigation report: no failure analysis, no test data, no internal documents. This chapter can write only what the notice's format holds and marks everything else as not stated. Primary source: U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission, "Bodum USA Recalls 5.9 Million French Press Coffee Makers with Replaceable Glass Carafes Due to Risk of Serious Burn Injury", Recall No. 27-055, 29 October 2026. The company statement is quoted in full from Daily Coffee News, 29 October 2026, a secondary source used only for the company's own words. The link is in Appendix B under mn-006.

    Case 3 (Aviation): The Design Inheritance of a Two-Stroke Engine

    av-029. Ultralight aircraft fitted with ROTAX two-stroke engines, spark plugs facing down. The Spanish aviation safety agency (AESA) safety bulletin BIS 01/24, published 8 October 2024 and read as a PDF for this book, reports that the lubrication system on this engine type can be installed incorrectly, leading to multiple engine failures. The bulletin runs to two pages and carries no individual casualty figures (no public quantification). What it records is a design inheritance: the spark-plug-down two-stroke configuration has been passed down through generations of types in the ultralight field, the lubrication system's installation sensitivity travels with the configuration from generation to generation, and each generation of users faces only its own aircraft.

    Stop at the decision point: the moment at each certification or modification approval of a new aircraft of the same configuration when the prior notices on lubrication system installation errors are or are not retrieved. On the table: the certification requirement items, the archive of prior notices (AESA issued a consolidated bulletin only in 2024), and the individual confidence that "my installation is fine". Outcome: AESA issues the bulletin to alert the industry, the bulletin carries no binding force, and individual handling depends on the operator. Primary source: AESA, BIS 01/24, 2024; link at Appendix B, av-029.

    The Evidence Gap

    There is a forensic difficulty specific to deep cases of lock-in: lock-in usually produces no accident report, since its cost is opportunity cost and not injury, and this book's strong cases therefore cluster in public infrastructure and long-lived consumer goods. Primary documents on technical standards fights, such as the historical battles over electricity distribution and videotape formats, sit mostly in the archives of the competing parties. The gap is marked as it stands; directions for further collection are dispute archives held by standards bodies and switching-cost arguments made in antitrust review.

    Beside the gap, this chapter offers four derivations, marked as mechanism derivations and not case cards. Derivation one, a software stack: framework and cloud choices take root in team skills, the CI pipeline and legacy code, and the migration cost curve rises faster than linearly with the age of the codebase; the check form for criterion ② is a re-estimate of migration cost, priced on paper every two years. Derivation two, a medical device ecosystem: a hospital's equipment choice grows compatibility roots in consumable specifications, the training system, the maintenance contract and the electronic record interface, and a single-brand ward ecosystem makes changing brand equal to rebuilding the ward; the check form for criterion ① is the alternatives item in the procurement review, since several bidders does not equal an alternatives assessment, the bids being for models inside the same ecosystem. Derivation three, a city and its transport: expanding the road network reinforces car dependence, through induced demand, since wider roads produce more driving, and the feasible space for transit and for walking and cycling contracts with it; the check form for criterion ③ is the annual curve of the active travel share in the transport budget. Derivation four, an organizational process: ERP and process shape each other, with the process set up for the software and the software written for the process, so changing ERP means changing the process, which means changing organizational habit; the check form for criterion ② is the why paragraph in the process document, Chapter 30's Device 1 third paragraph, where a process that cannot say why it exists is usually a process shaped by software. The four derivations run from a codebase to a city and share one chain of criteria; their promotion into case cards is on the collection list.

    The general solution across the four is a design principle that works at every scale: build a translation layer into the contract and the architecture of a new selection, with data formats, interface protocols and skill requirements all written so that they can be translated, and changing supplier costs whatever the translation layer costs. The translation layer is the valve on the depth of the lock. A lock without one is welded in; with one, it is plug-connected. Its price is a complexity premium in the design, and its return appears on the quotation for every future replacement. The only moment to build it in is at selection, since adding it later means rewriting, which ties it to Device 3, exit terms up front, in the same signing: the translation layer handles the technical side and the exit terms the commercial side, one soft and one hard, and both of them live in the drawer on signing day.

    34.4 What Shows Up Earliest

    Six weak signals, each with the reason that usually overrides it.

    1. No alternatives item on the agenda of the selection meeting. Reason given: "there is nothing to choose, so there is nothing to discuss."
    2. A claim about migration cost that quotes a figure from years ago. Reason given: "we assessed it at the time."
    3. A new project defaults to the old selection and nobody asks for a case. Reason given: "consistency."
    4. Attrition among suppliers or among the technical community, in people and in compatible parts, begins to show and nothing is done. Reason given: "market fluctuation."
    5. The organization shows irritation at the hypothetical "if we started from zero". Reason given: "a pointless question."
    6. Defects in the locked object are systematically redescribed as characteristics or as industry practice. Reason given: "being practical."

    Now put the six back against this chapter's cases and derivations. Signal one, the disappearing alternative, belongs to case one: by HS2's review seasons, a network-wide alternative was on nobody's agenda. Signal two, the old number, belongs to derivation one: the migration quote is older than the codebase. Signal three, the default, belongs to case two: fifty-two years of carafe material and never a serious quotation. Signal four, unattended attrition, belongs to derivation four: the loss of ERP-ecosystem people and compatible parts is a late signal of a lock. Signal five, irritation at the from-zero question, is the standing condition of case three's design inheritance in industry discussion, in the form of "my installation is fine". Signal six, defect as characteristic, belongs to case two: across fifty-two years of a fragile carafe, care with glass was the product's personality rather than its defect.

    Reorder the six by the cost of evidence. Signals one and three are cheapest, read from meeting agendas and from the defaults in a project charter. Signals two and six are middling, read from the age of an estimate and from the wording of a text. Signals four and five are dearest, needing staff turnover data and observation of meetings. The recommended start is signal two: collect every claim in the organization that something cannot be afforded to switch, a single all-hands transcript will do, and mark the year of the estimate each one cites. Any claim citing an estimate more than five years old goes into Device 2's re-estimation queue. The length of that queue is the map of the lock's stock, and every coordinate on the map deserves a twenty-minute comparison (the action in 34.7, question five).

    34.5 How to Defend

    Device 1: the from-zero derivation. Once a year, take the core selections and run them on paper: if we started from zero today, what would we choose, and what would the difference cost. The derivation commits to nothing. It exists to keep the assessment function from atrophying, and its output is an annual difference list, whose rate of growth is the rate at which the lock is deepening, or at which the case for staying is improving.

    Device 2: migration cost re-estimated. Re-estimate migration cost every two years and record it: which costs have amortized, which technologies have made migration easier. Most claims that something cannot be afforded turn, after re-estimation, into claims that it can be afforded but would be a nuisance, and the space for a decision sits between nuisance and impossibility.

    Device 3: exit and compatibility terms up front. Write a ceiling on exit cost and an openness requirement for data and interfaces into the contract and the architecture of every new selection. The depth of a lock is priced on signing day, and half a page of terms beforehand beats a feasibility study ten years later.

    Device 4: an alternative archive. Keep a living file of the outside world for each key selection, one page per alternative technology per year, covering maturity, price and adopters. The reader of that file is not the decision meeting. It is your future self. What stands against lock-in is the knowledge that replacement exists, rather than replacement itself.

    The four devices on this chapter's cases and derivations. Device 1 on case one: at the feasibility stage of a large project, run the network-wide from-zero derivation as a fixed step, with the difference list against the actual scheme entering the public parliamentary record. Device 2 on derivation one: refresh the technology migration quote every two years, and set the refreshed quote against the opportunity cost list, which is Device 4's data. Device 3 on case two: write interface openness into the spare-part ecosystem contracts, as a compatibility requirement for third-party carafes, and give the brand's tooling design a tolerance for material change. Device 4 on case three: have the community archive of the design inheritance carry the safety record of alternative configurations, one page a year on a lubrication system alternative.

    The failure modes of the four. The from-zero derivation dies of hostility, since it gets read as an attack on an existing decision and the person running it gets labelled the naysayer; the patch is authorization and indemnity, making the derivation a routine annual action with the author's identity protected or rotated, from the same source as the protection in Chapter 19. Migration re-estimation dies of distorted quotation, since a supplier quotes a migration price designed to avoid the job; the patch is multiple quotations and staged pricing, quoting a migration of one tenth first to find the real unit price. Exit terms die of unmeasured terms, where a ceiling on exit cost is written and the way to calculate the ceiling is not; the patch is a measurement annex, listing the components of exit cost and how each is priced, attached to the contract, the same pattern as the clause template in Chapter 27. The alternative archive dies of being one person's, maintained by a single pair of hands and a single body of knowledge, so the file dies when the person leaves; the patch is a rota, with a different author each year, and the rotation of authorship is what makes several people familiar with the file. The four patches share one check sentence: when was this device's output last cited in a decision.

    The psychology of the derivation carries one more obstacle that deserves separate handling. A from-zero derivation often confirms the status quo: run the exercise, and the answer is that we would still choose this. The confirmation makes the derivation look superfluous, and the appearance has to be taken apart. A confirming derivation is not wasted work, because it turns continuation from a default into a conclusion, and continuation renewed every year is a different thing from continuation by habit a decade old. The difference between a conclusion and a habit sits in the accountability structure. A habit has no signatory, since nobody knows on what day it was decided. A conclusion has one, and this year's derivation report carries a name. Signed continuation and unsigned continuation are treated very differently in an accident review: the first is a tested choice and the second is an unclaimed inheritance. The annual cost of the derivation is one meeting and one sheet of paper, and what it buys is the signature on every core selection the organization holds. That is the best value among this chapter's four devices.

    34.6 Boundaries with Adjacent Mechanisms

    Against sunk cost and escalation (Chapter 27): lock-in explains the structure, why switching is not possible, and escalation explains the behaviour, why more keeps going in. HS2 occupies both chapters. Chapter 27 handles the decision to continue at each reset, and this chapter handles the fact that continuing had long since stopped being a choice between options.

    Against the slack of success (Chapter 20): slack shrinks defensive spending and lock-in shrinks assessment spending, and both are by-products of an absence of bad news. Chapter 20's output ledger and this chapter's alternative archive are two accounts in one system of record.

    On the rationality of path dependence, one concession has to be made plainly: lock-in has returns, in ecosystem stability, in the efficiency of deep optimization, in the free-of-inspection cost of compatibility, and question group 34 in Chapter 37 asks specifically for those returns to be booked. The defences in this chapter promise no release from the lock. They promise to keep the option to leave visible.

    34.7 Five Self-Checks

    1. When did an alternative last appear on the agenda for your organization's most important selection?
    2. Which year's figure does your "we can't afford to switch" estimate come from, and has it ever been re-estimated?
    3. How much of your own skill set is bound to one platform or one technology? Can you tell the part that is an asset from the part that is a lock?
    4. Has a from-zero derivation, once a year and on paper, ever been run in your organization? If not, who is preventing it?
    5. Of the things you use because they are fine, which one have you not seriously compared in five years? Twenty minutes today is worth whatever the answer turns out to be.
