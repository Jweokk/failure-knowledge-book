    Chapter 30 Handover Rupture; Organizational Forgetting

    What this chapter covers: where knowledge falls between one pair of hands and the next; Polanyi's tacit knowledge, the criteria, four sites, the three-part handover template, and the shadow period. When to turn back: staff changes, project transfers, shift handovers, supplier changes. Estimated reading time: 50 minutes.

    Michael Polanyi's epistemological proposition, that we know more than we can tell, is cashed out in an organization every time a person is replaced, a shift changes over, or a supplier is swapped. What can be written into a handover file is one layer of knowledge: parameters, states, conclusions. Judgement, feel, and most of the answer to "why was it done this way" live in people. When the person goes, the paper is promoted to the whole, and everything under the paper is lost.

    What makes handover rupture distinctive is that it is painless. At the moment of rupture there are no symptoms. Every handover was completed, every form was signed, and the knowledge fell at the instant the departing person turned around.

    1 What It Is

    Definition: handover rupture means critical knowledge is lost along the transfer chain. It has two forms: lateral distortion at shift and step handovers, where information does not reach the next party at the same level; and longitudinal loss at personnel change, organizational forgetting, where judgement and lessons leave with the person.

    Three trigger criteria. Any one of them means the mechanism has started:

    ① Nobody can answer "why is it done this way" for a critical process, or the answer exists only inside one head;

    ② Handover transfers documents and states, not judgement and lessons (the field for "what has gone wrong here before" is missing);

    ③ After the long-serving person leaves, answers to the same question begin to diverge by version.

    2 How It Grows

    Three generating conditions.

    Condition one: documents carry conclusions only. A procedure says what to do, not why, and not what went wrong that year. The omission is usually made for schedule reasons and because the point seems obvious, and the obvious is exactly the part most easily lost.

    Condition two: people move faster than documents are updated. Knowledge is leaving and the paper is not being topped up. Turnover rate multiplied by the tacit share of the knowledge is the annual loss.

    Condition three: project-based organization. Teams assemble for a job and disperse when it is finished, and cross-project memory has no institutionalized container. Every project end is a miniature organizational dissolution.

    The distinction from Chapter 16 (attenuation up the hierarchy) sits here: attenuation is a message deforming as it travels vertically, while rupture is knowledge falling as it travels laterally and across time. Both share a root, a transfer bandwidth compressed until only conclusions fit through it.

    Polanyi's proposition has a second half worth adding: we can tell more than we can write. Speech (apprenticeship, mentoring, the pre-shift briefing) carries more tacit knowledge than documents, because conversation is two-way, the listener can ask and the speaker can watch a face and adjust, while a document is one-way, the writer guessing at the reader's questions and dropping whatever the guess misses. That layered distinction gives handover design a direct rule: documents carry what can be written, conversation carries what can be said, drills carry what can be done. Three layers, three carriers, and mixing any two of them leaks (using documents where conversation belongs, using conversation where a drill belongs). Device 1 in this chapter, the template, governs the document layer; the shadow period, Device 2, governs the conversation and drill layers. The division of labour now has an epistemological basis.

    One public finding from the research history of organizational forgetting is worth citing: an organization's memory of its accidents decays over years (the share of staff who lived through the event, the usage rate of review material, the overlap between drill scenarios and past accidents, all measurable decay indicators). Aviation has a mature paradigm against that decay: recurrent training scenarios are required to include adaptations of historical accidents, so every crew's annual recurrent training contains one scenario drawn from a real accident. That is institutionalized forgetting-resistance, the organization training itself continuously on its own accidents. Transplanting the paradigm to other industries has only two thresholds: a review library (no material, no adaptation) and mandatory recurrent training (no mandate, only goodwill). Those two thresholds are the relay between Device 2 in this chapter and the protective mechanisms of Chapter 19: material comes from a review culture, the mandate comes from teeth in the safety culture.

    3 The Mechanism in Four Domains

    Case 1 (Aviation): Two Understandings Before the Stop Line

    Case ID: av-037.

    When and where: 17:32 to 17:47:27 on 2 January 2024 (Japan time). Tokyo International Airport (Haneda), on Runway C (34R) north of its intersection with taxiway C5; Tokyo Aerodrome Control (the tower); aircraft A taxied from stand N957 via taxiways H and C to C5.

    What and who: Haneda Air Base, 3rd Regional Coast Guard Headquarters, Japan Coast Guard: DHC-8-315, JA722A (aircraft A), 6 on board, the captain as PF and the first officer as PM. Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism, Tokyo Aerodrome Control: the positions on duty were aerodrome control east (Tower East), ground control east (Ground East), and the aerodrome coordination position (TC); Tokyo Terminal Control, the Haneda departure coordination position (DF) and the Haneda search coordination position south (AFS). Japan Airlines flight 516, A350-941, JA13XJ (aircraft B), 379 on board. Positions are given as the report names them; the report gives no names.

    What they were doing: The Noto Peninsula earthquake struck on 1 January, and the 3rd Regional Headquarters had to move disaster support supplies from its own building to Niigata Airport; the formal instruction reached Haneda base at 14:55 on 2 January (p.4). The aircraft the base had allotted was G-V, and specialist officer A and captain A agreed to change it to JA722A (p.4). The supplies arrived late, at 15:55, and loading took 25 minutes (p.4). At 16:32 the aircraft was towed to N957, and on the way the APU generator dropped twice the moment it was connected; the maintenance technician judged it a GCU fault, and while he was away at the hangar for a part he was told Niigata could lend a ground power unit, so the plan became starting on a GPU at the destination (p.5). That chain of handling pushed the departure slot from 16:45 to 17:25, renegotiated by chief pilot A with the flight operations officer (p.5). Aircraft A began to taxi at 17:32 (p.6). One item was still open on board: no GPU had been arranged at Komatsu, and the legs beyond Niigata were still undetermined at takeoff (p.5).

    The control side was changing shifts in the same period. The team on duty numbered 10, one shift from 12:00 to 21:15, briefed at 14:05 and taking over the operations room from the previous shift at 14:15 (p.11). Tower East had already been through three positions that day: aerodrome control west at 15:05, ground control east at 15:50, aerodrome control south at 16:35, with about 20 minutes of rest between each, and from 17:20 he was at aerodrome control east (p.11). At 17:42 he saw aircraft A on taxiway H, and the flight plan had already told him this was a supply transport, not a search and rescue task of the priority kind (p.12). At 17:43:02 aircraft B checked in, and at 17:43:26 aircraft C (p.13). At 17:44:36 he told DF one decision: put aircraft A after B landed and before D landed, on the reasoning that B and D were about 7 nautical miles apart, A was slow and C was fast, and sending the slow one first would save the interval for the fast one (p.13).

    The decision point:

    ① The aircraft change on the morning of 2 January. From G-V to JA722A, with specialist officer A reporting to the chief operations officer A at the 3rd Regional Headquarters that it was executable (p.4). The carrier was the base allocation and the flight plan filed afterwards: movement began at 17:25, 6 on board, destination Niigata, purpose of flight written as transport of disaster support supplies (p.6). The change moved two crew who had been standing by on G-V onto JA722A, and put this aircraft into the evening peak departure sequence; captain A said in his oral account that he had stated the purpose when filing, and later read that as the reason the order had been moved up (p.7).

    ② The takeoff order at 17:44:36. A inserted between B and D, carried by DF's out-of-scope transmission and one verbal decision, with nothing in writing (p.13). In the wake of the same decision, DF said C's takeoff clearance would be issued once A had gone (p.14). Once the order was set that way, every later action was executing it.

    ③ The instruction at 17:45:14. Tower East transmitted "JA722A, Tokyo TWR, good evening. No.1, taxi to holding point C5"; the carrier is the control frequency recording, transcribed word for word in Attachment 1 (p.152). It came four seconds after A's first call on the new frequency, and used the format of the example sentences in the ATC Procedure Standards (p.46): the first half has a procedure behind it, the added No.1 has none. Inside one string of speech, half has a source and half does not. On the recording, at 17:45:18 the first officer read back "To holding point C5, JA722A. No.1, Thank you", and between 17:45:21 and 17:45:22 the captain repeated two words only, "No.1" and "C5" (p.152).

    ④ The stop bar lights were out of service during renewal of the control unit. The report states that Runway C has stop bar lights and that they were out of service because of renewal work on the control unit (p.53). Start and end dates, the approving document, and whether substitutes were in place are not stated in the report. The same page records the normal use: in service, departures on Runway C may enter the runway only via four taxiways, C1, C2, C13 and C14, and the controller manually switches off that taxiway's lights each time an entry clearance is issued (p.53). While the lights were out, that one-taxiway-at-a-time release went with them.

    What was visible then: ① At 17:43:02 aircraft B checked in. At 17:44:56 the tower issued B a landing clearance for 34R, and at that moment A was still on another controller's frequency, having been transferred at 17:44:54 and checking in on the new frequency only at 17:45:10 (p.152). So the material on A's table runs as follows: Ground East's taxi instruction and the readback "Continue C holding point" (p.152); the 17:45:14 instruction carrying No.1; at 17:45:23 a voice in the cockpit saying "no problem"; at 17:45:25 the captain ordering the before takeoff checklist (p.8, p.152); at 17:45:41 the first officer calling "Anti-Collision, WHITE" and turning the beacon white (p.152); at 17:46:05 the gust lock coming off (p.152); at 17:46:13 the aircraft crossing the stop position marking at C5 (p.9); at 17:46:26 the Haneda communications operator calling in about the Komatsu GPU (p.9); at 17:46:46 the aircraft stopping on the 34R centreline, 560 metres from the south-east end of Runway C (p.9); from 17:47:03 to 17:47:26 no voice in the cockpit at all (p.153).

    On the control side the table held 5 aircraft of his own, plus two departures on taxiway C heading for Runway 05 (p.128), and an airport surface surveillance display. After D checked in at 17:45:55, Tower East counted the five: A into C5, B cleared to land, C holding at C1, D slowing, E queued behind the two going to 05. All five matched what he expected (p.14). Information learned afterwards: at 17:46:20 the runway occupancy monitoring support function had already raised an attention alert, and it persisted until 17:47:28 (p.51); the function makes no sound, the tower had no handling rule, no training and no document explaining its principle, and all three controllers involved later could not recall anything lighting up (p.51); the flight monitoring position in principle watches traffic on Runway A only, not Runway C (second progress report p.4); captain A had flown 0 hours 00 minutes on the type in the previous 30 days (p.25).

    Stop here. Decide before you turn the page.

    1. What would you do? Write the action, not "pay more attention".
    2. Which item here are you most likely to miss, and why does it look unimportant?
    3. If you may set one checkpoint, when is it and what does it check?

    What happened: At 17:47:27 aircraft B landed on 34R and struck aircraft A standing on the runway. A was severely damaged and caught fire: 6 on board, the captain seriously injured, the other 5 dead. All 379 on B evacuated, 1 passenger seriously injured, 4 lightly injured, and 12 examined by a doctor for feeling unwell (p.1, p.2). Back to the three questions. The commonest answer to the first is "have the crew read back and confirm", and that action costs nothing here, because the readback was completed at 17:45:18, word for word correct. The item most easily missed under the second is the landing clearance at 17:44:56: it is short, it is ordinary, and it was issued in the 32 seconds when A was not on the frequency. The checkpoint under the third falls at the moment of frequency transfer, where the transfer would carry "clearances already issued on this frequency", which arrives earlier than one more look at the runway. The report reaches no causal conclusion: the second progress report still lists the items above as under analysis (second progress report p.3, p.5), the final report has not been published, and this card therefore stops at the point where two understandings part before the stop line.

    Options ruled out: Having C take off before B, rejected: C's taxi speed before C1 was below expectation, and the wake turbulence behind a long-haul international departure would affect D's landing (p.13). Putting A behind C in the sequence, rejected: it would miss the window between B and D, and A is slow, so waiting behind C means waiting longer (p.13). Letting A enter the runway from C1, rejected: C5 is the intersection nearest its position, and only via C5 could it take off immediately after B landed (p.14). Holding the stop position physically with the stop bar lights, unavailable: out of service for renewal work (p.53). Relying on the occupancy alert as a backstop, unavailable: no handling rule, no training, no sound (p.51). A go-around for B at the last moment, not done: the tower issued no go-around instruction, and the report lists it as an action the tower did not take (p.127). Passing the presence of a landing aircraft on 34R to A as traffic information, not done: the recording has the tower telling B "there is a departing aircraft", and that direction runs one way (p.13).

    Early signals: ① Stop bar lights out of service during renewal (p.53), a safety layer offline with nobody watching. What overrode it: the lights are lit only when visibility is below 600 metres or when the controller judges it necessary (p.53), and the visibility observed at 17:30 on 2 January was above 10 kilometres (p.52). ② A March 2008 review meeting on preventing runway incursions produced the occupancy monitoring function, in service at Tokyo Aerodrome Control from fiscal 2010 (p.49, p.50); the function had been live for more than ten years with none of the three, a handling rule, a training course, an explanation of principle (p.51). What overrode it: it was treated as a visual aid only, with the view out of the window taking precedence. ③ The same function arrived at Chitose Aerodrome Control in 2013 already carrying a three-level audio alert (p.50 n.34), and Haneda had none. What overrode it: each authority runs a different system (p.50 n.32). ④ On 10 June 2023 a Thai Airways wingtip struck an EVA Air tail on Runway A at Haneda, and around 17:46 Tower East was recalling it (p.15). The case was closed and filed when handled, and never became a review of the visual division of labour on Runway C. ⑤ The ATC Procedure Standards wrote standard wording and example sentences for "taxi to the runway holding position" (p.46), and wrote no wording at all for departure sequence (p.46). No document marks the gap, and no one treats it as a defect.

    The point of escalation: 17:45:25, captain A saying "yes, then, Before Takeoff Checklist"; the first officer begins reading at 17:45:30, calls "Anti-Collision, WHITE" at 17:45:41, and reports "Checked" at 17:46:05 while taking off the gust lock (p.152). All three actions carry second-level timestamps on the voice recording. The before takeoff checklist belongs procedurally to the items after "runway entry clearance obtained" (p.8, p.126), and once it is read the aircraft's configuration moves toward flight: beacon from red to white, control lock off. At 17:46:13 the aircraft crosses the C5 stop position marking; at 17:46:46 it stops on the centreline, the captain says "I have Control" and the first officer answers "You have Yoke" (p.153). After that, at 17:46:55 the cabin crew member asks "Captain, is now a good time?", the first officer answers "ah, later would be better", and the captain answers "yes, a little later"; from 17:47:03 to 17:47:26 the cockpit is silent (p.153). From the first word of the checklist to the collision is 2 minutes 2 seconds, and nothing in that stretch pulls it back. In the same stretch the tower's attention is on B's landing and D's deceleration (p.128).

    Failure chain: Technical layer: the stop bar lights were out, so the physical barrier was absent (p.53); the occupancy function turns the runway yellow on a screen and makes no sound, and it is eligible to report only from 48 seconds before an arriving aircraft passes the entry end (p.51), with the first actual report coming 7 seconds after A crossed the stop position marking (p.128), so its entire work is to display the error once the error has happened. Organizational layer: the transfer passed the track and not the landing clearance already issued, and the 17:44:56 clearance fell in the 32 seconds before A entered the frequency (p.152); the standards give wording for taxi and none for sequence (p.46); the monitoring function sat in place for over ten years with the handling rule and the training missing, its alert displayed on 14 screens while all three controllers could not recall it (p.50, p.51); the flight monitoring position's remit excludes Runway C (second progress report p.4); Tower East, from seeing A turn into C5 to the collision, said not one word to another controller about A's movement (p.127). Incentive layer: 17:47 sits in the period before the day's traffic peak (p.47), the departure slot had already slipped from 16:45 to 17:25 (p.5), Niigata's operating hours end at 21:30 (p.4), and the tower had to fit a slow aircraft into a 7 nautical mile gap (p.13). The break at the junction sits between wording and rule: one document prescribes how to bring an aircraft to the holding point, and the other thing, how to tell it that it is number one, has no document at all.

    Minimum counterfactual: At 17:45:14, split "No.1" out of the taxi instruction and issue it as its own transmission (taxi to holding point C5 first, then you are number one for departure). One action, doable at the time, violating no procedure, precisely because the standards prescribe no wording for departure sequence (p.46), so splitting it or not both sit inside what the rules allow. The cost is one extra transmission, about three seconds. The gain is that "stop at C5" and "you are first" no longer travel inside one string of syllables. The report lists No.1 separately as one contributing factor (p.127), and records that the captain's readback covered two words only, "No.1" and "C5" (p.152). What it does not stop is two things: the stop bar lights out of service, and the landing clearance missed at frequency transfer. Each has its own entry point, and neither is in this sentence.

    Outcome and sources: JTSB published the first progress report on 25 December 2024 and the second on 25 December 2025; the final report is not yet published, and this card rests on the two progress reports and the control communications record in Attachment 1. The second progress report adds three items for further analysis: that Haneda base had not introduced a sterile cockpit rule, that the first officer ran the before takeoff checklist after obtaining entry intent, and that the crew did not confirm to each other that no other aircraft was on the final approach path (second progress report p.3). Page numbers are the footer page numbers of the reports; in the first progress report the PDF page is 8 higher than the footer. Primary sources: JTSB, Air Accident Investigation Progress Report (1st and 2nd), links in Appendix B av-037.

    The cross-agency handover in Case 1 deserves one more organizational reading. Civil aviation and the coast guard each have mature internal handover systems, with their own radio procedures and their own instruction vocabulary, and the rupture appeared at the interface between the agencies, where the two vocabularies have no institutional translator. Interface governance has precedents in aviation and at sea (joint exercises, standardized phraseology templates on shared frequencies), and the precedents share one feature: the interface is made an object of training rather than an object of assumption. Assuming the other party understands is the most expensive assumption available. A question any organization doing cross-agency work can put to itself: when was your interface with the partner agency last jointly exercised? An organization whose answer is counted in years has an interface shaped like Case 1.

    The three-station transfer in Case 2 has a detail worth taking apart. The error passed the check at every station, because each station checked its own process and not the truth of what arrived from upstream. Trust between stations is a design choice: trust saves time, since nothing is checked twice, and discounted audit costs staff. The lesson here is that trust should be graded. Identity data is high risk and the receiving station re-verifies it independently; status data is low risk and can be accepted as given. The basis for grading is the consequence of an error propagating, an identity error amplifying down the whole chain, so the fields with large propagation consequences are the ones worth re-verifying. Limiting mandatory re-verification to a handful of high-risk fields is what keeps the audit cost bearable.

    The state handover in Case 3 has a general industrial solution worth writing out: a third eye on clearance verification. A line clearance at a strength change is self-certified by the shift, which clears, signs and confirms its own work, and the blind spot of self-certification is familiarity, since clearing the same line every day blurs the eye to residue. A third eye, QC or the neighbouring shift cross-verifying the clearance, gives self-certification an adversary. The cost of cross-verification is scheduling complexity; its benefit is written in the recall quantity, and 33,280 bottles of mixed pack is the price tag the self-certification model carried. Any state handover, a clearance, a changeover, a reset, can ask itself whether this step has an adversarial second pair of eyes. An eye that is missing is a blank left by the rupture.

    The label rupture in Case 4 has an institutional fix worth spreading: a single trigger point for change. When a formula change, a label change and a purchasing change each run their own process, the coupling between them (change the formula and the label must change) is held by human memory. A single trigger point means any change to a formula element automatically generates a label review work order, a system-level link that does not depend on memory, and no change takes effect until that work order closes. The cost is one process rebuild, and it closes this class of rupture at the root. Listing the coupling points that depend on memory and systematizing them one by one is the roadmap for governing handover rupture in manufacturing.

    Case 2 (Healthcare): A Wrong Identity Passed Through Three Stations

    Case ID: me-006.

    When and where: The report keeps a relative diary and gives no absolute dates: pre-admission assessment on day 0, admission on day 2, the first emergency call around 20:00 on day 14, the second around 08:45 on day 19, the third around 15:00 on day 20, surgery from 08:30 on day 21. Places: a care home in England; the ambulance trust's emergency operations centre (EOC); the majors reception desk in an acute trust's emergency department; radiology; the surgical assessment unit and the operating theatre; pathology. Report published 22 March 2022.

    What and who: The care home (resident a 93-year-old woman, called Mrs E in the report, with dementia, able to walk with a frame, prone to falls); the ambulance trust (the 999 call handling desk, the EOC tactical commander, the crew, the electronic patient report form ePRF); the acute trust (the emergency department majors flow co-ordinator and majors receptionist, emergency nurses, radiology, the surgical assessment unit, the operating theatre, pathology, the medical admissions team); HSIB local integrated investigation pilot 2. Responsibility is counted by position; the report gives no institutional or personal names.

    What they were doing: Mrs E was transferred into the care home by arrangement with the acute trust, for dementia and safeguarding reasons (p.9). Because of COVID restrictions, care home staff and acute trust staff completed the pre-admission assessment by telephone, and the date of birth was recorded wrongly at that step: day and year right, month wrong (p.9). She was admitted two days later, and the acute trust's discharge summary arrived at the home carrying the correct date of birth, to be used in building the personal care plan. The wrong assessment was never withdrawn and stayed in the same binder as the other paper (p.9).

    The first fall came around 20:00 on day 14 and the nurse on duty called 999. This time the handler recorded the name spelling and the date of birth correctly, the crew's handheld device found a match in PDS, and the NHS number was written automatically into the ePRF (p.10). The second fall came around 08:45 on day 19, call volume was high, the ambulance trust was operating its emergency rules apply procedure, and the handler did not have to take a full name for the crew (p.10). The date of birth the handler recorded came from the pre-admission assessment and was wrong; the surname spelling was not requested, and two letters were wrong in the middle of the name (p.10). The crew attended, assessed and decided to convey. The handheld PDS search failed, and what appeared on the ePRF was "processing or communication error" (p.10). The care home prepared the documents to travel with her: the medication chart, the precautions and concerns form, and the ReSPECT form, on which the surname spelling, date of birth, address, GP, NHS number and next of kin were all correct (p.11).

    The emergency side was busy in the same period. The majors reception desk had a dedicated receptionist only between 14:00 and 22:00, and the flow co-ordinator covered the rest of the day; observation records show that over about two hours in the morning the flow co-ordinator spent 5 to 10 minutes at the desk (p.27). When an ambulance could not get anyone at the desk, the crew waited 5 minutes and then booked in somewhere else (p.27).

    The decision point:

    ① The telephone assessment on day 0 and the filing on day 2. The date of birth was wrong by one month and written into the pre-admission assessment; two days later the correct discharge summary went into the same binder and did not replace it (p.9). Two paper documents carried it, the responsible positions were care home staff and acute trust staff, and the report states the assessment was completed by both on the telephone. From then on, every 999 call read the date of birth off that page (p.10, p.12).

    ② Emergency rules apply on day 19. The report describes it as a dynamic decision taken with the EOC tactical commander, switched on in bands according to call volume, and reviewed on risk against benefit (p.21). An EOC internal record carried it. The same page states that there is no evidence the ambulance trust had any obligation to tell other healthcare providers about this kind of risk assessment (p.21).

    ③ Booking in around 10:00 on day 19. The acute trust had no formal booking-in procedure, only a flow chart limited to how to save the ePRF into the hospital system (p.26). Booking in was done verbally between the crew and the flow co-ordinator, with the date of birth asked first and taken from the ePRF (p.11). The hospital system, the emergency system and the NHS Spine all had no record of Mrs E, so the system created a new record and a new admission number using the wrong date of birth and surname (p.11). On day 20 the person handling it was the dedicated receptionist (p.25), and what came across was the same new record.

    ④ The wristband on day 19. The emergency nurse applied the addressograph sticker printed with the wrong details to a white band (p.11). The acute trust's PPI policy states in terms that "a hospital addressograph sticker is not to be attached to a Patient Identification Band" (p.29), and requires the band to carry surname, first name, date of birth, NHS number and admission number (p.29); Mrs E's band had no NHS number (p.29).

    What was visible then: ① At the 999 call the nurse on duty held the full set of paper with the correct details, NHS number included (p.11, p.19). ② The crew held the same set (p.11). ③ PDS results on the ePRF appear in three forms: day 14 "PDS found a match", day 19 "PDS trace failed due to processing or communication error", day 20 "PDS trace was invoked and a response was received. A match was not found" (p.22 table 1). ④ On the day 19 ePRF the GP and next of kin details were correct and had not been entered by the handler (p.23). ⑤ At the desk the date of birth was asked first and the surname second, both fields taken from the ePRF (p.11). ⑥ The band sticker carried the wrong spelling, the wrong date of birth, the new admission number, the emergency episode number, the correct care home address and a barcode, with the NHS number field empty (p.29). ⑦ On radiology's CRIS the day 19 entry reads "confirmed by Mrs E herself" and the day 20 entry reads "confirmed by the care home escort", while the escort said she did not know Mrs E's identity details and remembered it being done between the radiographer and Mrs E (p.38). ⑧ The surgical checklist was signed, confirming that band and notes agreed (p.38). ⑨ Pathology treated the first blood tube as a new patient (p.40); the second, crossmatch tube carried the same wrong details, so the two tubes matched each other (p.40). Information learned afterwards: PDS returns no candidates if a single field is inaccurate (p.24); the trust recorded 192 events of this kind between October 2020 and October 2021 (p.7); Mrs E suffered no substantive harm from the wrong identity and received no transfusion (p.41).

    Stop here. Decide before you turn the page.

    1. What would you do? Write the action, not "pay more attention".
    2. Which item here are you most likely to miss, and why does it look unimportant?
    3. If you may set one checkpoint, when is it and what does it check?

    What happened: A day 20 X-ray confirmed a fractured neck of femur, she was transferred to the surgical assessment unit, and a second set of inpatient paper notes was opened under the wrong details (p.13). At 08:30 on day 21, because the patient could not sign, "form 4" was signed, noting no known allergies, and the surgical checklist confirmed band and notes agreed (p.13). The second pre-operative blood tube went to pathology, and the pathology system could not link it to the first automatically, because the link runs on the NHS number alone (p.40). Pathology found a patient with a slightly different surname spelling, a date of birth one month out, the same care home, the same GP and an NHS number, and reported it to theatre; by then Mrs E had left theatre and was in recovery (p.41). Theatre staff telephoned the GP and the care home, the correct details were confirmed, and the two sets of notes were merged (p.41). The report makes 5 local safety recommendations (R/2022/170 to 174) and 2 local safety observations (O/2022/143, 144), and records one national safety risk: where the NHS number is not used as the primary identifier, patient identification is at risk (p.6). Back to the three questions. The commonest answer to the first is "re-check identity on arrival", and the cost of that action here is not money. It is that nobody was assigned to do it, since the desk had someone in place for 5 to 10 minutes in two hours (p.27) and no line in the procedure says whose step it is (p.26). The item most easily missed under the second is the empty NHS number field on the band, because a blank looks like a data tidiness issue and not like a signal. The checkpoint under the third falls at the moment the system finds no such patient and is about to create a record, where the recorded action was to create one directly, without asking the care home and without asking the escort standing there (p.28).

    Options ruled out: Asking the care home or the escort to verify before booking in, not done: the care home's name was on the ePRF and the escort was on site before the new record was created (p.28). Checking the ePRF against the paper in the vehicle, which crews usually do and did not do here: the report gives two possible reasons, that the home is very close to the hospital and that clinical care took priority (p.23), and states it could not interview the paramedic to confirm (p.23). Having pathology link the two tubes manually by the typed admission number, which needs a manual override, not done: the report says the link runs on the NHS number alone and stops at that step when the number is missing (p.40). Pausing surgery until crossmatch closed, a policy other acute trusts hold and this one did not (p.41). Obtaining a number through the NHS App or a similar route, which the report notes was possible and the call flow at the time did not require (p.19). The emergency department telling downstream at booking that PDS had not matched, not done: that state entered no field (p.23). Writing the band by hand instead of applying the addressograph sticker, which is what the policy says (p.29), not followed on the floor.

    Early signals: ① Day 14 PDS matched and the NHS number wrote itself into the ePRF (p.10), so the whole chain looked sound; the one success and the two failures went through the same steps without a procedure, and a success does not add a check to the next attempt (p.26). ② Day 19 the ePRF read "processing or communication error", and the report calls such disconnections "not an uncommon event" (p.22). What overrode it: the device was under a procurement and replacement review (p.23); and the crew had the correct paper in the vehicle (p.11). An unreliable device and correct documents in hand were true at the same time, and no step connected them. ③ The trust logged 192 events related to correct patient identification in 12 months (p.7). What overrode it: identification was treated as an industry-wide problem (p.7, citing WHO 2007). ④ NPSA/2008/SPN001 in 2008, reissued in 2009 to the chief executives of organizations in England and Wales, required the NHS number as the national identifier (p.19, p.20). What overrode it: it was an alert and not a mandate, and the 2014 NHS Number survey was still saying the number had to move from good practice to core practice (p.19). ⑤ Two documents with different dates of birth, one right and one wrong, lay in the same care home binder (p.9). What overrode it: a complete file was taken for a reliable file.

    The point of escalation: Around 10:00 on day 19, when three systems had no record of Mrs E and creating a record was the only action that let the process continue; once created, every later station took it as true (p.11). The new admission number and the sticker on the band carried it. One other person was present, the care home escort. The report says she did not know there had been any difficulty in booking in, that she believed she could have helped, if only by pointing at the documents that came with the patient, and that her recollection is that from entering the hospital to leaving that day she never saw that set of documents again (p.28). In the following 24 hours radiology, the surgical assessment unit and theatre all checked, and what they checked was the new record against its own copies: radiology wrote "confirmed by herself" on CRIS, and theatre signed a checklist confirming that band and notes agreed (p.38). This is the step between nobody can be found and no way back. Of everyone who checked across the three stations, none asked why a 93-year-old from a local care home had no previous notes (p.38); by day 21 the question came from pathology (p.40).

    Failure chain: Technical layer: PDS returns no candidates when any field is inaccurate (p.24); the crew's search had also hit a communication error (p.23); the pathology system links two tubes automatically only by NHS number, and without it a manual override is needed (p.40). Organizational layer: the emergency department had no formal booking-in procedure, only a flow chart for saving the ePRF (p.26); the PPI policy forbids putting the sticker on the band and it was done both times (p.29); the policy requires the NHS number on the band and the field was empty (p.29), while under DSCN 04/2009 the four core identifiers should be surname, first name, date of birth and verified NHS number (p.29); the policy pushes confirmation onto the care home escort, who may never have met the resident (p.36); the report's own conclusion is that a correctly detailed band applied on admission is the only line of defence against a wrong identity following the patient between departments (p.39); radiology guidance says most identification events originate in the emergency department (p.37) and emergency practice did not change because of it; a 93-year-old from a local care home with no previous notes raised no question (p.38). Incentive layer: the EOC traded risk against benefit while 999 calls queued, and the benefit was counted as one more question against one fewer call answered (p.21); the desk had someone in place 5 to 10 minutes in two hours (p.27); a crew waits 5 minutes and books in elsewhere (p.27). The break at the junction sits between two documents: the risk was accepted, reviewed and approved inside an internal assessment, and the receiving organization never received it (p.21).

    Minimum counterfactual: Before the crew left on day 19, correct the surname spelling and the date of birth on the ePRF once, using the paper travelling with the patient. One action, the documents were in hand (p.11), the correction is a single comparison, and the report puts it as a recommendation to the ambulance trust (R/2022/171, p.5). The report also states that crews locally already checked the ePRF against the travelling documents (p.23); in other words the action needs no new capability, equipment or authority, only a change from custom to requirement. It does not change how busy the department was and does not depend on anyone being especially careful. It asks only that an error does not travel while correct documents are in the room. What it does not stop is the next three stations: nobody at the desk is assigned to check, the sticker still goes on the band, pathology still links by NHS number alone. Put the other way, adding a check in the emergency department without touching this station would still have the new record created at 10:00 on day 19 with the same wrong details.

    Outcome and sources: Mrs E suffered no substantive harm from the wrong identity, surgery needed no transfusion, and identity was confirmed after the operation by telephone with the GP and the care home (p.41). The report's safety recommendations all point at standardization and process, and the authors write that such recommendations are "unlikely to eliminate incidents" (p.41); they also write that the investigation could not interview the paramedic, so why the documents were not checked has no answer (p.23). This report is one of four in the HSIB local integrated investigation pilot (p.2), and the five recommendations point at the care home, the ambulance trust and the acute trust (p.5, p.6). Primary source: HSIB, Local integrated investigation pilot 2: Incorrect patient details on handover, published 22 March 2022, PDF on the HSSIB site, link in Appendix B me-006. Page numbers are PDF pages.

    Case 3 (Drugs and Devices): Two Strengths in One Bottle

    ph-003. Mixed Amphetamine Salts Tablets 5mg / Teva Pharmaceuticals. FDA recall record (D-1285-2020, API read for this book): some bottles contained tablets of a different strength, and 33,280 bottles were recalled (Class II). The packaging line has a handover interface between a batch changeover and a strength changeover: clearance confirmation, material reconciliation, first-piece inspection. The exact location of the rupture is defined by the investigation; its type is textbook, in that information about the previous state, what was left in the line, was not fully passed to the operation of the next state. A line's handover does not happen between people, it happens between states, which makes it less visible than a shift handover: no briefing can remind anyone, and only records can speak.

    Stop at the decision point: the handover moment between strength changeover production, line clearance and first-piece confirmation. On the table: the ticks on the clearance record, the visual similarity of the two strengths, the scheduling pressure. 〔Outcome〕Class II recall. Primary source: FDA, D-1285-2020, 2020, link in Appendix B ph-003.

    Case 4 (Agriculture and Food): The Allergen That Never Reached the Label

    ag-009. The 19th Hole Snack Mix (Better Business...Better Foods, Inc.). FDA recall record (event 96758 / F-0801-2025, API read for this book): the product contained undeclared almond and sesame, and 18,000 units were recalled (Class II). A rupture sits between the formula change information and the label update flow: allergen declaration depends on the formulation room and the label room speaking the same language, and any change notice that fails to arrive turns the rupture into product on a shelf. The consequence of a wrong allergen declaration is counted on the most sensitive population: a consumer allergic to almond or sesame does not need to misread the ingredient list, only for the label to be out of date.

    Stop at the decision point: the moment the formula adjustment is signed off and the label print file circulates. On the table: the new formula's ingredient list, stock of the old labels, the distribution list for the change notice. 〔Outcome〕Class II recall. Primary source: FDA, F-0801-2025, 2025, link in Appendix B ag-009.

    Case 5 (Construction and Transport): The Stop the Radio Never Delivered (condensed)

    ce-004. A crane pushing a wagon struck and injured two track workers, March 2025, United Kingdom. RAIB report (2026, pages read for this book): the radio stop instruction did not reach the crane driver, the crane had no forward view, the wagon carried no lights, 1 person was seriously injured and 1 slightly injured. The rupture takes a channel form here: the link between sending and receiving, the frequency, the blind spots, the confirmation process, was never closed, the sender believed the message had arrived, the receiver never heard it, and no mandatory handshake sat in between. Minimum counterfactual: radio instructions in mobile work carry a mandatory read-back, with no read-back treated as not delivered. Primary source: RAIB, 2026, link in Appendix B ce-004.

    Case 6 (Construction and Transport): Where the Rule Book Did Not Reach (condensed)

    ce-005. A wrong points position in a reversing move caused a derailment, June 2025, United Kingdom. RAIB report (2026, pages read for this book): the reversing scenario was not covered by the rule book, the signaller worked from experience where there was no rule to follow and made an error, the 4 crew on the train were uninjured, and the train and infrastructure were damaged. The non-transferability of experience is exposed in the uncovered scenario: routine scenarios travel by rule, exception scenarios travel by experience, and experience lost, or never passed on, makes exception scenarios the worst ground for rupture. Minimum counterfactual: write a rule for each exception handover scenario (turn personal experience into a work instruction), and update the list of uncovered scenarios annually. Primary source: RAIB, 2026, link in Appendix B ce-005.

    Case 7 (Aviation): The Two Numbers Missing from the Joint Briefing (condensed)

    av-016. A glider was towed over speed and the rope released, May 2024, Japan. JTSB report (2025, read for this book): the joint briefing before takeoff did not confirm the tow speed limit or the emergency release altitude, the rope released over speed during the tow, the forced landing at low altitude struck trees, the pilot was slightly injured, and the glider was destroyed. Tug and glider are a temporary pairing of two sets of people, towing across clubs, and the joint briefing is the only window where the two sets of experience hand over; the two numbers that window missed, the speed limit and the release altitude, are exactly the parameters that save lives in an emergency. Minimum counterfactual: joint briefings for cross-crew operations always include a double confirmation of emergency parameters, each reading them to the other and getting a read-back. Primary source: JTSB, 2025, link in Appendix B av-016.

    Case 8 (Nuclear): Two Tagging Errors in Two Weeks (condensed)

    nu-001. Gravelines nuclear power plant, unit 3, major outage. ASNR event notification (October 2026, notification read for this book): an electrical tagging error during the outage left a standby diesel generator without power beyond the 24-hour limit (INES level 1), and the same plant repeated it within two weeks (nu-002, equipment unavailable for 7 hours 45 minutes). Tagging and restoration during an outage is the highest-density handover site there is, with hundreds of tags and multiple crews rotating, and two similar errors in a short interval show the handover interface was not corrected after the first: the lesson from the error went through a handover rupture of its own, in that the fall the previous shift took never entered the next shift's briefing. Minimum counterfactual: tagging errors become a fixed review item in the daily outage briefing. Primary source: ASNR, 2026, link in Appendix B nu-001.

    4 What Shows Earliest

    Six weak signals, each with the reason that usually overrides it.

    1. A newcomer asks "why", and the answer is "it has always been done this way". Override: "getting up to speed comes first."
    2. The handover checklist carries states and parameters only, with no "what has gone wrong here before". Override: "that is what the review is for."
    3. The same question draws different answers from different long-serving staff, and each calls theirs the standard practice. Override: "everyone has their own experience."
    4. A key post's handover period is under a week, or there is none. Override: "the work cannot wait."
    5. After a supplier or partner change, the wording of quality complaints changes and the number does not. Override: "running-in period."
    6. The phrase "it has not been the same since X left" circulates in the organization. Override: "hero culture." (Put the other way: hero culture is a symptom of organizational rupture, not a cause.)

    Put the six signals back against this chapter's eight cases. Signal one, asking why and being told it has always been done this way, maps to the simulated scenario: in the place with no rule to follow (Case 6), the why was never asked out loud. Signal two, a checklist without what went wrong, maps to Case 8: the outage briefing carried no record of the previous shift's fall. Signal three, several versions of one answer, maps to Case 1: where the same aircraft should go next had one version at the tower, one at the flight monitoring position and one with the captain, and the standards wrote wording for taxi and none for departure sequence. Signal four, no handover period, maps to Case 7: the temporary towing pairing had not even a formal handover period, only the briefing window. Signal five, wording changing after a supplier change, maps to Case 3: the running-in period of a strength changeover was priced in a recall. Signal six, not the same since X left, is the organizational signature of the simulated scenario: the more scenarios without rules, the more often the phrase is heard.

    Reorder the six by the cost of evidence. Signals one, two and four are cheapest (interview a newcomer, read a template, check a roster); signal five is middling (compare complaint texts); signals three and six are dearest (they need cross-person checking and exit interviews). The starting recommendation is signal one: next week, find someone within three months of joining and ask them to list the three whys they did not dare ask. That list is the first map of the rupture, and every point on the map is an object for a redemption plan (Device 4).

    5 How to Defend

    Device 1: the three-part handover template. A handover document carries three fixed parts: status (current parameters and open items), why (the reason for key settings and how they evolved), and what has gone wrong here before (incidents and near misses in this step, each with its review number). Parts two and three are where rupture concentrates, and a document without them is not signed for. Format point: parts two and three carry a minimum line count, and a blank fails, because "there is no why" and "nothing has ever gone wrong" are themselves claims that need verifying.

    Device 2: the shadow period. A key post handover gets at least two weeks with two people on the job, the incoming person operating and the outgoing person supervising without doing the work, since rupture is cheapest to expose during the shadow. The acceptance standard is not "handover completed" but "the incoming person completed N full tasks independently". Where two people are impossible, a single-crewed fishing boat or a one-person department, a written question list replaces it: the incoming person asks, the outgoing person answers in writing, and the record of the exchange is the handover document.

    Device 3: continuity of decision records. When the three lines of the Chapter 7 decision record (point of no return, confirmation seat, what was visible then) are archived entry by entry, those records become the core of the handover pack: a new holder reading ten decision records beats ten pages of state description. Decision records turn handover from "I tell, you listen" into "you look at the desk as it was", with the lowest distortion of the three.

    Device 4: a redemption plan for the why. For any process that only one person knows, set a redemption deadline: N months before that person leaves, the judgement is written down and verified by a second person executing it. Verification means the second person completes one full task from the text alone; where they stall is the break point, and each stall is written up. The schedule is set at hiring, because starting redemption when the resignation arrives is usually too late.

    Four devices, applied to this chapter's cases. Device 1 on Case 2: the pre-hospital handover form gains a what-has-gone-wrong section, covering this patient's previous identity events and the known risks in this handover. Device 2 on Case 7: the shadow period for towing is the double confirmation in the joint briefing, plus listening to each other on the first tow. Device 3 on Case 1: radio traffic at the cross-agency interface is archived on the three decision-record lines (instruction, confirmation, what was visible), so the review reads the desk directly. Device 4 on Case 6: the signaller's exception-scenario experience is redeemed into text one line at a time against the uncovered-scenario list, and verification is a second person running the scenario from the text alone.

    The failure modes of the four devices. The three-part template dies in padding of parts two and three, with the why written as "per procedure" and the incident record written as "nothing abnormal"; the patch is padding detection, cross-reading someone else's handover pack, and returning any pack in which "per procedure" appears. The shadow period dies when the shadow becomes a helper, the incoming person fetching and carrying for two weeks with an independent-task count of zero; the patch is a counter of independent tasks, with N completions as the sign-off metric. Decision records die in lagging archiving, the record sitting on a personal laptop and not to be found in the pack; the patch is system-level archiving, in the same repository as the work orders. The redemption plan dies in loose verification, the second person reading and not executing; the patch is execution verification, one full task from the text alone, with any stall returned for rewriting. The four patches share one check sentence: in the most recent handover, did the incoming person independently find a rupture. A handover process that has never found one is a formality.

    One dimension of handover frequency deserves a line of its own: the handover at an abnormal moment. Routine handover has a template (state, parameters, open items), and handover at an abnormal moment, mid fault handling, mid temporary change, mid drill, is the easiest to break, because both parties are busy and the template does not get filled. The simplest form is one structured call: what state we are in, what I am doing, what you do first when you take over, what must not be touched. Four elements said out loud and the minimum handover is complete. The template goes into the emergency plan, on the same clause as the crisis command provision in Chapter 18, and it is drilled, because the muscle for abnormal handover has to grow in drills.

    6 Boundaries with Adjacent Mechanisms

    Against information attenuation up the hierarchy (Chapter 16): attenuation is vertical deformation, rupture is lateral and longitudinal falling. Chapter 16 repairs the channel (side-by-side reading, direct copies); this chapter repairs the container (templates, shadow periods).

    Against the invisibility of maintenance (Chapter 29): the loss of tacit knowledge is the hidden form of maintenance, since the upkeep of judgement (reviews, mentoring, document updates) also produces nothing visible and is also postponed. The ledger principle of Chapter 29 applies to knowledge assets: a knowledge ledger has to be kept too.

    Against why the truth-teller goes first (Chapter 19): the commonest silence in a ruptured organization is newcomers no longer asking why, either because no answer comes or because the question embarrasses the old hands. Restoring a culture of asking is the precondition for Device 4 in this chapter.

    7 Five Self-Checks

    1. How many things on your post can you list that only you know? Do those entries have a redemption arrangement in your exit plan?
    2. In the handover material you received most recently, was there a "what has gone wrong here before" section?
    3. In your team, how many versions of the answer exist for one operational question? Have those versions ever been reconciled?
    4. How long is the spoken part of your shift handover? How much of it enters no record at all?
    5. If the most senior person in your team left tomorrow, which processes would have to be reinvented? Did you ask them for that list before they left?
