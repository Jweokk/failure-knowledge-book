    Chapter 24: Crossing a Scale Threshold

    What this chapter covers: why the same method fails at a larger scale; three criteria, four field sites, the rhythm of re-validation at each magnitude step, and coordination cost put on the books. When to turn back: when growth accelerates, when a single site turns into a platform, when a point turns into a network, when self-operation turns into openness. Estimated reading time: 50 minutes.

    Organizations in growth run on an illusion: what worked at a hundred people will work at a thousand if everyone tries harder. The arithmetic behind that illusion is wrong. Coordination cost grows superlinearly with the number of nodes. Management bandwidth decays with every layer added. Validation's sample assumptions were set at the old magnitude. What makes the crossing dangerous is the way it fails. The system does not collapse. It changes in kind. The same method is still running, everyone is still working hard, and the quality of the output has quietly taken on a different nature, while every current indicator still looks healthy, because the deterioration grows in a dimension the indicators do not measure.

    24.1 What It Is

    Definition: an organization's capability function is not linear. Coordination cost, information loss, the count of anomaly exposures and the count of interfaces all grow superlinearly with scale, while the governance structure (process, systems, headcount, validation rhythm) was designed at the size the organization last had. Crossing a scale threshold means the same method quietly stops working at some magnitude, and the failure arrives before any alarm does.

    Three trigger criteria. Any one of them means the mechanism has already started.

    1. The same method has changed in kind at twice the size: it holds together only if someone coordinates a little more.
    2. A management layer has been added, or the meeting count grows faster than headcount.
    3. Local optima begin to cancel each other, every department optimizing while the total indicator does not move or moves down.

    24.2 How It Grows

    Three generating conditions.

    Condition one. Growth runs faster than governance's renewal cycle. The governance structure was built at the size the organization had when it was designed, and growth leaves that boundary of validity behind. The boundary raises no alarm, because the old method's record of success at the old magnitude keeps being produced. Only the sample behind that record already belongs to an earlier scale.

    Condition two. Validation's sample size was never reset with scale. A defect rate validated on a thousand users scales its exposure count in front of a million: the probability unchanged, the absolute count multiplied by a thousand. A rare defect becomes a daily report, and the capacity to process daily reports was not multiplied by a thousand.

    Condition three. Coordination cost is invisible. Meeting time does not enter product cost, layer overhead does not enter the accounts, interface staffing does not enter gross margin. This is the sibling of Chapter 29. Maintenance is invisible, and coordination is equally invisible. An invisible cost defaults to zero in a growth decision.

    The threshold's most insidious property is that it is retrospective. It becomes visible only after it has been crossed, and the sign is usually the veteran's sentence: "it didn't use to be like this." In an organization that sentence is not nostalgia. It is data.

    Three curves of nonlinear growth are worth keeping in the head, because each maps to a symptom. The coordination curve: fully connected coordination demand grows with the square of headcount, the organization answers with layers and division of labour, breaking one square into several small squares, and each added layer costs one more round of information attenuation (Chapter 16) and one more interface. The interface curve: connections between subsystems grow as combinations, interfaces are the breeding ground of hidden coupling (Chapter 21), and scale gives the dark lines geometric room to grow. The exposure curve: users, devices and transactions multiply the absolute count of anomalies, and validation's low probability enters the zone of the inevitable once volume arrives (Case 1). What the three share is that none of them enters the current set of accounts. Accounts record the linear items, revenue, output, order count. The nonlinear items, coordination, interfaces, exposure, are reconstructed backwards only after a symptom appears.

    The difference between changing in kind and collapsing needs its own passage, because the two are defended differently. Collapse is a hard failure: the system goes down, the service stops, and there is an alarm, a review, a repair. A change in kind is a soft failure: the process still runs, the indicators still climb, the quality has changed its nature, and there is no alarm, no review, no repair. Detection therefore runs on comparison, Device 4's teardown question in this chapter, the external comparison of Chapter 12. Monitoring the indicators themselves will not find it, because a change in kind is very good at keeping indicators looking well. One question for the reader: the last time you discovered that a process had changed in kind, did an indicator tell you, or did a new employee's question? Most people answer the second. An indicator system goes collectively blind in front of a change in kind, because what it measures are the linear items.

    24.3 How It Looks in Four Domains

    Case 1 (Manufacturing): 338,800 Units Recalled in the First Year

    mn-003. The DREO DR-HSH015 electric heater. CPSC recall notice, page checked for this book: about 338,800 units recalled, with about 36,300 more in Canada, 17 ignition reports, 1 hand burn and 3 reports of property damage, a wide recall triggered within one year of launch. With the per-unit defect rate unchanged, channel volume multiplied exposure, the summed hours of operation, by several orders of magnitude. The low probability that validation had found acceptable entered the zone of the inevitable in the first sales season at volume. The threshold sat in the channel, not in the factory.

    Stop at the decision point: the moment the first production run and the channel distribution plan were fixed. On the table: the sample size of the validation batch, the channel's distribution multiple, and a relation nobody had written as an equation, since validation covered the usage intensity of a thousand units while the channel promised the exposure of a million. What happened. A large recall. Primary source: CPSC, 2026; link in Appendix B, mn-003.

    Case 1 carries a second subject worth a paragraph of its own, an exchange rate between marketing and validation. The validation sample size and the distribution multiple come from two different departments, engineering setting the sample and marketing setting the distribution, and no exchange rate exists between the two numbers. Neither department has to answer what a thousand-unit validation means under a million units of exposure. That missing rate is the common blind spot of cross-department scale: each department optimizes locally at its own magnitude, and the summed risk has no owner, which is Chapter 18's dilution replayed on Chapter 24's ground. The fix is to write the exchange rate into the release process. The approval pack for a first release carries two columns side by side, exposure covered by validation and exposure planned for distribution, and a gap of more than one order of magnitude between the columns triggers additional validation or a staged release. Staged release is the engineering answer to the exchange rate problem: let exposure grow with validation rather than with the channel's ambition.

    Case 6 (Manufacturing): Half a Century of Compound Exposure (condensed)

    mn-006. A French press with a glass carafe. CPSC recall notice, October 2026, page checked for this book: 5.9 million units recalled, 70 breakage reports, 48 scald injuries. Half a century of cumulative sales amplified a per-unit small probability into a certainty for the user population. Micro-damage in glass cannot be seen before it fractures, and thermal shock and knocks in use push the exposure of a defect past the recall line across a user base built over decades. This is the time-axis version of the scale threshold. No volume ramp is needed, only time multiplied by installed base. Minimum counterfactual: monitor the ratio of cumulative sales to incident reports, an exposure-calibrated incident rate, and let a shift in the ratio trigger a design review. Primary source: CPSC, 2026; link in Appendix B, mn-006.

    Case 7 (Fintech): The Scale Disease of Growth-Fed Spending (condensed)

    st-001. A legal technology startup, about 200 employees and about 450 customers. Failory Cemetery archive, quoting the founder Justin Kan's public statements, checked for this book: $75.5 million raised, then closed. The founder's retrospective names the problem in his own words, continued expansion and further investment before product-market fit, so that scale only amplified the cost of an unvalidated model. This is the self-imposed version of the scale threshold. The organization applies resource scale to a business function that has not been validated, the function's error is amplified in proportion, and the amplification runs until the capital stops. Minimum counterfactual: bind each increment of expansion to a validation milestone, with no milestone meaning no next increment. Primary source: Failory Cemetery, 2020; link in Appendix B, st-001 (evidence grade E).

    Case 2 (Public): Eleven Thousand Schedules

    Case ID. po-001.

    When and where. Report issued 14 September 2026, with an audit period from December 2024 to September 2026; field data runs to May 2026 and cost figures to June 2026 (GAO-26-107992 pp. 1, 4, 26). Location: Washington, DC, for FAA and the Department of Transportation, plus the Airspace Modernization Office created in January 2026; installation sites are 409 towers, 119 towers with terminal approach control facilities, 25 stand-alone terminal approach control facilities and 21 en route centres (pp. 5, 22).

    What and who. The FAA's Brand New Air Traffic Control System programme, BNATCS. Execution sits with the Airspace Modernization Office, set up in January 2026; oversight sits with the BNATCS Executive Steering Committee, established in August 2025 (p. 22). The prime systems integrator is Peraton, with the contract awarded on 5 December 2025 (p. 3, footnote 4). Accountable posts: the FAA Administrator, the programme managers of the 13 programmes and the integrator's project manager (pp. 29, 30, 32).

    What they were doing. In May 2025 the Transportation Secretary announced BNATCS, compressing the modernization of 138 legacy air traffic control systems into two phases, phase one covering 13 programmes, all to be complete by December 2028 (pp. 2, 14, 15). Phase one was not a new start. It accelerated investment already running: voice switches from September 2037 to November 2028, 106 months earlier; radio sites from September 2037 to December 2028, 105 months earlier; information display systems from September 2034 to September 2028, 72 months earlier (p. 17, table 2). The workload is physical: 450 voice switches, 1,581 radio sites, 5,170 copper cables, close to 30,000 telecommunications services (pp. 13, 17). In July 2025 Public Law 119-21 appropriated $12.5 billion, $9 billion for systems modernization and $3.5 billion for facilities (p. 15, footnote 24). On 5 December 2025 the integrator contract was awarded at a ceiling of $1.5 billion and settled at about $876 million (pp. 22, 23 footnote 36). In January 2026 the FAA told the White House that the $9 billion would cover 10 of 12 programmes in full and that the other 2 were short by $450 million (p. 19). By March 2026, $1.8 billion was obligated, 20% of the appropriation (p. 23). By May 2026, 2,560 of the 5,170 copper cables were replaced and radio sites were 17% complete (pp. i, 31); 9 of the 13 programmes had started, and the remaining 4 were due to start between June 2026 and September 2027 (p. 24). In June 2026, 30 months remained to the December 2028 deadline (p. 26). Installation is scheduled by project, and what has not reached site is 2,610 fibre locations, 435 enterprise information display system sites, 72 terminal flight data manager units, 398 voice switches, 1,311 radio sites and 166 ground situational awareness systems (p. 30).

    The decision point. Four moments.

    May 2025. The Transportation Secretary announced BNATCS on presidential direction and wrote "substantially accelerate" into the programme's objectives, compressing phase one to 3 years (pp. 2, 14). The vehicle was a public announcement with an accompanying programme document, signed by the Transportation Secretary.

    July 2025. Public Law 119-21 completed the appropriation (p. 15, footnote 24). The vehicle was a statute, signed by the President. This step fixed more than an amount. It fixed the shape of the accounts. The same law parcelled the $9 billion out by programme, so a shortfall could only be declared by programme. In June 2026 the FAA put the additional need at $10.8 billion, $574 million for phase one and $10.2 billion for phase two, while the fiscal 2027 budget request asked for $565 million and $860 million (pp. 27, 21). Table 3 sets appropriation against estimate row by programme: information display $300 million, terminal flight data manager $300 million, telecommunications $4.75 billion, radar $3 billion, runway safety and surface surveillance $500 million, weather $80 million, advanced training $100 million (p. 15 footnote 24, p. 20 table 3). No row in that table is the programme as a whole.

    August 2025. The FAA Administrator established the BNATCS Executive Steering Committee, applied new accelerated acquisition rules to every BNATCS project, and signed a blanket waiver releasing all projects from nearly all requirements of the Acquisition Management System, AMS (p. 22). Page 28 records this waiver as one of the key reasons no complete, documented life-cycle cost estimate exists: after it, producing that estimate stopped being a written obligation (p. 28).

    5 December 2025. The FAA awarded the prime systems integrator contract to Peraton at a ceiling of $1.5 billion (p. 3 footnote 4, p. 22). Existing programme contracts were modified in the same step, giving the integrator authority to manage those contractors on the FAA's behalf (p. 23). The contract states that the integrator is responsible for an integrated master schedule covering phase one (p. 29). Ninety days of negotiation followed the award, and the deployment schedule was settled inside that negotiation (p. 4 footnote 7, p. 29). The money saved in the negotiation went elsewhere: the gap between ceiling and final price was more than $600 million, oversight of radar installation moved from the integrator to the radar manufacturer, and the traffic flow management data and services system was deferred (p. 23 footnote 36). Scope, responsibility and deadline locked at this step, and the integrated master schedule was written as a duty, with the delivery condition left empty.

    What was visible then. On 11 January 2023 the notice to air missions system went down, aircraft nationwide were held on the ground for about 2 hours, more than 1,300 flights were cancelled that day and close to 10,000 were delayed; the same system failed again in February and in March 2025 (p. 6). On 23 September 2024 an earlier GAO report had already found 51 of the 138 systems (37%) unsustainable and 54 (39%) possibly unsustainable, with 58 of them carrying significant operational impact on airspace safety and efficiency; 17 were of particular concern, and 4 had no investment under way at all (pp. 1, 6, 7, 11). That same material carried the terminal flight data manager record: nearly 3 years late, cost up 20%, deployment sites cut from 89 to 49 (p. 12). In late April 2025 the old communications lines at the Philadelphia terminal approach control facility failed, controllers at Newark Liberty International lost radar and radio contact for about 90 seconds, and the FAA ordered a ground stop; the same facility failed again in May (pp. 2, 8). In May 2025 the FAA terminated the outside contract for the enterprise information display system and moved the work in-house (p. 26). In September 2025 a telecommunications failure at the Dallas terminal approach control facility sent controllers back to manual clearances and delayed or cancelled more than 1,800 flights (p. 8). In January 2026 the comparison table sent to the Office of Management and Budget was in hand, $9 billion covering 10 programmes with 2 short by $450 million (p. 19), and in March 2026 the obligation figures were in hand, $1.8 billion, 20% (p. 23). In April 2026 the integrator delivered the first version of the integrated master schedule (p. 29). In May 2026 the counts for six categories of installation site were in hand (p. 30) and the executive dashboard was updating monthly (p. 22); GAO could not confirm the dashboard's completeness and reliability, because the FAA did not explain where the data came from or what validated the updates (p. 3). What only became known afterwards: the overlap of the 11,389 schedules at the same installation site, where the report can offer only the St. Louis example and writes of the rest that "numerous overlaps may exist" (p. 30); and the exact size of the full life-cycle cost gap, where the report establishes that the $10.6 billion figure is an underestimate and does not say by how much (p. 27).

    Stop here. Decide before you turn the page.

    1. What would you do? Write the action, not "pay more attention".
    2. Which item here are you most likely to miss, and why does it look unimportant?
    3. If you may set one checkpoint, when is it and what does it check?

    What happened. On 14 September 2026 the report framed the programme as needing improvement in cost and schedule planning and made two recommendations to the FAA: prepare a life-cycle cost estimate following GAO cost estimating best practice, and prepare a phase-one integrated master schedule following GAO schedule assessment best practice (p. 32). The Department of Transportation partially concurred with both and did not list the actions it would take (p. 32). No casualties, no penalty, no grounding. The outcome is a risk characterization and two recommendations not yet accepted. The numbers stay on the table: a phase-one estimate of $10.6 billion against an appropriation of $9.03 billion, a gap of $1.585 billion (p. 20 table 3); a phase two needing another $10.2 billion with no start date and no completion date (pp. 15, 21); and, from June 2026, 30 months of phase-one work left (p. 26). Back to the three questions. The commonest answer to the first is to make the integrator deliver the integrated master schedule before work starts, and that action costs a full stop on installation in the 9 programmes already started, with 30 months left, which pushes completion past 2029. The cheaper place to stand is the 90-day negotiation window, writing the integrated master schedule in as a payment condition, when the cost of stopping was still zero. The item most often missed in the second question is the dashboard, which updates monthly and looks like something that already exists, while nobody can explain its data sources or its update parameters (p. 3). The right point for the third question is the 90 days after contract award, and the object of the check is whether the integrated master schedule covers repeat visits to the same installation site; whether the document exists is not a check item.

    Options ruled out. Five.

    Merging all 11,389 schedules into the scheduling tool. The FAA officials' reason is in the report: there are too many projects, and merging everything into the tool "would not be helpful" (p. 29). Ruling the option out amounts to a declaration that the tool's magnitude has been overtaken by the project count. The occasion was an oral briefing to GAO in May 2026, with no document.

    Rescheduling by facility rather than by project. This would require each of the 13 programmes to hand over the authority to decide installation timing. The report records no programme-level arrangement of that kind, only manual identification in "limited cases" (pp. 29, 30).

    Delaying installation until the integrated master schedule is finished. The December 2028 deadline and the 30-month remainder (p. 26) make this infeasible on budget and on politics.

    Cutting the number of projects and merging similar installations. The contract was awarded on 5 December 2025 with scope divided by 13 programmes (pp. 22, 23), and changing scope means changing the contract.

    Using an AI tool to identify conflicts automatically. This is the route the FAA chose in May 2026. The tool is in testing, and neither rules for its use nor a new process exists (p. 30).

    Early signals. Five, each with the reason it was overridden.

    The 1982 estimate. The FAA's first comprehensive modernization plan, in 1982, estimated $10 billion and expected full benefits by the late 1990s; in 1995 GAO put the effort on its high-risk list, the estimate rose to $36 billion, and the largest component, the advanced automation system, had to be substantially redesigned (p. 9). Reason: every round was treated as a new starting point.

    NextGen. Launched in 2004 with completion planned for 2025; a 2023 GAO report put completion at 2030 or later; fiscal 2007 to fiscal 2022 spending passed $14 billion, and completion needs at least $35 billion from federal and industry sources combined (p. 10). The old ledger carries one more entry: in 2023 GAO recommended that NextGen establish a life-cycle cost estimate, and the FAA did not do it (p. 28, box). Reason: progress was presented by milestone, and the missing piece was the cross-project total.

    The sustainability rating. The ratings of 23 September 2024 found 105 systems unsustainable or possibly unsustainable (pp. 6, 7). Reason: it was read as fleet ageing, with hardware replacement and more money as the answer.

    The OMB table. The January 2026 table reconciled 12 programmes line by line, and one of them, Alaska automation capability, received none of the appropriation at all (p. 19, p. 20 footnote b). This table was the only occasion in the case to put 12 programmes on one page, and the page carries no total row. Reason: every row was compliant, and no row was over budget.

    The first integrated master schedule. Delivered in April 2026 and still not connected to the 11,389 schedules in May 2026 (p. 29). Reason: the deliverable exists, and the work is described as in progress.

    The point of escalation. In August 2025 the FAA Administrator signed the blanket waiver that took every BNATCS project out of nearly all AMS requirements (p. 22). The waiver text states its grounds: AMS life-cycle process steps are numerous and document-heavy and may cause substantial delay, and financial, legal, technical, safety and programme review requirements may add months or years to new system deployment (p. 28). The same text struck the life-cycle cost estimate off the list of obligations (pp. 28, 33). After that stroke the integrated master schedule went from a missing output to a missing obligation, and no post was answerable for its absence. The Department of Transportation later commented that the Administrator had not waived the cost estimate requirement; GAO answered that the waiver text explicitly includes that requirement and that the high-level documents the FAA supplied do not constitute a reliable estimate (pp. 32, 33). In May 2026 FAA officials said that integrating all the projects "would not be helpful" and switched to waiting for an AI tool still under test (pp. 29, 30). By then the room left to retreat was short: 9 phase-one programmes had started, the contract scope was cut by 13 programmes, and the only lever remaining was a contract modification.

    Failure chain. Technical layer. The scheduling tool's magnitude is a single-project, single-programme network. The 11,389 schedules, at an average of 30 activities per project task on the Department of Transportation's own figures (p. 33), put the activity count in the 340,000 range. At one installation site, projects from different programmes schedule themselves separately. The St. Louis example in the report has the voice switch doing site preparation in April 2027 and the information display system back at the same site in January 2028, about 8 months apart (p. 30). Two visits, two disturbances to controller positions. The voice switch carries one further constraint: old and new equipment must run in parallel for every controller (p. 25), and the length of the parallel window depends on how densely the site is scheduled, which is the quantity nobody computes without a total. Organizational layer. The unit of management is the project, not the facility. Each project's schedule can be defended inside its own programme; cross-project conflicts are identified by hand in "limited cases" and have not reached scale (pp. 29, 30). Each of the 13 programmes holds a detailed master schedule covering three years (p. 33, Department of Transportation comments). Every layer has one, which is exactly the shape of a missing total. Incentive layer. The deadline is December 2028, speed is written into the waiver text (p. 28), and money is appropriated programme by programme (p. 20 table 3). The cost of stopping to integrate is carried by the programme that stops, and the benefit of integration is shared by 13 programmes and the controllers. All three layers break at once. The break at the boundary sits between the organizational and the incentive layer. The only carrier that could make someone answerable for cross-project conflict is an integrated master schedule with a signature and a payment condition; the waiver removed the requirement, the contract wrote the duty onto the integrator, the delivery condition column stayed empty, and so no layer had to answer for the absence of a total (pp. 28, 29).

    Minimum counterfactual. In the 90-day negotiation after the 5 December 2025 award, running to March 2026, write "an integrated master schedule covering all installation sites" as the first payment condition of the contract, with the FAA spot-checking the overlapping schedules at three installation sites before payment. One action, placed between award and start of work, with no luck required: the negotiation window exists in the contract (p. 4 footnote 7, p. 29), and with a ceiling of $1.5 billion against a settled price near $876 million, the headroom was enough to hold the clause (p. 23 footnote 36). If the negotiation fails there is a second moment, at the April 2026 delivery of the first integrated master schedule, sent back for rework with "same-site overlaps eliminated" written as the acceptance criterion (p. 29). Neither moment depends on a change of personnel or on extra appropriation, and neither asks the FAA to build a new system first: the integrated master schedule needs the activities and dependencies on one network, and the contract already names the integrator as its author (p. 29).

    Outcome and sources. A phase-one estimate of $10.6 billion, an appropriation of $9.03 billion, and a phase two needing another $10.2 billion with no timetable; both recommendations are addressed to the FAA Administrator, and the Department of Transportation partially concurred with both (pp. 20, 21, 32). Primary source: U.S. Government Accountability Office, Air Traffic Control Systems: Ambitious New Modernization Effort Needs to Improve Cost and Schedule Planning, GAO-26-107992, September 14, 2026. Key pages: p. 1 for the system count and sustainability, p. 15 for two-phase funding and the 13 programmes, p. 17 table 2 for the acceleration, pp. 19–20 for table 3, p. 26 for the cost estimate deficiency, pp. 29–30 for the eleven thousand schedules and the St. Louis example, pp. 32–34 for the recommendations and the Department of Transportation's comments. Body text runs to p. 34; appendices with the methodology and the department's comments run to p. 47. Link in Appendix B, po-001.

    The 11,389 schedules are worth a second reading from the angle of tool evolution. Project-by-project tracking is the previous magnitude form of the tool, the aggregation need of 138 systems is the next one, and the distance between the two is GAO's structural finding. Changing the tool is not buying new software. It is changing the unit: a full life-cycle cost estimate, a single milestone framework, a cross-system dependency view, which is Chapter 21's mapping applied directly to a very large programme. Resistance sits locally, since every system's team has to hand over part of its autonomy, and the size of the resistance is proportional to the political standing of the system. External oversight earns its place here by giving the change of unit an institutional reason, in the shape of congressional questioning. The lesson for anyone running a large programme: the coordination tool has to lead the engineering magnitude by one step. Leading costs redundant tooling; following costs what Case 2 cost.

    Case 3 (Software): The Day of Record Load

    sw-005. The GitHub platform, 17 August 2026. Official statement, page checked for this book: record load broke through components with insufficient headroom, and the outage lasted 7 hours 47 minutes. The core assumption of capacity planning was calibrated on historical peaks, and the record day reset that assumption's historical extrapolability to zero. The scaling coefficient was not wrong. The object it extrapolated from, the previous generation's load shape, was no longer this load. Scale arrived as a double growth, in traffic and in task shape, and the old peak curve could not measure the new workload.

    Stop at the decision point: the last time the capacity model was parameterized on historical peaks and left no unjustified headroom for a record day. On the table: the growth curve, the architecture at the last peak, and a set of new behaviors that could not be verified. What happened. Close to eight hours unavailable, with recovery in stages and the follow-up work named "the work ahead". Primary source: GitHub, 2026; link in Appendix B, sw-005.

    Case 3's capacity headroom has a further connection, to the illusion of redundancy in Chapter 22. Redundancy is a form of capacity, standby processing power, and its magnitude calibration faces the same record-day problem: backup capacity is sized on historical peaks, and a record day fills primary and standby together. The three cloud-era cases, Case 3 here with Cases 2 and 5 of Chapter 21, add up to a capacity governance checklist for platform readers: parameterize on historical peaks, keep unjustified headroom for the record day (Device 3), monitor load shape as well as load volume, and re-validate at each magnitude step, which is the exam re-sat on the ladder. What the four share is a philosophy, a humility coefficient, believing by default that this time differs from last time. Humility is not cheap, since headroom costs money, and its price is written on the bill for every outage.

    Case 4 (Finance): Thirty-Eight Shells Registered in Bulk

    fi-004. The SEC's action against 38 fictitious investment adviser entities, Litigation Release No. 26622, page checked for this book: through 2025 and 2026 the defendants made material false statements in Forms ADV filed with the Commission, presenting themselves as legitimate advisory firms, and the cases were consolidated in the federal district court in Colorado. The registration system's design capacity assumed firm-by-firm human review. Bulk, automated, template-driven fabrication voided that scale assumption wholesale: the threshold did not move, and the way of crossing it did. This is the adversary's version of the scale threshold, where growth happens in the attack rather than in the organization, while the defence's granularity stays one firm at a time.

    Stop at the decision point: every day the registration system's structure, its bulk submission interfaces and its formal review rules, stays as it is in front of the new automated use. On the table: the anomalous growth in submission volume and the compliant appearance of each firm's materials. What happened. The 38 cases consolidated in one action; systematic registration anti-fraud entered the agenda. Primary source: SEC, Litigation Release No. 26622, 2026; link in Appendix B, fi-004.

    The adversary's version has a symmetry worth adding. Defence calibrates its magnitude to the history of attacks, designing review throughput against past submission volume, and attack calibrates its magnitude to the holes in the defence, with automation using the gaps in firm-by-firm review in bulk. The two calibrations chase each other, and the defence is always half a step behind for a structural reason: changing the defence requires procedure, and a rule change needs a process, while changing the attack requires a script and one person. What compensates for the half step is anomaly monitoring. Shifts in the distribution of submissions, bulk volume, template similarity, machine timing signatures, are the fingerprints scaled attack leaves behind, and fingerprint monitoring costs far less than scaling firm-by-firm review. Readers in regulatory technology can file this case as a pattern: when defence granularity and attack granularity stop matching, put fingerprint monitoring in first and discuss rule revision second.

    Case 5 (Healthcare): The Oxygen Peak in the Pipes (full card)

    Case ID. me-011.

    When and where. 19 May 2021, report published (HSIB p. 1), official PDF of 61 pages. The event happened in the autumn of 2020, during the second wave; the report redacts the trust's name and the specific dates, leaving only the weekday sequence "Thursday evening, late Friday night, Saturday, Sunday" (HSIB pp. 25–26). Location: two sites of one acute hospital trust in England. The points involved are the high dependency unit (HDU) and the operating theatres; the HDU had been built at the end of an existing Nightingale ward and used that ward's existing pipework (HSIB p. 42).

    What and who. The trust's medical gas pipeline system (MGPS) and the estates and facilities team. Inside the hospital, HTM 02-01 sets four posts: the medical gas committee (MGC, a multidisciplinary forum), the authorising engineer (external reviewer, producing an annual report, the only external assurance mechanism in the HTM), the authorised person (the engineer running day-to-day management, appointed on the authorising engineer's recommendation and re-examined every three years), and the designated nursing or medical officer (the ward-level clinical contact who clears work affecting supply) (HSIB pp. 19, 35, 36). Outside: NHS England and NHS Improvement (NHSE/I) issue guidance and alerts, the CQC regulates site safety, and HSIB ran this investigation. The report does not disclose the trust's name or its bed count; each of the two sites has its own radial MGPS (HSIB p. 44).

    What they were doing. In 2012 the trust recorded "lack of understanding of hospital-wide oxygen flow" on the estates risk register, and because of its severity the same entry went onto the trust-level risk register (HSIB p. 23). In April 2020 the estates team and a senior nurse with surgical and critical care experience produced the "red dot plan": red dots on the site floor plans marking where COVID patients could be placed, so that oxygen pickup points would be spread out (HSIB p. 23). The plan was laid out by where oxygen enters the hospital from the MGPS, and the trust at the time believed this also covered the flow limits of each ward (HSIB p. 23). The flow basis was the understanding of treatment then current: patients on ventilators, about 15 litres per minute each (HSIB p. 23). On that basis the HDU was calculated to have 145 litres per minute available (HSIB p. 23). In March and April 2020 two CAS alerts reached the estates team by email, and the team replied to the trust's CAS officer confirming the measures had been taken (HSIB p. 24). The first wave hit this trust lightly and the red dot plan was never tested against real demand; operational need also ruled out simulation or dedicated testing, which would have affected patient care (HSIB pp. 24, 31). In the summer of 2020 attention moved back to outpatient clinics and elective surgery, non-COVID patients returned, and hospital-wide activity ran higher than in the first wave (HSIB p. 24). The autumn brought the second wave, with the red dot plan still in place (HSIB p. 24). Treatment had changed: CPAP and nasal high flow had become mainstream, and both consume more oxygen than a ventilator (HSIB p. 25). Typical flows for the three devices: ventilator 15 litres per minute, CPAP about 30, nasal high flow more than 60 (HSIB p. 11).

    The decision point. Four moments.

    1. The oxygen flow knowledge gap entered two levels of risk register (HSIB p. 23). The vehicle was the register itself, with "identified mitigations" in the action column. For the next eight years the risk sat on the register and the capacity was never recalculated; the authorising engineer's reports for 2019 and 2020 were still recording too few authorised persons and designated officers not appointed (HSIB pp. 36, 39).

    April 2020. The red dot plan was finalized (HSIB p. 23). The vehicle was the site floor plans and the plan document, handled by the estates team and that senior nurse. The HDU's available flow went into the calculation as 145 litres per minute (HSIB p. 23). That single number decided how many patients the HDU could take and which oxygen therapy they could receive (HSIB p. 24). The report's record of this step is direct: the trust initially considered that the HDU should be able to deliver the specified flow, and calculated patient numbers and therapy types accordingly (HSIB p. 5). The plan defined "balance" between areas on the floor plan, never down to pipe sections.

    July 2020. The MGC held its scheduled meeting. The two NHSE/I alerts on piped oxygen supply, of 31 March and 6 April, were in the meeting pack, not listed as agenda items, and not raised under other business (HSIB p. 24). The alerts' requirement was specific: engineers, clinicians and managers should plan peak capacity together (HSIB p. 20). Nationally, some trusts held only 2 of 12 planned MGC meetings in three years (HSIB p. 33); this trust held the scheduled July meeting, with the alerts in the pack and not on the agenda.

    Early September 2020. The authorising engineer emailed the NHSE/I guidance on piped oxygen, published in August, to the trust's two authorised persons (HSIB pp. 24, 49). One was on leave awaiting planned surgery, the other was in hospital with COVID-19, and the trust did not register the email (HSIB p. 24). This route was standard practice at the time: the guidance did not go through CAS and was delivered directly by the authorising engineer (HSIB p. 49); because it was not published on CAS, it carried no CAS target date and no completion return (HSIB pp. 49, 52). The four moments have four different vehicles, a register, a floor plan, a meeting pack, an email, and none of them required an answer to the question of how much flow a given pipe section can deliver.

    What was visible then. The 2006 edition of HTM 02-01 sets minimum flows: general wards at 6 litres per minute for 1 bed in 4 plus 10 litres per minute for 1 further bed, giving 50.5 litres per minute for a 28-bed ward; the HDU at 10 litres per minute for 1 bed and 6 litres per minute for three quarters of the remaining beds, giving 37 litres per minute for a 7-bed HDU (HSIB p. 19). Beyond that, system redundancy is used first, and at the pipe limit there is a pressure drop and a loss of flow (HSIB p. 19). The HTM runs to 336 pages (HSIB p. 19). The report distinguishes the terms: capacity is the system's maximum oxygen production, flow is the volume delivered in a given time (HSIB p. 14). Network shape: most NHS hospitals are radial, so take-off early in the network lowers the pressure available downstream (HSIB p. 18).

    The estates alert of 31 March 2020: when demand at several wall outlets exceeds the VIE delivery limit, supply pipes lose pressure quickly and hospital-wide failure becomes possible, and VIEs can be exhausted quickly and unpredictably (HSIB p. 20). The alert of 6 April 2020: some hospitals were already drawing more than the MGPS design maximum flow, and icing in the pipes can reduce flow unexpectedly (HSIB p. 20). Clinical guidance from NHSE/I on 9 April 2020: the adult oxygen saturation target was lowered from 94%–98% to 92%–96%, with 90%–94% to be considered when necessary (HSIB p. 22). Guidance in August 2020: the concerns were weaknesses in equipment, in system configuration and in operational management, and none of them concerned liquid oxygen running out (HSIB p. 21). The alert of 19 November 2020: winter demand could run higher than in spring, because elective surgery and recovery areas had restarted, and both new wards and old estate would add load (HSIB p. 21).

    The trust's own reading: total hospital demand never exceeded 56% of total capacity, and oxygen production had headroom (HSIB p. 5). One more document is on file, the estates team's written confirmation to the CAS officer, in March and again in April, that measures were in place (HSIB p. 24). The division of labour is in the material too: estates understood pipes and pressure and not changes in clinical treatment, while clinicians understood patients and saturation targets and not the oxygen consumption of different devices (HSIB p. 31).

    What only became known afterwards: in a pressure test the HDU pressure fell from 4.2 bar to 3.1 bar in "about 10 seconds" and the alarm sounded (HSIB p. 25); the shortfall came from existing pipework upstream of the HDU being drawn on first (HSIB p. 25); and a causal link between the two patients' desaturation and the pressure drop was never established (HSIB p. 26).

    Stop here. Decide before you turn the page.

    1. What would you do? Write the action, not "pay more attention".
    2. Which item here are you most likely to miss, and why does it look unimportant?
    3. If you may set one checkpoint, when is it and what does it check?

    What happened. A major incident was declared on the Saturday afternoon, and every patient arriving at the hospital was diverted to other trusts (HSIB p. 26). The diversion was later narrowed: one site cancelled it, and the other diverted only patients needing more than 6 litres per minute (HSIB p. 26). At least 40 elective operations were cancelled (HSIB p. 26). The major incident was stood down seven days later (HSIB p. 26). Two patients recovered with no adverse outcome, and the report records no deaths (HSIB p. 26). The MGC met in emergency session and thereafter weekly (HSIB p. 26). HSIB made four recommendations: define the duties and competences of the posts listed in the HTM (R/2021/132), establish continuing assurance of authorising engineer competence (R/2021/133), revise and reissue the HTM (R/2021/134), and have the CQC adjust its site assessment model (R/2021/135); an interim bulletin carried one further recommendation on the MGC (R/2021/120), with four observations and three actions, one of them the trust procuring pipeline flow meters and electronic telemetry (A/2021/041) (HSIB pp. 7–9). Back to the three questions. The commonest answer to the first is to increase oxygen production capacity, and that action buys no flow here: the report states that increasing the oxygen available cannot overcome the limits of the hospital's internal pipeline infrastructure (HSIB p. 43). The item most often missed in the second question is the two lines of HTM minimum flow, 50.5 and 37 litres per minute, which look like a compliance floor and are in fact a design ceiling. The point for the third question is the flow basis in the April 2020 red dot plan: whether the basis is 15 or 60 decides how many patients the HDU can take.

    Options ruled out. Six.

    Simulating the peak before real demand arrived. The report states that operational need made it impossible to simulate MGPS demand or run more specific tests, because that would have affected patient care (HSIB p. 31).

    Expanding the production side. NHSE/I had a capital programme replacing VIE components, and the report states in the same place that it does not solve the pipework (HSIB p. 43).

    Converting to a ring main. Both trust sites are radial, the HTM does not require ring mains in new work, and it assumes most work will still be built radial (HSIB p. 44); the report lists ring main conversion as a mitigation other trusts used (HSIB p. 45), and this trust's two sites were still radial at the time of the event (HSIB p. 44).

    Spreading patients across the hospital. This is the ideal from the estates side, since it balances pressure across the network (HSIB p. 32). On the clinical side, infection control requires COVID patients to be cohorted, and rota efficiency has to be weighed as well (HSIB p. 32). Two local optima collide directly over bed allocation.

    Manifold cylinder packs as backup. The report lists this as mitigation engineering at other trusts; it tops up gas during a pressure drop and does not change the delivery capacity of the pipes (HSIB p. 45).

    Waiting for the HTM revision. The revision work started in late 2019 and was shelved in 2020 because of the pandemic (HSIB p. 18), so it was unreachable inside the event window.

    Early signals. Five.

    The 2012 register entry (HSIB p. 23). Reason: registration was taken as disposal.

    The authorising engineer's annual reports for 2019 and 2020, naming three things not done in two consecutive years: appointing designated officers, adding authorised persons, and cover out of hours (HSIB pp. 36, 39). The designated officer vacancy has a history: a cohort was trained about six years earlier, restructuring and staff loss left nobody to succeed them, and the DMO post stayed vacant (HSIB p. 39). Reason: the annual recommendation is copied from year to year and does not constitute a condition for starting work.

    The two alerts of 31 March and 6 April 2020 (HSIB p. 20). Reason: they were issued as estates alerts, not as national patient safety alerts, and carried no completion return, so the trust did not treat them at the highest priority (HSIB p. 52); the 31 March one went to a wider list than comparable estates alerts and its level was still estates (HSIB p. 52). Nationally, CAS alert volume was up 177% year on year (HSIB p. 51).

    The July 2020 MGC meeting, with the alerts in the pack (HSIB p. 24). Reason: not on the agenda, so nobody read them out; the MGC had long been treated as "the estates meeting", and clinical attendance was already unstable (HSIB p. 33).

    Thursday evening, the theatre pressure alarm sounding intermittently (HSIB p. 25). Reason: the alarm stopped once patients were moved under the red dot plan, and that was taken as resolution. The HDU sounded no pressure alarm that night (HSIB p. 25).

    The point of escalation. When the new HDU was completed, no additional money went into raising pipeline capacity. It was designed to HTM minimum flow and connected to the end of existing pipework, and that pipe passes the Nightingale ward beds before it reaches the HDU, so redundancy in the system may already have been drawn off upstream (HSIB p. 42). This step turned a pipework problem that could still have been changed into poured concrete, and raising capacity afterwards means building work. In the same period infection control concentrated COVID patients into a few wards, and the HDU sat at the end of the network (HSIB pp. 32, 42). The new ward got new equipment and new beds; the pipework stayed old (HSIB p. 42). The report records no signatory for this step, because every new bed and every new device was approved separately and complied separately; the two written replies to the CAS officer in March and April were already the latest conclusion in the file about this system (HSIB p. 24).

    Failure chain. Technical layer. The report states that the radial network showed no problem before the pandemic, because the expected oxygen use sat inside the capacity of a radial system (HSIB p. 45); upstream draw lowers downstream pressure (HSIB p. 18); the design basis is bed count multiplied by per-bed flow, the actual use is device flow multiplied by devices running at once, and there is no conversion between the two measures (HSIB pp. 19, 11); and once redundancy is used up, pressure drops and flow is lost (HSIB p. 19). Organizational layer. The unit of capacity review is the site and the ward, and pipe sections are not in it; the unit of the red dot plan is a dot on a floor plan, and pipe routing is not in it; the MGC agenda carries no capacity item (HSIB pp. 23, 24). Training is missing too: the NHS electronic staff record contains a subject called medical gases but no usable course module (HSIB p. 41). External assurance is missing as well: before April 2020 PAM was completed voluntarily, about three in ten trusts did so, and no data submission was required (HSIB p. 47); CQC inspection looks at the ward environment, and site-level pipeline assurance is not a routine check item (HSIB p. 48). Incentive layer. Capital goes to new clinical areas, and existing infrastructure does not get investment at the same priority (HSIB p. 42); the maintenance backlog for sites in England rose from £6.46 billion to £9 billion within a year, a 40% increase (HSIB p. 42); and the level of an alert decides the resources that answer it (HSIB p. 52). All three layers break at once. The break at the boundary is in the unit of evidence: technical readings arrive per outlet and per ward, organizational action starts per site and per specialty, and no post in between is responsible for turning a pipe section into a network.

    Minimum counterfactual. When the red dot plan was finalized in April 2020, change the flow basis from the ventilator's 15 litres per minute to the CPAP and nasal high flow settings, 30 litres per minute and above 60, recalculate available flow at the most upstream pipe section on each site, and write the simultaneous draw limit for a single pipe section as a hard constraint on bed allocation. One action, in the window between the first and the second wave, April to September 2020. No luck required: the mainstream devices in November were CPAP and high flow (HSIB p. 25), and both flow figures could be obtained from the equipment side in April (HSIB p. 11); the authorising engineer's annual report mechanism was already running (HSIB p. 36). What gets checked is specific: how many beds draw on the most upstream section at peak, multiplied by the device flow per bed, against the available flow of that section. This is paper work, with no new equipment, no building work, and no dependence on a change of personnel. It costs one calculation and one MGC agenda item. Not doing it cost the seven days in November and the 40 operations.

    Outcome and sources. A major incident, diversion at both sites, at least 40 elective operations cancelled, two patients recovered, and no deaths recorded. Primary source: Healthcare Safety Investigation Branch, Investigation report: Oxygen issues during the COVID-19 pandemic, 19 May 2021, official PDF of 61 pages, downloaded locally and read section by section (the archived hsib.org.uk page returned 429, so the current PDF at hssib.org.uk was used; archived link in Appendix B, me-011). Evidence base: all MGPS documents and interviews at the trust concerned, comparable material from four other trusts, and interviews with nine organizations including the DHSC, NHSE/I, the CQC, the MHRA and the British Thoracic Society (HSIB p. 29). The report frames the case as a national safety risk: at least a dozen NHS trusts had raised the same problem, each of them had enough liquid oxygen, and what was limited was the section of pipe that carries gas to the bedside (HSIB p. 28). Key pages: p. 5 for the 56% total demand, p. 11 for device flows, p. 19 for HTM minimum flows, pp. 20–22 for alerts and guidance, pp. 23–26 for the event sequence, pp. 42–45 for infrastructure and network shape, pp. 51–52 for alert levels.

    24.4 What Shows Earliest

    Six weak signals, each with the reason that usually overrides it.

    1. A management layer is added, on the grounds of strengthening coordination. Override: "we have reached that scale."
    2. The number and length of meetings grow faster than headcount, and decisions get slower. Override: "people want to align."
    3. The veterans' phrase becomes "it didn't use to be like this." Override: "nostalgia."
    4. Every local indicator improves while the total stalls or worsens. Override: "every department is working hard."
    5. Rare events rise in frequency in proportion to scale, with absolute counts growing, and get read as quality deterioration. Override: "a quality problem." (The numerator grows with the denominator, which is not a quality signal.)
    6. The staffing share of interfaces, reconciliation and cross-department scheduling quietly passes the staffing share of core output. Override: "big companies are all like this."

    Take the six back to this chapter's seven cases. The added-layer signal maps to Case 2, where coordination layers and system count inflated together across a multi-phase programme. The meeting-growth signal maps to Case 5, where coordination cost stayed off the books and cross-department peak planning at the hospital ran entirely on ad hoc meetings. The local-optima signal maps to Case 1, where engineering's conservative validation and the channel's aggressive distribution were each locally optimal. The assumption-reset signal maps to Cases 3 and 6, where capacity coefficients and exposure assumptions both ran at the old magnitude. The granularity signal maps to Case 4. The veteran's phrase is the spoken signature of all of them: an old hand senses the change in kind before any data does.

    Reorder the six by cost of discovery. The local-optima and assumption-reset signals are cheapest, both being a matter of comparing numbers, coordination hours as a share and the date an assumption was last reset. The added-layer and meeting-growth signals come next, since they need the organization chart and the calendar. The granularity and veteran signals are dearest, needing external comparison and interviews. The starting suggestion is to make the coordination-hours signal a standing action of Device 2: publish coordination hours as a share of core hours once a quarter. That number is the thermometer of the scale disease. A single rise is not necessarily illness; a sustained rise combined with local optima cancelling is the diagnosis.

    24.5 How to Defend

    Device 1: re-validation at each magnitude step. Divide organizational scale into steps, ten people, a hundred, a thousand, ten thousand, and force a re-validation of the core assumptions at every crossing: coordination method, validation sample size, capacity coefficient, review throughput. Not linear extrapolation. The problem is worked again from the beginning. GitHub's capacity coefficient, DREO's validation sample and the FAA's schedule tool should all re-sit the exam at the step, and the exam has one question: does this method still hold at the new magnitude, and on what evidence.

    Device 2: coordination cost on the books. Hours spent in cross-department meetings, on reconciliation and on interface maintenance are counted quarterly and published. When the share passes a threshold, self-set, a fifth of core hours say, a structural review is triggered. Invisible costs are the ones that need booking, since a visible cost announces itself. Booking has a second use: it gives expansion justified by "we hire in order to coordinate" a real unit price.

    Device 3: unjustified headroom in capacity assumptions. For record-day scenarios the capacity model keeps one tier of headroom that cannot be argued from history, two tenths on top of the peak coefficient for instance, and the document states plainly that the basis for this tier is that we do not know. The basis of headroom may be luck, provided someone remembers it is luck. Write it down and luck can be audited.

    Device 4: the annual teardown question about structure. Once a year, run the thought experiment: if we designed from zero today, how would the coordination structure differ from this one? The difference list does not serve an immediate reorganization. It serves early warning, and the speed at which the list lengthens is the speed at which the threshold approaches, which is Chapter 34's path-lock contrast question used here in reverse.

    Put the four devices against this chapter's cases. Device 1 on Case 1: when a distribution plan crosses an order of magnitude, force the exposure model to be rerun, with the validation sample reset to the target exposure. Device 2 on Case 5: count the hospital's cross-department coordination hours quarterly, and when the share rises look for a capacity gap first, since more coordination usually means someone is compensating for missing capacity. Device 3 on Case 3: write the record-day tier's basis as not knowing, and audit headroom with a single question: what is this tier's basis? Device 4 on Case 2: the annual exercise for a multi-phase programme asks how the governance structure for 138 systems would be designed if the programme were started today, and the difference list goes to the external overseer.

    Each device has a characteristic way of dying. Re-validation dies at the step that is never defined: re-validation is postponed after every small increase, on the grounds that the step has not been reached, and the definition of a step is relaxed by personal favour. The patch is to write the steps into the rules, round-number thresholds for headcount, users and volume, so that the crossing triggers by itself with no human review. Coordination accounting dies by definition drift: meeting hours go from all staff to a sample, and numerator and denominator each quietly shrink. The patch is to write the definition into the rules and put it in the annual internal audit. Unjustified headroom dies by being borrowed, with the record-day tier scheduled away in ordinary times; the patch is to make the tier unschedulable in the scheduling system, a hardware logic lock rather than discipline. The teardown question dies when the exercise turns into complaint, the difference list becoming a grievance collection with no entry reaching the early-warning ledger; the patch is that the exercise must produce measurable warning indicators, each entry in the difference list paired with a monitor. All four deaths share one health-check question: when did this device last change how resources were allocated?

    The time structure of the scale problem deserves a passage of its own. Threshold crossings usually happen in the fastest-growing quarter, and the fastest-growing quarter is exactly the quarter when the organization has the least bandwidth for re-validation, with everyone chasing growth. That mismatch decides the design: re-validation has to be scheduled ahead, as an automatic process in which crossing the line queues the work into next quarter's validation calendar, rather than as an ad hoc decision. Scheduling ahead costs a rigid validation calendar, since people have to be freed during growth. Not doing it costs what Cases 1, 3 and 5 cost, and all three crossings happened in growth quarters with no re-validation.

    24.6 Boundaries with Adjacent Mechanisms

    Against extrapolating past the validated domain, Chapter 23. A change of scale is the item most often left off an extrapolation list. Chapter 23 governs whether the differences got listed; this chapter governs how they are re-validated once listed. Pilot to rollout goes through Chapter 23's declaration form, and every crossing of a magnitude step goes through this chapter's re-validation.

    Against cascading failure, Chapter 33. Dependency concentration at networked scale is cascading's ground. This chapter governs the nonlinearity of coordination and validation; cascading governs a single point amplified through the network. A platform in growth should read the two together.

    Against diffusion of responsibility, Chapter 18. The habitual answer to scale is more people and more signatures, and a longer signature chain is exactly what manufactures dilution. Treating this chapter's disease with Chapter 18's disease is the commonest self-injury of a growing organization. Responsibility design during expansion uses a single accountable person, not added signatures.

    24.7 Five Self-Checks

    1. Which magnitude step is your organization on now? At the last crossing, which core assumptions were re-validated?
    2. What is coordination's share of core hours? Is there a number? Having no number is itself the answer.
    3. The most recent reset of a capacity or review-throughput assumption: did data trigger it, or an incident?
    4. What process did a veteran last describe with "it didn't use to be like this"? When did that process last run as designed?
    5. If scale doubled tomorrow, what breaks first? Does what you are betting on match the headroom you have set?
