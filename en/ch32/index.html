    Chapter 32 Compliant and Unsafe (Hollnagel: Safety-I and Safety-II)

    What this chapter covers: why a system that passes every item can still be lethal; Hollnagel's split between Safety-I and Safety-II, three sites, and the scenario inspection that follows from it. When to come back to it: after every check passed and the accident happened anyway; whenever someone proposes adding one more compliance requirement. Reading time: about 50 minutes.

    A compliance system makes a promise it never states out loud: meet every item and you are safe. Erik Hollnagel calls this mode of inspection, which looks for failure and assigns blame, Safety-I, and he names its blind spot. It can prove the absence of a violation. It cannot prove safety. Safety is never the sum of clauses; it is how the system performs as a whole in real situations. The seams between clauses, the new situations no clause covers, the way a clause gets carried out on a wrong premise: all three sit outside Safety-I's field of view. The three sites in this chapter show what that blind spot costs, three times over. One is a physical gap you can compute. One is a wrong classification. One is a consent mechanism that went on violating the rules after two rounds of remediation.

    1 What It Is

    Definition: compliant and unsafe describes the structure in which an inspection system passes item by item while an accident still happens. Rules are written clause by clause, inspections are run clause by clause, every individual pass is real, nobody owns the space between clauses, and the compliance record gradually takes the place of safety judgment as the proof of safety (Hollnagel, Safety-I and Safety-II: Safety Thinking in Action, Ashgate, 2014; the Crossref record has been checked).

    Three trigger criteria. Any one of them means the mechanism is already running:

    ① Every inspection item passes and the accident happens anyway; ② Nobody owns the gaps between compliant items: no position signs for "is the whole thing safe"; ③ The compliance record starts being used as proof of safety ("we meet every standard" becomes the final answer to a challenge about risk).

    2 How It Grows

    Three generating conditions.

    Condition one is the historical path by which rules are written. Regulations and standards grow naturally in clause form. A clause is a unit you can inspect, and inspection can only see units. Condition two is the cost structure of checking. Item-by-item checking can be standardized, outsourced and documented; a whole-system assessment cannot, and so it loses ground in every budget round. Condition three is self-protection in the assignment of responsibility. For the inspector, "each item compliant" is the safest conclusion available; for the inspected, "each item meets standard" is the cheapest defence available. Supply and demand push the inspection system toward clauses together, and the end point of that push is criterion ③, where the compliance certificate replaces the safety judgment outright.

    Hollnagel's distinction deserves a second layer, because Safety-II is often misread as a soft cultural slogan. His original argument is an engineering one. Safety-I, the search for failure, is mathematically condemned to a blind spot. Failure is a low-probability event, so inspecting for failure means inspecting for the absence of a sparse sample. Success is the normal state, and the normal state is where the system's real way of working is stored. So the operational definition of Safety-II is observation first. Treat the ordinary, successful way of working as the object of study. Which workarounds, which deviations, which on-the-spot combinations let the system run every day. Where the workarounds cluster is where the map of the gaps between rules lies. Safety-I can never draw that map, because inspecting for failure only ever marks failure points. This is the theoretical base of Device 3 in this chapter, sampling: sampling success is not for reward, it is for the map.

    One more correction, in the other direction. Safety-II does not replace Safety-I; compliance inspection certainly continues. It adds a layer of whole-system judgment on top. The relation is the one between a medical check and a life. The check answers whether there is disease; the life answers whether the body is well. A person who only has check-ups and never looks at how they live can go down with every number in range, which is the whole meaning of the three cases in this chapter. The combined system has a ready name, borrowed from the sentence pattern of Chapter 11: a clause ledger plus a scenario ledger. The formats of both ledgers are delivered in section 5.

    3 Field Sites

    Case 1 (Aviation): A Valid Certificate and a Flammable Tank

    Case ID: av-036.

    When and where: 17 July 1996, 20:19 US Eastern Daylight Time, takeoff from runway 22R at New York's Kennedy International Airport; 20:31, the aircraft broke up in the air over the Atlantic about 8 miles south of East Moriches on Long Island and went into the sea, at an altitude of about 13,800 feet when the explosion occurred. Before departure it stood at gate 27, Terminal 5, at Kennedy (AAR-00/03 p.19, p.22, p.88).

    What and who: Trans World Airlines, Inc. (the operator, running a scheduled international service from Kennedy to Paris Charles de Gaulle under 14 CFR Part 121); the Boeing Company (the aircraft was a 747-131, part of the 747-100 series, and Boeing held the type certificate); the Federal Aviation Administration (the authority that writes airworthiness standards and issues type certificates). 230 people were aboard: 4 flight crew (captain, first officer and check captain, flight engineer, flight engineer and check captain), 14 flight attendants, 212 passengers (p.19, p.22).

    What they were doing: The aircraft had left Athens at 05:37 that day as TW881 and landed at Kennedy at 16:31, at gate 27 by 16:38. TW800 had been scheduled to depart at 19:00 and was delayed by a ground service vehicle that broke down and blocked the stand, and by a question about a passenger and a bag that matched (p.19). From 16:38 to 20:19 the aircraft sat at the gate with the auxiliary power unit and two of its three air conditioning packs running, about two and a half hours, which was standard TWA ground practice (p.19, p.34 n.33). The work done in that window was turnaround support: fuelling, loading, cabin cleaning, crew change. The centre wing tank had been full at the Athens departure and the transatlantic leg had burned it down to about 300 pounds; it arrived at Kennedy with that much in it, no fuel was added to it during refuelling, and after 16:38 nothing further was done to that tank. Total fuel after refuelling was 181,800 pounds (pp. 60–61). In later tests the report describes the quantity as "50 gallons, similar to TW800" (p.148 n.249), against a tank capacity of 12,890 gallons (p.159 n.287). On the 747-100 the centre wing tank sits inside the centre section structural box of the wing, directly above the bay holding the three air conditioning packs, and the hot air those packs discharge passes through louvers in the bay bulkhead into that bay (p.30, pp. 34–35). For those two and a half hours there was no insulation between the heat source and the tank, 300 pounds of fuel in the tank, and vapor in the rest of it.

    The decision point: Three, and in each the signatory can be recorded only as a body and a document; the report gives no individual names.

    ① 1969: the FAA installed a nitrogen inerting system on a DC-9 in a flight test. The conclusion was that the system "provided adequate inerting of the fuel tank", but that "the cost of the system at the time outweighed the benefit" (p.215 n.395, quoting the Federal Register notice of proposed rulemaking of 3 April 1997, 62 FR 16013). The same notice cites safety recommendation A-71-59 of 1971: in an accident at New Haven, 27 of the 28 occupants survived the impact and died in the fuel fire and explosion that followed it. The recommendation was classified on 13 November 1985 as "closed, acceptable action".

    ② 8 March 1979: the FAA issued airworthiness directive AD 79-05-04, requiring Teflon sleeving on wiring inside the main fuel tanks of some 747s and repeated inspection. It was triggered by reports of fuel pump wiring chafing through its insulation inside an aluminum conduit and arcing against the conduit hard enough to burn a hole in it. A fleet survey after the directive found many aircraft carrying long runs of wiring "missing up to 80% of its total insulation thickness", and AD 79-06-02 of 19 March extended the inspection to other aircraft (p.230). The remedy was inspect and sleeve.

    ③ The initial certification of the 747-100. In NPRM 99-18 of 29 October 1999 the FAA looked back: at certification, "the degree of fuel tank contamination, and the significance of certain failure modes of the fuel tank system, were not considered to the extent that recent events and service experience have shown to be necessary". The compliance showing at the time was that the fuel quantity indicating system wiring, even in contact with the highest voltage on the aircraft, would not arc under contamination-free conditions (p.237). The proof began from no contamination.

    What was visible then: The crew had the flight manual. Its note on the centre wing tank temperature limit said that running the air conditioning packs could raise the tank temperature by 10°F to 20°F; flight tests afterwards measured 40°F to 60°F (p.220). The cockpit had no centre wing tank temperature indication, so the crew could not read the temperature in the tank at all (p.221). The operator had a continuing airworthiness maintenance programme accepted by the FAA, with tank inspection done as a zonal visual check. NPRM 99-18 records how tank system inspection on transport aircraft had historically been assembled: operational checks, functional checks after maintenance, component overhaul done to avoid delay, and zonal visual checks merged with other maintenance. "Dedicated maintenance instructions to find and correct conditions that reduce fail-safe capability were considered unnecessary, because the existing fail-safe capability was assumed not to degrade in service" (p.237). The airworthiness authority had §25.981, two sentences long: a maximum temperature must be established below the lowest expected fuel autoignition temperature with a safety margin, and no temperature at any place in the tank where fuel ignition could occur may exceed that temperature under all possible operating conditions, failures and malfunctions of any component (p.195). Both halves prove something about temperature. What was learned afterwards, the flammability of the vapor in the tank and the paths by which ignition energy reached it, came from the wreckage recovery, fuel sampling and laboratory combustion testing done after July 1996. The investigation concluded that the most likely ignition source was a short circuit outside the tank that sent excessive voltage into it along the fuel quantity indicating system wiring, and that neither the energy release mechanism nor the point of entry could be determined (p.326).

    What happened: At 20:31:12 on 17 July 1996 the cockpit voice recorder stopped, the last radar return was recorded in the same second, the aircraft broke up at about 13,800 feet, the wreckage fell into the sea about 8 miles south of East Moriches, and all 230 people aboard died (p.19, p.88). The report was adopted on 23 August 2000 (p.3). Back to the three questions. The most common answer to the first is "include flammable vapor in the certification checks". In this case that action has a price on the record: inerting or foam in the tank, at least $20 billion over ten years; targeted ventilation, $3.5 billion over ten years; raising the fuel flash point to 140°F, $15 billion in the United States and $60 billion elsewhere, with a possible aviation fuel shortage (p.234). All of those answers had been put forward, and each time the cost was booked. The item most often missed under the second question is the scope of §25.981. The words "fuel ignition" appear in the clause and read as though ignition were already governed; what the clause governs is a maximum temperature, and whether vapor is present is not in the text. The checkpoint under the third belongs on the conclusion page of the 1969 inerting evaluation: write "technically feasible, cost not justified" as a decision with a review date attached, rather than as a final ruling.

    Options ruled out: Five, and the report records a cost or a reason for each. ① Raise the fuel flash point to 140°F: $15 billion in the United States and $60 billion elsewhere over ten years of fuel manufacture, plus engine redesign and recertification, and the working group judged the change "beyond the current experience base" (p.234). ② Inerting or foam filling: at least $20 billion over ten years, and retrofitting the in-service fleet "very difficult" (p.234). ③ Targeted ventilation: $3.5 billion over ten years, feasible only for the in-service fleet, while a new design can simply move the heat source (p.234). ④ Restrict ground operation of the air conditioning packs and use ground cooling carts instead: the FAA's initial assessment of 3 December 1997 replied that this would have "little effect on the overall exposure" (p.216); a recalculated version on 17 May 2000 had the exposure falling from about 35% to about 25% (p.219); Boeing's service letter of 5 May 2000 only "recommended" the carts where they were "available and practicable", and it never became mandatory. ⑤ Fit temperature probes in the tank with a cockpit display (A-96-177): the FAA judged a tank temperature red line tied to flammability impractical on the existing configuration, and noted that "adding another set of electrical components and wiring inside the tank would introduce another possible ignition source" (p.221). What the five share: each was converted into a ten-year total, and each total went onto the same table as the expected value of an accident.

    Early signals: Five. ① Since 1959 the FAA's list carries at least 26 explosions or fires in the fuel tanks of military and civil transport aircraft, TW800 among them (p.197). Why it was filed away: the mechanisms differed from case to case, and there was no common clause to hang them on. ② 9 May 1976: a 747-131 of the Iranian Air Force suffered a left wing tank explosion on approach to Madrid, all 17 people aboard died, and the wreckage showed the explosion had begun in the left wing tank near a fuel valve (p.197). Why it was filed away: lightning and a JP-4 fuel mix, meaning an external energy source. ③ The fleet survey after AD 79-05-04 of 8 March 1979, with insulation missing up to 80% of total thickness in many places (p.230). What was done: inspect and sleeve. Vapor was not touched. ④ A Boeing study of centre wing tank heating done for the US Air Force (document D226-20582-1) concluded that "the primary heat load appears to come from the high temperature of the air conditioning equipment bay" and recommended insulation between the packs and the tank (p.159). Why it was filed away: it was done for the military E-4B and never entered civil certification or operating documents, and Boeing staff retrieved it only late in the investigation (p.159 n.286). ⑤ The same study mentions an aircraft of a Japanese airline, departing Hawaii, losing centre wing tank fuel feed during climb (p.159). Why it was filed away: it read as a fuel pump performance issue, and the temperature limit in the manual existed to prevent fuel freezing and to guarantee feed on a hot day, not to prevent the vapor from becoming flammable (p.220). All five went the same way. Not one of them could be hung on the checklist of the day.

    The point of escalation: 18 February 1997, when the FAA answered in writing the four recommendations A-96-174 to -177 that the Safety Board had issued two months earlier. The reply stated that the current airworthiness standards "assume that the fuel vapor in the tank is flammable, and that the design requirement is to eliminate potential ignition sources in the tank". Controlling the flammability of the vapor "would require a major change in design concepts", and holding fuel temperature below the flammable range was "a major task with significant economic impact" (pp. 214–215). On 3 April 1997 the notice went out for comment: 340 commenters, 976 pages. Public comment concentrated on the need to act; industry comment concentrated on technical soundness and cost (p.216). On 23 July 1998 the FAA's Fuel Tank Harmonization Working Group concluded that bringing the whole fleet down to the flammability level of the wing tanks was a worthwhile goal, but that "retrofitting the in-service fleet is not currently feasible" (p.218). On 21 September 1999 the Safety Board recorded that two and a half years after the recommendations went out, "no design changes to the aircraft have been made", and classified A-96-174 and -175 as "open, unacceptable response" (pp. 217–218). No hearing took place between these four documents; the whole exchange was in writing. This is the step from a known gap to a point of no return. The framework wrote its own premise into the reply, and from then on the gap was part of the premise.

    Failure chain: Technical layer: the centre wing tank sits directly above the bay holding the three air conditioning packs, and it held only 300 pounds of fuel. Two and a half hours of ground heating pushed the fuel temperature into the 104°F to 122°F band, which at the pressure of 13,800 feet is already above the lower flammability limit of Jet A; in the laboratory the fuel ignited at 96.4°F (p.150). The fuel quantity indicating system wiring carried the energy of a short circuit outside the tank into it, the vapor ignited, and the aircraft broke up. Organizational layer: §25.981 governs the relation between temperature and autoignition temperature and says nothing about whether vapor is present (p.195); the compliance showing at initial certification started from "no contamination" (p.237); the maintenance programme assumed that the fail-safe capability of the original design would not degrade in service (p.237); failure mode and effects analysis and fault tree analysis were used as proof that ignition sources had been eliminated, and the investigation states plainly that they "should not be used as the sole means" (p.325, conclusion 19). The report also records that the condition of this aircraft's wiring system "was not unusual for an airplane of this age" (p.326, conclusion 23). Incentive layer: the three paths to lower flammability cost between $3.5 billion and $20 billion over ten years, and the cost-benefit table converted "do nothing for ten years" into "equivalent to three TW800s" and still judged that acceptable; this is the position the working group's consumer representative quoted word for word in a letter of dissent (p.235 n.414). The break at the junction sits between the organizational and the incentive layers. Because the rules wrote flammable vapor in as a premise, no spending to reduce flammability could be booked as correcting a noncompliance. It could only be booked as a new cost.

    Minimum counterfactual: 1969, in §25.981, the FAA writes that the fuel tanks of new transport aircraft types must reduce the flammability of the vapor, and does not judge inerting cost-ineffective. One action, on the conclusion page of that DC-9 test, and no luck required: the system had already been shown on the same DC-9 to "provide adequate inerting", and the installation and its operating data sat in the same document (p.215 n.395). The action needed no new experiment, no fleet survey, and no one's goodwill. What was missing was the term of the judgment "cost outweighs benefit": written as a requirement for new types, rather than as a one-off retrofit of the entire in-service fleet. Run it the other way and the chances in 1979, 1988 and 1996 each added to the ignition source list without any of them reaching flammability; every extension of the list extended the gap with it. Had the clause existed in 1969, certifying the 747-100 would have required answering why vapor is allowed to be there, and the space above those 300 pounds of fuel on 17 July 1996 would not have been flammable.

    Outcome and sources: 230 people died, the aircraft was destroyed, and its insured value was $11 million. The report was adopted on 23 August 2000 and found the probable cause to be an explosion caused by ignition of flammable vapor in the centre wing tank, with the design and certification concept of preventing tank explosions solely by eliminating all ignition sources listed as a contributing factor (p.326). Conclusion 20 states that this concept "is fundamentally flawed, because experience has shown that all possible ignition sources cannot be predicted and reliably eliminated" (p.325). The report also issues 5 new recommendations, A-00-105 to -108, and records the disposition of each of the four 1996 recommendations (pp. 327–328). By the time the report was adopted, measures to reduce flammability were still at the study and comment stage, and the 1999 notice of proposed rulemaking covered new designs only (p.241). Primary source: NTSB, Aircraft Accident Report NTSB/AAR-00/03, adopted August 23, 2000, link at Appendix B av-036.

    Case 2 (Chemical and Process): A Dust Workshop Whose Classification Passed

    Case ID: ch-008.

    When and where: 07:34 on 2 August 2014, at 189 Nanhe Road, Kunshan Economic and Technological Development Zone, Kunshan, Suzhou, Jiangsu Province: polishing workshop No. 2, that is Building 4, of Kunshan Zhongrong Metal Products Co., Ltd. The workshop had a floor area of 2,145 square metres, 44.24 metres north to south and 24.24 metres east to west, a two-storey reinforced concrete frame with a storey height of 4.5 metres and 3 bays on each floor (report I(2)1).

    What and who: Kunshan Zhongrong Metal Products Co., Ltd (wholly owned by Taiwanese capital, registered capital US$8.8 million, designed annual capacity 500,000 pieces, main business revenue in 2013 RMB 165 million); the designer of Building 4, the Huai'an Architectural Design Institute of Jiangsu Province; the builder, the Lianshui County Construction and Installation Company of Jiangsu Province; the general contractor for the dust removal system, Kunshan Lingzheng Electromechanical Environmental Protection Equipment Co., Ltd; the drawing review body, the development zone office of Liyue Drawing Review Company; the fire design review and acceptance authority, the Kunshan Public Security Fire Brigade; the completion acceptance filing authority, the quality supervision station of the Kunshan Housing and Urban-Rural Development Bureau (report I(1), III(2)).

    What they were doing: The workshop was running its daily output of wheel hub grinding and polishing. It was designed for 32 production lines, 16 on each floor, 12 stations per line, 384 stations in all; in fact 13 lines ran on the first floor and 16 on the second, 348 stations, with all grinding and polishing done by hand using hand-held electric grinders. Each floor's 1,072.5 square metres carried 16 lines, each 13-metre line carried 12 stations, some adjacent lines had workers standing back to back less than 1 metre apart, and hubs were stacked in the aisles. The layout and the choice of equipment were done by the general manager from his own experience, with no formal design (report I(2)2, III(2)1(1)). Dust removal was 8 systems built in March 2006, one system shared by every 4 lines and 48 stations, using mechanically shaken bag filters; after a modification in 2012 all 8 outdoor discharge ducts were connected together and discharged through a single main duct. Neither the dust equipment and collection ducts nor the hand tool sockets and their distribution boxes had any grounding (I(2)3). The workshop had no dust cleaning regime: the first direct cause found in the report is that "the dust removal system in the accident workshop had not been cleaned as required for a long period, and aluminum dust had accumulated", and the management cause states that "the enterprise did not clean the dust in time as required, leaving large amounts of aluminum dust in the dust ducts and in the work area" (III(1), III(2)1(3)). The workshop had 250 people on its books before the accident, working 07:00 to 19:00; 265 were on site when it happened (I(2)4, I(3)).

    The decision point: Four, and in each the signatory can be recorded only as a post and a document; the report records every individual by post and by the dates that post was held.

    ① June 2004, when construction of phase three began and the fire hazard category of Building 4 was set at the design stage as Class E. The designer's basis was the use of the building as a "metal products grinding workshop", as supplied by Zhongrong. The report finds that the designer, "without seriously understanding the hazards of the various metal dusts", took one statement of intended use supplied by Zhongrong and improperly classified the workshop as Class E (III(2)4).

    ② The drawing review, which took place before February 2005 (the report dates it by the tenure of the deputy director in charge, April 2000 to February 2005), and whose conclusion was Class E. The report finds that the building "should have been approved as Class B but was approved as Class E", and that "there were errors in the review procedure" (III(2)3(4), IV(2)35).

    ③ The fire design review and acceptance. The building went into use in 2005, and the review and acceptance by the Kunshan Public Security Fire Brigade "failed to find and correct, in accordance with the Code for Fire Protection Design of Buildings (GBJ16-87, 2001 revision), the erroneous fire hazard classification made by the design department, and the simplified review and acceptance procedures were not strict" (III(2)3(2)).

    ④ The completion acceptance filing. The quality supervision station of the Kunshan Housing and Urban-Rural Development Bureau "filed in violation of the regulations, the preconditions for filing not being met", and the filing materials were missing the environmental approval document (III(2)3(4), IV(2)32). All four shared one input: Class E. Once that input was in force, every later inspection, every approval and every form took it as a premise.

    What was visible then: Design, drawing review and fire all held the same drawing set and the same statement of intended use. The enterprise held the design parameters of the dust system: each hood 500 millimetres by 200 millimetres, the hub centre 500 millimetres from the hood mouth, 600 cubic metres per hour per hood, 28,800 cubic metres per hour per system, and a mean branch duct velocity of 20.8 metres per second (III(2)1(3)). The enterprise also held standards that were already in force. GB17269-2003, the safety regulation for dust explosion protection in aluminum and magnesium powder processing, requires a mean branch velocity of 23 metres per second, which for this layout means 31,850 cubic metres per hour, a shortfall in the original design of 9.6%. GB/T15605-2008, the guide for dust explosion venting, requires venting devices. GB50058-1992, the code for electrical installations in explosive and fire hazardous atmospheres, requires explosion protection and grounding (III(2)1(2), 1(3), 1(5)). The regulator held a dust monitoring report issued by Jiangsu Laibo Environmental Testing Technology Co., Ltd, and the report finds that the company "did not, as GBZ159-2004 requires, sample dust concentration at the polishing positions under normal production conditions before issuing the monitoring report" (III(2)4). What was learned afterwards, the moisture-driven exothermic path in the aluminum powder inside a collection bin, the possible part played by a thermite reaction, and the way the absence of venting and the shared main discharge duct turned one explosion in a bin into an explosion of the whole workshop, came from post-accident site inspection, simulation and bench testing (III(1)).

    What happened: At 07:34 the No. 1 dust collector exploded, the shock wave travelled along the dust ducts into the workshop, and the aluminum dust it raised exploded in series. 47 people died on the spot, 28 died in hospital the same day despite treatment, and 185 were injured (II(1)). Within the 30-day reporting period the count was 97 dead, 163 injured and RMB 351 million of direct economic loss; the report separately records 49 further deaths after the reporting period (opening section). Back to the three questions. The most common answer to the first is "correct the category and redo the whole review". In this case that action costs four retrofits: recompute the venting area on the first floor to Class B, convert the evacuation stairs to enclosed stairwells, make all the electrical installations explosion-proof, and add vents to the dust system and redo the air volumes. The item most often missed under the second question is the monitoring report. It has an issuing body, a conclusion and a signature; the one thing missing is the sentence "sampled under normal production conditions". Because it is formally complete, it is the least gap-like document in the entire set. The checkpoint under the third belongs in the conclusion column of the drawing review: one check, whether the process behind the drawing still matches the category. Change the process and leave the category alone, and every later inspection will pass.

    Options ruled out: The report leaves no record of an options comparison at all, and that absence is itself testimony. What can be seen in the documents is six options already written into the rules at the time, each replaced by a cheaper practice. ① Approve the building as Class B: replaced by deciding on the strength of the intended-use statement the enterprise supplied and nothing else (III(2)4). ② Fit venting devices to the dust system (GB/T15605-2008): none were fitted, and the report finds that "because there was no venting device, the hot gases and combustion products of the explosion were discharged instantly through the dust ducts and out of every hood" (III(1)). ③ Explosion protection and grounding for the electrical installation (GB50058-1992): not done, "none of it explosion-proof", and the cable and wiring installation was non-compliant (III(2)1(5)). ④ Design the branch ducts at 23 metres per second: actual 20.8 metres per second, a shortfall in the original design of 9.6% (III(2)1(3)). ⑤ Give the dust system to a firm qualified to design and install it: it was awarded as a whole to an unqualified firm, which took design, fabrication, installation and the later modification together (III(2)1(2)). ⑥ Test dust concentration under normal production: not done, and the report was issued anyway (III(2)4). The six replacements all point the same way: work that needed professional judgment was exchanged for a form that could be signed.

    Early signals: Five. ① The Kunshan development zone had "seen several metal dust deflagration accidents" before this one, and local supervision did not draw the lesson and organize a full survey (III(2)2(1)). How it was pushed aside: the zone's accidents were never converted into an inspection item for this enterprise. ② In 2004 workshops 3 and 4 were built without approval and without environmental impact procedures, and the environmental inspection squadron "did not find and correct this" (IV(2)31). How it was pushed aside: built first, paperwork afterwards. ③ In 2007 the environmental facilities for two new production lines, and in 2012 the technical modification of the dust system in Building 4, neither went through completion acceptance (IV(2)28, 29). How it was pushed aside: modification was scheduled to production, acceptance was scheduled behind it. ④ In 2013 the Jiangsu provincial fire service ran a campaign on fire safety in illegal buildings, and the Kunshan Public Security Fire Brigade "implemented it poorly and did not identify the problems at Zhongrong" (III(2)3(2)). How it was pushed aside: the campaign worked from a roster, and this plant was not on the roster. ⑤ The Work Safety Committee Office of the State Council set up a special programme on safety in aluminum and magnesium processing enterprises. The Kunshan safety supervision bureau did not verify the township statistics, "so that Zhongrong was not entered on the list of aluminum and magnesium processing enterprises and did not carry out the special programme as required", and the second safety supervision division of the Suzhou bureau "did not forward the superior's work plan for the special programme" (III(2)3(1), IV(2)16). How it was pushed aside: the document stopped at municipal level. The five have the same shape. Every campaign really happened, and every one of them walked past this building.

    The point of escalation: 2012, when the dust system in Building 4 was modified and all 8 outdoor discharge ducts were connected into one main duct (I(2)3). The work went to the unqualified Kunshan Lingzheng; neither the ducts nor the collectors were given vents, no grounding was installed to drain static, and no environmental completion acceptance was organized afterwards (III(2)1(2), III(2)3(3)). The motive is not stated in the report; what can be verified is the arrangement after the modification and the firm that carried it out. Before this step, the 8 systems were 8 units that could each relieve pressure on its own. After it, an explosion in any collection bin had a duct path to all 348 stations. The report describes the consequence this way: the shock wave travelled along the dust ducts into the workshop, the dust it raised exploded in series, and "because there was no venting device, the hot gases and combustion products of the explosion were discharged instantly through the dust ducts and out of every hood" (III(1)). The date of the modification is recorded only to the year; the report gives no month or day.

    Failure chain: Technical layer: the collection bin of the No. 1 dust collector was corroded through, and about 20 kilograms of aluminum powder had accumulated in it. Rain had fallen continuously for the two days before, with a mean temperature of 31°C, a maximum of 34°C and humidity peaking at 97%; moist air entered through the hole in the bin floor, the aluminum powder absorbed the moisture and oxidized exothermically, and the iron oxide produced by the corrosion could support a thermite reaction once the earlier heat had triggered it, raising the temperature to the ignition temperature of the dust cloud. At 07:10 the dust extraction fan started, creating negative pressure and a dust cloud above the bin; at 07:34 it ignited (III(1)). Organizational layer: the fire hazard category of Building 4 was set as Class E in 2004, so the whole set of requirements that follows from a category, venting area, stair form, electrical explosion protection class and vents on the dust system, never entered that workshop's inspection list (III(2)1(1)); the enterprise was not on the aluminum and magnesium processing list, so the special programme did not cover it (III(2)3(1)); repeated safety inspections "all failed to find the major hazard that dust at this company had long exceeded limits and could cause an explosion" (III(2)3(1)). Incentive layer: the single shift from 07:00 to 19:00, the 348 stations, the back-to-back spacing under 1 metre and the hubs stacked in the aisles are all the shape of capacity; the dust system modification went to an unqualified firm and was not accepted afterwards (III(2)1(1), 1(2)). The break at the junction sits between the organizational and the incentive layers. One classification decision lowered the compliance cost and the safety level at the same time, and both effects are recorded on the same event: the review passed.

    Minimum counterfactual: At the design review in 2004, approve Building 4 as Class B. One action, in the conclusion column of the drawing review, and no luck required: that polishing aluminum wheel hubs produces combustible aluminum dust was not new knowledge in 2004 and needed no experiment at this plant, and the category table of the fire code was in the reviewer's hands; the report's own wording is that the building "should have been approved as Class B". Change the category and four requirements trigger at once, the venting area on the first floor, the enclosed stairwell, explosion-proof electrical installations and vents on the dust system, and all four fall before the building is occupied, without stopping production for a minute. The cost of this step can be checked: the category table, the drawings and the review record were all on the desk in 2004, and the change happens on paper. In the other direction, after the building went into use in 2005 the same change means stopping lines, altering structure and redoing fire acceptance. None of the four happened in the nine years before the accident.

    Outcome and sources: 97 dead and 163 injured within the 30-day reporting period, RMB 351 million of direct economic loss; judicial measures were taken against 18 people, disciplinary action within the Party and the administration was recommended against 35, the Jiangsu Provincial People's Government was to be criticized in a circular and required to report to the State Council, and Zhongrong was fined at the upper limit (IV). The accident was classified as a production safety responsibility accident (III(3)); part five of the report sets out the rectification requirements. Primary source: the State Council investigation team for the "8·2" particularly serious explosion at Kunshan Zhongrong Metal Products Co., Ltd, "Investigation Report on the '8·2' Particularly Serious Explosion Accident at Kunshan Zhongrong Metal Products Co., Ltd, Kunshan, Suzhou, Jiangsu Province", published on the website of the Ministry of Emergency Management on 30 December 2014, link at Appendix B ch-008.

    The two rounds of remediation in case three still owe a layer of psychological reading. At the end of the second round the team was certain it met the requirements, since every remediation item carried a closure record, and the source of that certainty is the self-reinforcement of compliance records: the internal version of criterion ③, in which the remediation record becomes proof of safety. What breaks self-reinforcement is an outside view, here the users' true consent rate, meaning not that users clicked agree but that they really knew and were really free. Measuring true consent has an established method in user research, the comprehension test, which has users restate what they agreed to; the method costs far less than the €325 million of tuition, and it is absent because the compliance list never carried that item. The list asks whether a consent mechanism exists, not whether consent actually happened. Adding one line to the list costs one user study, and that line is where Safety-II lands in data compliance.

    The common point of the three cases can be made precise here. Their safety gaps all grew in the blind spot of the question form: TWA800 did not ask about vapor, Kunshan did not ask how far the category was from reality, and the CNIL case did not ask whether consent was real. That blind spot is structural, because the question list comes from past accidents, and a new class of gap is never on the list before the next accident. The only remedy is Device 2 in this chapter, the scenario inspection: the scenario is new, so the questions follow the scenario. What scenario inspection really does is keep the question form fluid. Clauses are static, scenarios are dynamic, and only a dynamic question lights up a new gap between static clauses.

    Case 3 (Software): Still Non-Compliant After Remediation

    sw-007. Google LLC and Google Ireland. The CNIL decision (pages read for this book): September 2025, a total fine of €325 million for inserting advertisements between emails in Gmail without consent, and for cookies set when accounts were created without valid, freely given, informed consent (Google LLC €200 million, Google Ireland €125 million). The decision records a more telling sequence: two rounds of remediation had already been carried out, and the authority found the violations still in place. The compliance process was running in full: remediate, re-inspect, remediate again. The whole question, whether users really consented and did so informed and free, did not change with item-by-item remediation. Remediation changed how clauses were implemented. What was violated was the overall design of the consent mechanism.

    Stop at the decision point: the moment the second remediation plan was signed off and the team confirmed that it met the requirements. On the table: the item-by-item closure of the remediation list, the true consent rate from user research, and the compliance status of each of those two sets of information. 〔Outcome〕A €325 million fine, and the overall design of the consent mechanism named. Primary source: CNIL, 2025, link at Appendix B sw-007.

    Note on the Evidence Gap

    The fourth case slot in this chapter is reserved for the same shape in agriculture and food (systemic outbreaks under compliance systems such as HACCP; the compliance history of the outbreak cases already held in CDC MMWR needs retrospective verification) and will be filled once collected; the three existing cases already cover aviation, chemical and process, and data compliance. The reserved slot is filled for now by a case from the same library: ag-003, case four of Chapter 13, revisited here with a different reading and replaceable by a deeper HACCP-specific case once collection is complete.

    Case 4 (Agriculture and Food): Cheese Made to a Compliant Process (condensed)

    ag-003. Whatcom Blue, a raw milk cheese from Twin Sisters Creamery. FDA enforcement report (event 97880 / H-0193-2026, API record checked for this book): E. coli O103:H2 detected, Class I recall of 3,438.2 pounds. Making raw milk cheese violates no current process tradition, the compliance narrative of the aging process is complete, and safety rests on a process assumption, that aging inactivates the pathogen. The state laboratory's finished-product test broke the assumption. Compliant and unsafe takes this form here: compliance is about the process, how the cheese is made, and safety is about the result, what is in the cheese; every item of the process passes and the test of the result is absent. Minimum counterfactual: put finished-product testing into the release standard, which puts the safety question onto the compliance list. Primary source: FDA, 2026, link at Appendix B ag-003.

    Case 5 (Drugs and Devices): Voids in a Seal (condensed)

    ph-010. Voids in the Tyvek pouch seal of a surgical pack. FDA recall (December 2025, API record checked for this book): the seal voids put the sterility barrier in doubt, and 14,789 units were recalled (8,651 in the United States, 6,138 outside it). The compliance checks on the packaging (seal parameters, material specification, process records) can all pass; a void is a defect at the level of the result, since a passing parameter does not mean every seal is intact. Sterile barrier integrity is a safety attribute and a parameter is a compliance attribute, and the difference between them is this chapter's definition. Minimum counterfactual: add sampling destructive testing of seal integrity to batch release, trading the destruction of the test for confirmation of the barrier. Primary source: FDA, 2026, link at Appendix B ph-010.

    4 What Shows Earliest

    Six weak signals, each with the reason usually used to push it aside.

    1. The safety meeting's whole agenda is "is there a violation", and no item asks "where could it go wrong". Reason: "compliance is the floor."
    2. The findings of an inspection are all single-clause items, with no systemic finding that crosses clauses. Reason: "inspections are designed by clause."
    3. "Everything meets standard" is used to answer a challenge that has nothing to do with the clauses. Reason: "let the data speak."
    4. The approval route for exceptions and waivers runs more smoothly than the remediation route. Reason: "waivers have a dedicated channel."
    5. A risk raised at the front line has no inspection item to answer to, so it never enters the inspection result. Reason: "no clause, no check."
    6. The inspection clauses added after each accident accumulate year on year; inspection hours double and the accident rate does not move. Reason: "keep strengthening."

    Put the six back against the five cases of this chapter and each one lands somewhere. Signal 1, meetings that only discuss violations, lands on case three: the agenda of every remediation meeting was clause closure, and the authenticity of consent never reached the agenda. Signal 2, no finding that crosses clauses, lands on case one: every finding of the certification inspection was a single-item pass, and the fact of flammable vapor as a whole had no clause to hang on. Signal 3, a compliance answer to a challenge, lands on all the cases: it is criterion ③ in the field. Signal 4, a smoother waiver channel, lands on case four: the waiver for a process tradition, raw milk without pasteurization, ran through a cultural channel and not a testing one. Signal 5, front-line risk with no item to hang on, lands on case two: the maintenance arrears of the dust system had no line on the Class E checklist. Signal 6, clauses accumulating while the accident rate holds, is the long-run portrait of a Safety-I system: the clauses grow, and the gaps grow with them.

    Reorder the six by the cost of the evidence. Signals 1 and 3 are cheapest, since they need only the minutes and the reply texts. Signals 2 and 4 are middling, needing the finding structure of the inspection reports. Signals 5 and 6 are the most expensive, needing statistics that cross clauses and a historical comparison. The place to start is signal 3. The next time "everything meets standard" is offered as an answer about safety, ask one follow-up: do the compliant items, taken together, cover the scenario in front of us today. The follow-up costs one sentence, and it leaves a challenged mark on every run of criterion ③ in the organization.

    5 How to Defend

    Device 1, the extra question after the compliance check. On the conclusion page of every compliance inspection, append one fixed question: "Taken together, are these compliant items safe?" The answer has to come from a person who signs for the whole (Chapter 18, the single accountable person), and it has to cite at least one cross-clause integrated analysis, a scenario walkthrough or a joint failure analysis. One question does not close the gap. It puts the gap into the text.

    Device 2, the scenario inspection. Once a year, switch the inspection system from clause mode to scenario mode: pick three real accident scenarios, from your own library or the industry's, walk every relevant clause and the site along the scenario, and record the stretches the clauses do not reach. Those stretches are the map boundary of the system. The misclassified category at Kunshan, the flammable vapor on TWA800 and the overall design of consent at the CNIL are all places on that boundary.

    Device 3, Safety-II sampling. At regular intervals, collect how things go right. This is observation rather than violation hunting: a record of the difference between the actual practice of an ordinary successful day and the rule, which is Hollnagel's method in operational form. Most of these differences are harmless and a minority sit exactly on the gap. They can only be seen from success, because failure exposes the failure point and success exposes the whole way of working.

    Four devices against this chapter's cases. Device 1, the extra question, on case one: the question on the certification conclusion page writes down the physical fact of vapor flammability and where the residual risk sits. Device 2, the scenario inspection, on case two: walk the dust explosion scenario through every clause that applies to the workshop, and record the list of stretches the Class E standard does not reach. Device 3, Safety-II sampling, on case three: sample how users actually complete the consent flow, how many screens they skip and how many points they misread, and the place where the workarounds cluster is the gap map of the consent mechanism. Device 4, the use prohibition, on all the cases: write the ban on compliance answers into the spokesperson's handbook and the rules of internal meetings.

    Each of the four devices has a way of dying. The extra question dies in watered answers, the word "safe" filling the blank; the patch is a citation requirement, so that an answer must cite the number of the scenario walkthrough and an unnumbered answer is returned automatically. The scenario inspection dies in selection bias, the same three old scenarios every year, where the new gap cannot be found; the patch is rotation with outside input, half the scenarios taken from the industry's latest accidents. Safety-II sampling dies in sampling drift, where sampling becomes an award ceremony that records good deeds and not workarounds; the patch is anonymization, so that a record carries the place and the practice and not a name. The use prohibition dies in rephrasing, the compliance answer returning as "we went through all the necessary reviews"; the patch is a semantic version of the ban, so that answering a question about overall safety with the completeness of inspection is prohibited whatever the wording. All four patches share one check sentence: when did the scenario inspection last find a new stretch the clauses do not reach. A scenario inspection that has never found one has turned the scenarios back into clauses.

    The formats of the two ledgers are delivered here. The clause ledger stays as it is: item, compliance status, inspection date, governing clause. The scenario ledger adds four columns: scenario (the name of a real accident or an industry accident scenario), coverage judgment (the stretches covered and the breakpoints left after the clause chain is walked through the scenario), breakpoint disposition (whether the breakpoint went into a clause revision or was listed as a known boundary), review date (the annual re-walk of the scenario). The two ledgers have different readers, the clause ledger for the inspector and the scenario ledger for the designer and the governance layer, and the scenario ledger's slot on the quarterly agenda should sit before the compliance report. Look at the gaps first and the compliance second, and the language of the meeting changes with the order.

    Device 4, the prohibition on using compliance as proof. Answering a challenge about safety with "everything meets standard" is prohibited; the answer has to point to a scenario-based integrated analysis. This prohibition changes a language habit, and until the habit changes, criterion ③ is already running in every organization.

    6 Boundaries with Adjacent Mechanisms

    Against regulatory lag and capture (Chapter 31). Lag and capture supply the compliance system with its stale rules, which is the climate; this chapter runs inside the old rules, which is the weather. The longer the old rules run, the louder Chapter 32's "everything meets standard" sounds.

    Against the illusion of redundancy (Chapter 22). The illusion is redundancy's same-source error, counting the number and not the kind; compliant and unsafe is the clause-ization of inspection, counting clauses and not scenarios. Both share one audit habit: counting the countable and ignoring the uncountable.

    Against normalization of deviance (Chapter 11). The form normalization takes inside an inspection system is criterion ③, answering a challenge about risk with the compliance record. Chapter 11 watches the register; this chapter watches the language of meetings.

    7 Five Self-Checks

    1. At the last accident in your field, did every compliance check pass beforehand? What share of the causal chain did those passing checks cover?
    2. How many items on your checklist were added ten years ago? When did each of them last stop a problem?
    3. When was your organization's compliance certificate last used to answer a challenge about safety, and on what occasion? Who asked about the scenario as a whole?
    4. If next year's budget halved the compliance inspection and doubled the scenario walkthroughs, would the visibility of risk in your organization rise or fall?
    5. Take one scenario you own, a fire, an outage, a contamination, and walk it along the accident chain through all your clauses: how long is the written list of stretches the clauses do not reach?
