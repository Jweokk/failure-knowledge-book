    Chapter 16 Information Attenuation up the Hierarchy | MUM Effect

    What this chapter covers: how bad news is softened on its way up; the psychology and the structure behind the MUM effect, three start criteria, scenes from four domains, and the design points of a channel that skips the hierarchy. When to turn back: when you notice that the top's read of a situation plainly disagrees with the front line; before you write a report that says "overall under control"; when you are designing a reporting system. Estimated reading time: 50 minutes.

    Bad news travels through an organization in a way that one sentence can hold: every floor it climbs, it puts on a more presentable coat. "Both units have a major safety hazard" arrives at the department manager as "there is some risk", at the director as "worth watching", at the executive vice president as "overall under control", and by the time it reaches the person who can release the money, the thing no longer looks like itself. Organizational psychology has a sour and accurate name for this: the MUM effect, mutual unwillingness to uphold bad news upward. The sourness is in "mutual". The sender does not want to report, the receiver does not want to hear, and the two of them complete every step of the softening together. No step in that sequence requires anyone to lie.

    The experimental origin is worth stating. Sidney Rosen and Abraham Tesser named the phenomenon in a series of studies around 1970, and the core fact they measured was this: the discomfort of delivering bad news is large enough to change delivery behaviour even when the truth of the message has nothing to do with the messenger's interests. Unwillingness to report sits close to the default setting. What an organization has to do is build channels against that default, not ask its members to overcome discomfort. That placement decides the shape of every defence in this chapter: they are structural devices, and not one of them depends on courage.

    The moral asymmetry between attenuation and lying deserves to be spelled out, because it is the reason this mechanism is so hard to govern. A lie has an author, a moment and a content, and can be traced to someone. Attenuation has none of that. Each layer's wording survives inspection ("I reported what the department knew"), and no layer has lied. Moral judgement fails here. Informational judgement still works: put the versions from each level side by side and the points where intensity drops are plain to see. The defences in this chapter therefore point at material that can be checked (minutes, originals, numbers), and not at anyone's attitude.

    The receiving end's unwillingness to hear needs a paragraph of its own, because it is usually reduced to a matter of temperament. Three structural sources feed it. The first is asymmetric burden: the person who hears bad news is expected to solve it, and plenty of bad news (market shifts, policy changes, technology routes) lies outside the listener's remit. Every hearing loads a pack onto his back, and the appetite drains. The second is attribution inertia: the person who brings bad news is easily read as the person who creates problems, and the stronger the association in organizational memory between bearer and trouble, the less the receiving end wants to hear. The third is agenda pressure: bad news has no seat. Agendas are organized around the period's targets, so bad news can always be deferred to next time, and by next time its wording is one layer lighter. The devices aimed at the receiving end sit in Section 16.5, Devices 1 and 2: side-by-side reading turns hearing into an obligation, and a named reader turns hearing into a post.

    16.1 What It Is

    Definition: information attenuation up the hierarchy is the systematic dilution of bad news as it climbs. Each level makes a small wording choice, for self-protection or out of consideration for the person above, and the choices compound until the decision-maker's version and the front-line version have drifted apart. Nobody has to lie for this to happen. That is the distance between attenuation and falsification, and the reason it resists governance: you cannot discipline a behaviour assembled entirely from compliant wording.

    Three start criteria. Any one of them means the mechanism is running.

    1. The same piece of bad news reaches different levels at different intensity. Lay the minutes side by side and check.
    2. The decision layer's judgement of the situation has visibly diverged from the front-line version. The front line laughs when it hears what the top thinks.
    3. Skipping a level is defined in the organization's language as disloyalty, as not knowing how things work, or at minimum as "talk to your manager first".

    16.2 How It Grows

    Three generation conditions. Condition one: the reporter's interests are bound to what he reports. When the severity of the news decides his appraisal, his bonus and his prospects, the wording tilts light. This is Chapter 17's misaligned incentives projected onto the information layer: the message takes the colour of the reporter's interests rather than the colour of the facts. Condition two: many layers, narrow bandwidth. Each layer is one compressed retelling, compression is lossy, and the direction of the loss is set by the reporter's interests. A layer is not a relay station. It is a filter, and each filter takes out a little, little enough that nobody is answerable for it, enough that the accumulation is lethal. Condition three: no receiving mechanism for bad news. The organization runs channels that reward good news (wins, highlights, targets beaten) and no channel through which a problem can be reported without cost. Where the receiving end sets no place, the sending end trims the message to fit.

    The concealment is worth repeating: every step is small. From "serious accident hazard" to "some risk" to "overall under control", each choice survives examination inside a single meeting. Only five sets of minutes read side by side make the slide visible. Side-by-side reading is the core action of this chapter's defence.

    The arithmetic behaves like compound interest. Suppose each layer passes on eighty per cent of the intensity, which is a conscientious layer by the standards of most organizations. After five layers the decision layer receives a little more than one fifth of the original. Every one of those five layers believes it merely distilled the message. This arithmetic explains why training people to "report more faithfully" does nothing: no layer is skimming anything beyond its eighty per cent, and the problem lives in the multiplication. Multiplication admits two remedies. Cut the number of layers, which is the direct channel. Or keep a raw value in a path that does not pass through them, which is the original document delivered unedited. Devices 1 and 2 in Section 16.5 are those two remedies.

    Good news and bad news do not climb at the same speed, and the gap is one of the steadier findings in this literature. Good news moves fast, because every layer is glad to pass it on. Bad news moves slowly, because every layer wants to digest it, dress it and pick a moment. The speed gap is itself a probe: the arrival-time difference between the two measures how deep the attenuation runs. The practical measurement: take one group of good-news items and one group of bad-news items of the same class, count the average days from origin to decision-layer minutes, and the ratio of the two is the organization's attenuation index. The index does not need precision, only trend. Two consecutive quarters in which bad news slows down, and criterion three is very likely met.

    Trust is the moderator that keeps turning up in the evidence. Every notch down in a reporter's trust in the receiver adds a layer of self-protection to his wording. In an organization short on trust, attenuation is not a discipline problem. It is the individually rational choice of every reporter. Trust builds slowly and is spent in one transaction: a single instance of being held to account for bad news wipes out ten years of speaking freely, and the count restarts at zero. That asymmetry is why Device 3, the immunity clause, has to be carried by the institution and not by a leader's verbal promise. A verbal promise is honoured at the moment of accountability, and the discretion at that moment is precisely what the promise cannot bind. Institutional immunity goes into two documents, the performance one and the accountability one, and any blank left in either will be cited in the first real case.

    16.3 Shapes in Four Domains

    Case 1 (Chemical & Process): The Information Chain after the Report Came In

    Case ID: ch-007.

    When and where: 22 November 2013, 02:12 to 10:25; the junction of Qinhuangdao Road and Zhaitangdao Street, Qingdao Economic and Technological Development Zone, Shandong, where the Donghuang crude oil pipeline crosses the municipal drainage culvert; the information chain reaches the Weifang oil transport division dispatch centre, the zone main duty office and the Qingdao city main duty office.

    What and who: The pipeline storage and transportation branch of Sinopec Corp, with its Weifang oil transport division, Qingdao oil transport station and Huangdao tank farm; the Qingdao Economic and Technological Development Zone management committee with its emergency office, safety supervision bureau and municipal bureau; the Qingdao city main duty office and the municipal economy and information technology commission. Responsibility attaches to posts: the duty dispatcher in the dispatch centre, the marketing and sales section, the division head and his deputy, the Qingdao station master, the zone emergency office director and deputy director, and the committee's deputy director in charge.

    What they were doing: The Donghuang pipeline went into service in July 1986, 248.5 km long, 711 mm in diameter, design throughput 20 million tonnes a year, reversed to opposite flow in October 1998 at 10 million tonnes a year (report p. 2). The accident section passed overhead under the south half of the Qinhuangdao Road bridge culvert roof, 110 cm from the top of the pipe to the culvert roof slab and 148 cm from the bottom of the pipe to the culvert floor (p. 2). At the time of the accident the line carried light crude with a closed-cup flash point of −16 °C, running at full load, outlet pressure 4.67 MPa, outlet temperature 27.8 °C (p. 2). The Weifang division tested the external coating and local pipe body in 2009, 2011 and 2013, and none of the three found serious corrosion in the accident section (p. 6). A coating overhaul was scheduled from 2011, and by October 2013 the 15 km containing the leak point had still not been overhauled (p. 6). Over the same period the zone carried out the Qinhuangdao Road comprehensive improvement project without consulting the pipeline company and without calculating the project's effect on pipeline safety (p. 7). The Qingdao station's patrol system was incomplete and the patrol staff lacked the professional knowledge, so no pipeline protection requirement was raised when the municipal open channel was covered and slabbed or when the road was widened and resurfaced; the emergency plan had no drills and the rescue staff did not know their own duties (p. 6). At the Sinopec Group and listed company level the safety inspections had dead corners and blind zones, found no hazard in the accident section and took no protective measure (p. 6). In 2013 the Qingdao city and zone pipeline protection departments ran 6 special rectification inspections, and none of them found the effect of the road works on the pipeline (p. 7).

    The decision point: Four moments.

    One. At 02:12 the supervisory control and data acquisition system in the Weifang division dispatch centre showed the Huangdao tank farm outlet pressure falling from 4.56 MPa to 4.52 MPa. Two telephone calls to the tank farm ruled out any operating cause, and the drop was judged a leak; at 02:25 the pumps were stopped and the line shut down (p. 3). At 02:35 the Qingdao station was told to close the block valve at the Yanghe valve chamber, which was shut around 03:20; the chamber sits 24.5 km from the Huangdao tank farm and was the nearest downstream chamber to the leak (p. 3). At that moment the desk held a pressure drop of 0.04 MPa and one distance to a valve chamber.

    Two. At 02:57 the repair centre was called out. At 03:40 Qingdao station staff were on site and confirmed the leak about 1.5 km from the Huangdao outlet (p. 3). Around 04:00 excavation of the leak point and pipeline repair began, alongside clearing materials and cleaning up oil spilled at sea (p. 3). The excavation came first. Combustible gas detection does not appear in the record (p. 6).

    Three. At 04:47 the marketing and sales section reported to the head of the Weifang division, at 05:07 reported the overall situation to the pipeline company dispatch centre, and at 05:30 the division head sent his deputy to the site (p. 3). At 07:40 a work pit of 2 m by 2 m by 1.5 m was dug out, and at 08:20 the leak point was found and reported (p. 4). At 09:15 the pipeline company gave the order to form an on-site command under the emergency plan (p. 4). From the shutdown to that order, 6 hours and 50 minutes.

    Four. The government ran a second chain. At 02:31 the zone public security sub-bureau's 110 line took a call reporting crude oil near the south gate of Lidong Chemical; at 03:10 the call was passed to the zone main duty office (p. 4). By 04:17 the emergency office, the municipal bureau, the safety supervision bureau, the environmental protection sub-bureau and the Huangdao street office had received it in batches; at 04:51, 07:46 and 07:48 the committee's deputy director, director and party working committee secretary received it (p. 4). At 07:49 it reached the Qingdao city main duty office. Between 08:18 and 08:27 the city duty office telephoned three departments "requiring further verification of the information"; between 08:34 and 08:40 text messages went to the secretary-general and deputy secretary-general; between 09:01 and 09:06 to the mayor and 4 deputy mayors (p. 4). The zone management committee graded the event a general emergency on the basis of the enterprise's report (p. 8), and the report does not state when the grading was made or who signed it.

    What was visible then: On the enterprise side, the pressure drop at 02:12, the shutdown at 02:25, the valve closed at 03:20, the position of the leak at 03:40, the dimensions of the work pit at 07:40, the leak point confirmed at 08:20 (pp. 3–4). On the government side, the content of the 02:31 call, the 03:10 report to the duty office, the units that had received it by 04:17, three zone leaders receiving it between 04:51 and 07:48, the city's demand for verification at 08:18 (p. 4). The units that arrived between 04:10 and 05:00 were the emergency office, the municipal bureau, the safety supervision bureau, the environmental protection sub-bureau and the Huangdao street office, and their work was cleaning oil off the water (p. 4); the report records no department as responsible for the culvert under the road surface. One report stands apart: Lidong Chemical told the zone safety supervision bureau that crude oil had been found in an open channel inside its plant (p. 8), and that information stopped at the bureau, never passed to the government or the other departments. The text messages that went up between 08:34 and 09:06 carried "basic situation of the leak accident" (p. 4); by the time of the explosion no upward text contained a leak volume. The crews on site had excavators and hydraulic breakers, and the record holds no combustible gas reading (p. 6). Every report on the enterprise side carried a time and an action, and none carried a quantity. At 09:01, 1 hour and 24 minutes before the explosion, the mayor was still receiving that basic situation (p. 4). Known only afterwards: the leaked volume of about 2,000 tonnes, established by calculation (p. 5); the 8 hours and more between the leak and the explosion (p. 6); the drainage culvert's total length of about 1,945 m (p. 3); the mechanism of the corrosion thinning (p. 5); the temporary shed Lidong Chemical had put up over the culvert without authorisation (p. 8).

    Stop here. Decide before you turn the page.

    1. What would you do? Write the action, not "pay more attention".
    2. Which item here are you most likely to miss, and why does it look unimportant?
    3. If you may set one checkpoint, when is it and what does it check?

    What happened: At 10:25 the crew on site perforated the culvert cover slab with a hydraulic breaker, and the impact spark ignited the oil vapour inside. The explosion affected more than 5,000 m of the culvert and damaged water, power, heating and gas lines (p. 5). 62 people died, 136 were injured, and direct economic losses came to 751.72 million yuan (p. 1). By 2 December all 62 of the dead had been identified (p. 5). 15 people were subject to judicial measures (pp. 8–9) and 48 were recommended for Party and administrative discipline (pp. 9–15); the Shandong provincial safety supervision bureau was ordered to fine Sinopec Pipeline Company at the statutory maximum and to fine its party secretary and general manager 80% of their 2012 income each (p. 15).

    Back to the three questions. The commonest answer to the first is "raise the response level". This case did grade the event, as a general emergency, and the grading rested on the enterprise report alone, which carried no volume and no direction of flow. That action costs one telephone call and one signature. What was missing is the line of numbers before the signature. To the second question, the item most easily missed is the 02:31 call to 110: it came 5 hours and 18 minutes before the enterprise report reached the city at 07:49, it came from outside the system, and it was never merged into the same chain. The third question lands before the 04:00 excavation, on one gas test.

    Options ruled out: Activating the emergency plan (the report finds no activation order was issued in time, p. 6); raising the response level (not raised after the general grading, p. 8); marking a cordon, closing roads, notifying residents to evacuate (the report finds none of it was done in time, p. 8); combustible gas detection and explosion-proof equipment (the report finds neither was done and non-explosion-proof equipment was used blindly, p. 6); passing Lidong Chemical's report to the government (the report finds it was not passed in time, p. 8); calling in the repair centre (first raised by the deputy division head at 09:30, 55 minutes before the explosion, p. 4). All six carry "not" in the report's wording. Not one carries "considered and rejected": no record shows anyone proposing, anyone vetoing, anyone signing a refusal. The excluded option here takes the form of never reaching the agenda. In its prevention section the report turns these into positive requirements: measure the oil and gas concentration on site, identify and assess the hazards and risks, and mark off a cordon and evacuate the surrounding population in time according to conditions on site (p. 17).

    Early signals: Six items, each with the excuse that carried it past.

    1. Three tests in 2009, 2011 and 2013 found no serious corrosion in the accident section (p. 6). Each closed as "not found", so the hazard ledger stayed empty, and the reason it was pushed past was "three tests in three years".
    2. The coating overhaul scheduled from 2011 had not started on the 15 km section by October 2013 (p. 6). It sat in the annual plan and counted as in hand, and the reason it was pushed past was "it is on the plan".
    3. The Qinhuangdao Road improvement project ran without consulting the pipeline company and without calculating its effect on the pipeline (p. 7). The works completed the municipal approval route on their own, and the reason it was pushed past was "the paperwork is complete".
    4. 6 special rectification inspections in 2013 found no effect from the road works (p. 7). The inspections were completed by ticking a list, and the reason it was pushed past was "not in this round's scope".
    5. The patrol system was incomplete, the patrol staff lacked professional knowledge, the emergency plan had no drills (p. 6). All three were booked under training, and the reason it was pushed past was "training pending".
    6. The pipe ran suspended inside the culvert in an area subject to seawater backflow (p. 7). It was a 1986 design, compliant when handed over, and the reason it was pushed past was "the design was approved".

    None of the six was hidden. They sat in a test report, an annual plan, a works approval, an inspection checklist, a training plan and a design file (pp. 3–7).

    The point of escalation: After the valve closed at 03:20 the source was cut, and the oil was still in the culvert. The excavator came in at 07:00, the pit took shape at 07:40, the leak point was found at 08:20; from that moment the object of the work changed from plugging a leak to breaking up a structure above an oil-bearing culvert. Only at 09:15 did the pipeline company order the on-site command formed, and at 09:30 the deputy division head reported that the repair could not be finished independently and asked for support (p. 4), 55 minutes before the explosion. The decision to open the culvert cover and switch to the hydraulic breaker is recorded only as "decided on site" (p. 5), with no name and no time. The shape of this escalation: every step advanced the repair, no step needed approval, and no step needed a magnitude written upward. Across the two hours and five minutes from 08:20 to 10:25 the site held actions and no numbers. At 09:55 the deputy director of the municipal economy and information technology commission arrived, at 10:21 reported sea-surface pollution to the city's deputy secretary-general, and at 10:27 reported the explosion and fire on site (p. 4): the word "explosion" enters the government chain two minutes after the explosion. The report's prevention section answers with one sentence: "text messages shall not be used in place of telephone reports of accident information" (p. 17).

    Failure chain: Technical layer: the pipe wall thinned by corrosion in alternating wet and dry seawater and salt spray, with road load and vibration on top; the rupture released about 2,000 tonnes of crude into the culvert; vapour accumulated in a confined space; the spark from the breaker ignited it (pp. 5–6). Organizational layer: the leak volume was never assessed under the plan, the risk assessment was seriously wrong, no activation order was issued, no cordon was set, no combustible gas was measured (p. 6); at group and listed company level the division of pipeline protection and safety duties was unclear and responsibility unnamed (p. 6); the zone graded the event a general emergency, did not raise the level, did not close roads, did not evacuate, and the emergency office suppressed and delayed reports of the accident and misreported whether the responsible leader had attended (p. 8). Incentive layer: the line ran at full load (p. 2), the overhaul scheduled for three years never reached the 15 km section (p. 6), and six inspections and three tests all produced "not found" (pp. 6–7); the outputs of inspection and testing pointed the same way, and nobody had to answer for failing to find anything. All three layers broke between 04:00 and 10:25. The magnitude gap has an exact address: the figure of 2,000 tonnes first appears in the investigation report's calculation (p. 5) and in no upward document. The junction broke between the organizational and the incentive layer: the arrangement that made "not found" a qualified output also made "unquantified" a qualified report, and neither needed a signature.

    Minimum counterfactual: Before the excavation around 04:00, one combustible gas measurement at the work face and at the culvert opening, with the reading entered in the site record, and no non-explosion-proof equipment used while it was over the limit. One action, 1 hour and 35 minutes after the shutdown. The instrument is standard kit for a repair crew, the crew was already on site, no new budget was needed, no approval had to be waited for, and no judgement was required: over the limit, change the method; within it, the demolition may proceed. The timing is 04:00 because the leak was not yet opened, the road surface was not yet broken and the culvert cover was untouched; one step later and the action falls inside demolition already under way. The report lists "no combustible gas detection" among the indirect causes (p. 6), which says the step was available on the day and was not taken. It is also an information action: once the reading is in the record it has to travel up with the site report, and the receiving chain then has something to pass at that cell.

    Outcome and sources: 62 dead, 136 injured, direct economic losses of 751.72 million yuan; 15 people subject to judicial measures, 48 recommended for Party and administrative discipline; the company fined at the maximum, the party secretary and general manager each fined 80% of 2012 income; the Shandong provincial government and the Sinopec Group ordered to report to the State Council with a deep self-examination (p. 15). Primary source: State Council investigation team for the "11·22" Sinopec Donghuang crude oil pipeline leak and explosion, Investigation report, 10 January 2014, published on the website of the State Administration of Work Safety, link in Appendix B, ch-007. The official release is a full web page with no official PDF; the card's page numbers follow an A4 print of the full text, 18 pages, and the page-to-section map is in _evidence/ch-007.md.

    Case one carries a reading that deserves a paragraph of its own: in Qingdao the horizontal failure to assemble and the vertical delay existed side by side. The vertical one is pinned by the report's timestamps. The leak was found at 02:12 and reported up the company chain only at 05:07; the 110 call came at 02:31 and the city duty office heard of it at 07:49. The horizontal one is this chapter's subject. The company, the local government and the site have no line of authority between them, and the mechanism at work is that each holds one facet. Every party's information is true, local, and sufficient for its own duties. The act of assembly has no owner. Reading therefore has to widen: the minutes of each level side by side, and then the information versions of each party side by side. The check has one question. Where on which desk does the full picture of this event exist at this moment. If you cannot answer, you are looking at the Qingdao shape.

    Case 2 (Maritime & Transport): The New Procedure That Stopped at the Paper

    ma-007. The ro-ro cargo ship Laureline, 13 July 2024, Purfleet terminal, United Kingdom. MAIB report (Report 7/2026, page verified for this book): a sailor was crushed to death by a trailer during vehicle loading. The investigation found that the new vehicle-deck loading safety procedure was not understood by the crew and was being carried out in distorted form; supervision was assessed as ineffective; the trailer driver did not know where the crew were. The procedure had been signed off at the management end and had never landed at the execution end. Between issuance and accident, "the procedure has been understood and is being followed" was management's default information, and that information had never once been verified.

    Stop at the decision point: the moment after the new procedure was issued and before the first shift worked to it. On the desk: the procedure text distributed, the training records on file, the next shift's work plan, and one missing item: no mechanism anywhere answers the question "did the people on board understand it". Outcome: one death. Primary source: MAIB, Report 7/2026, 2026, link in Appendix B, ma-007.

    The phrase "supervision was assessed as ineffective" deserves magnification. Between the supervisor and the supervised lies the distance from the deck to the wheelhouse, and the supervisor's version came from the ship's routine reports: procedure trained, operations normal. In MAIB's language that version was wrong. Upward information completed its attenuation on one clause, "the procedure has been understood": the training record is true, and the absence of understanding is also true. This is the same phenomenon as the checklist ticking in Chapter 15, seen from the other side: paperwork proves delivery, not receipt. The equivalent of Device 2 on board is management reading one deck record or one interview a month and asking "how do you understand the new procedure". One question, and the depth of attenuation is measured once.

    Case 3 (Public Policy & Administration): The Conceptual Schedule

    po-002. The US Department of Veterans Affairs Electronic Health Record Modernization programme (VA EHRM). GAO report (GAO-26-109393, September 2026, page verified for this book): the programme plans to complete deployment at about 170 sites by 2031; deployment was paused in 2023 after poor user reviews and restarted at the end of 2024; GAO noted that what VA gave Congress was only a conceptual schedule, with no cost estimate and no detailed schedule document; of 18 recommendations, 4 had been implemented. This is attenuation in its public-administration form. Information passed to the overseer is deliberately set at a granularity that can be reported. Nobody concealed anything. A form of presentation was chosen that cannot be interrogated.

    Stop at the decision point: the reporting season in which the conceptual schedule was chosen over a detailed plan in answer to congressional oversight. On the desk: the real risks and setbacks of deployment, the expectation that a detailed plan would be interrogated item by item once written, and the complete procedural legality of the word "conceptual". Outcome: the oversight body recorded the gap in information baseline in a dedicated report. Primary source: GAO, GAO-26-109393, 2026, link in Appendix B, po-002.

    The conceptual schedule pushes attenuation into its public-administration form: information is deliberately parked at a granularity that cannot be interrogated. It is the same coin as the granularity choice in case four, the single incident. Whoever chooses the granularity decides whether interrogation is possible. GAO's countermeasure is worth recording: the report asks directly for a cost estimate and detailed schedule documents, which forces the granularity down. A reader can borrow the move inside his own organization. Whenever the words conceptual, framework or directional appear in material submitted to an overseer, a board, a regulator, a superior, ask one question: can you give me a version I can check line by line. What is being demanded is not honesty. It is checkability, and checkability by itself changes how the upstream writes.

    Case 4 (Manufacturing & Supply Chain): Six Hundred Incident Reports

    mn-001. The HAIARA power bank (Mitrytech, Amazon channel). CPSC recall notice (page verified for this book): about 11,000 power banks recalled; the company knew of 600 reports of fire and other thermal incidents, among them 3 burns and 9 cases of property damage. Six hundred incidents are not one piece of bad news. They are six hundred. Each looked small on its own: one overheating, one smoking; each travelled its own reporting path to the end; and the act of adding them up happened at no level at all until the regulator did it.

    Stop at the decision point: the window in which incident report N arrived and the sales decision was not touched. On the desk: the severity of each single case, the channel pressure from units still on sale, the cost of a recall, and a number nobody was responsible for watching, the cumulative count. Outcome: CPSC intervened and a recall followed. Primary source: CPSC, 2026, link in Appendix B, mn-001.

    Case four has a layer that needs saying plainly. Every one of the 600 reports completed the compliance flow. The company logged it, filed it, assessed it as a single case, and the assessment concluded that each one fell below the reporting threshold. Attenuation here works through a choice of granularity rather than softened wording: at the granularity of the single incident, six hundred add up to zero; at the granularity of the cumulative count, six hundred is a number that demands action. The regulator did one thing, and it changed the granularity. The conversion for a reader: who set the granularity of your safety ledger, and what colour does it show at that granularity, and what colour at another.

    Case 5 (Healthcare): Twelve Days and One Unclaimed Chest X-ray

    Case ID: me-005.

    When and where: 15 July 2017, 07:40, to 31 January 2018. The emergency department, the assessment ward and cardiology ward 24 of an NHS trust in England, plus a coronary ward at another hospital in the same trust. The report refers throughout to "the Trust" and gives no hospital or place names (HSIB pp. 12–16). The clock times: the X-ray taken at 10:18 on 15 July, discharge on the evening of 21 July, the report issued on 27 July, the handover on 28 July, the GP retrieving it on 20 October.

    What and who: the national investigation team of the Healthcare Safety Investigation Branch (HSIB; the report cover carries HSSIB), published 21 October 2022. The trust departments under investigation were three: the emergency department, which had a consultant on results management duty; radiology, with its consultant radiologists, radiology administrative team, the Return to Accident Room tray and the bouncer register; and cardiology, with ward 24, the coronary ward and the consultant who performed angioplasty. Outside the trust: the GP practice, the national serious incident database StEIS, the Royal College of Radiologists (RCR) and the Care Quality Commission (CQC) for England. Responsibility attaches to posts: the junior doctor who wrote "lung fields clear", the consultant radiologist who issued the report, the emergency consultant on results management that day, the cardiology consultant who received the patient, the radiology administrative courier, and the receptionist who signed for the report.

    What they were doing: At 07:40 on 15 July the patient arrived at the emergency department with chest pain and shortness of breath; the workup included a chest X-ray and an ECG; symptoms and results pointed to a myocardial infarction and she was moved to the assessment ward (p. 12). The film was taken at 10:18. At 13:50 the assessment ward junior doctor wrote "lung fields clear" in the notes. At 14:00 the consultant ward round recorded only the blood results and the ECG and did not mention the film (p. 12). At 14:30 cardiology saw her, and she was transferred to the cardiology ward that day, where she stayed five days waiting for transfer for angiography (p. 12). On 20 July she moved to another hospital in the same trust, had angiography and a stent the next morning, and went home that evening with a cardiology outpatient appointment six weeks out (p. 13).

    The action on the day of discharge was the most ordinary of the lot: the discharge summary went to the GP practice and did not mention the emergency chest X-ray still waiting for a report (p. 13). That same week radiology was running a manual process the staff called the bouncer. The reporting doctor emailed the administrative team, or put the paper report in the RTAR tray in the radiology office; the administrators printed it and entered the patient's details in the bouncer book; they delivered twice a day, at 09:00 and 16:00, to the emergency department reception; reception put the report in the results tray and signed the bouncer book (p. 26). Interviewees described it as long-standing customary practice, and no policy document set it out (p. 25). Radiology had just changed its information system, and stability and speed had been a problem for months before it was resolved, with the backlog taking months more to clear (p. 24). The clinical director said emergency chest X-rays were reported "broadly" within 48 hours (p. 24).

    The decision point: Four moments.

    One. At 13:50 on 15 July the assessment ward junior doctor wrote "lung fields clear" in the record system. Ten minutes later the consultant did the ward round, and the 14:00 note records blood results and ECG supporting the diagnosis of infarction, with no mention of imaging (pp. 12, 21). At the moment that line was written the formal report did not exist. On the desk was a film nobody had formally read.

    Two. Discharge on the evening of 21 July. The discharge summary went to the GP practice and its body carried no item for "emergency chest X-ray awaiting report"; the summary arranged a cardiology clinic appointment six weeks out (p. 13). The discharge was handled by the coronary ward team, while the requesting department for the film was the emergency department. Six days had passed since the X-ray, and no report existed.

    Three. On 27 July, the twelfth day after the examination, the consultant radiologist completed the report. The text: "There is a 45mm opacity projected over the liver which may represent a right lung base mass." (p. 13). It carried no recommendation for the next step and no time frame (p. 23). Under radiology policy the radiologist emailed the administrative team asking that the report go back to the emergency department; that day the report was printed, delivered to the department and signed for at reception (p. 13). The comparison sits on paper. RCEM best practice guidance from 2016 requires that emergency imaging be reviewed by a radiologist or a reporting radiographer within 48 hours of the request (p. 23). The trust's own policy set no time limit on reports at all, requiring only that clinically urgent cases be telephoned to the requester promptly and that suspected cancer be notified within one day (p. 24). This report travelled on day twelve. The notification travelled by letter.

    Four. On 28 July the emergency consultant on results management reviewed the report, looked the patient up in the system, found her under cardiology, wrote to the cardiology consultant and emailed at the same time, copying the GP (p. 13). The letter was generated by the system, and the report says it "would normally be printed and posted"; nobody could establish whether this one had been printed (p. 14). The cardiology consultant opened the email but could not recall reading it (p. 14). He had just come back from three weeks of leave, estimated 50 to 100 emails a day and up to 700 a week, and was on the emergency cardiac surgery rota in that first week back (p. 26). Both the letter and the email were accurate: 45 mm, right lung base, possible mass.

    What was visible then: The image from 10:18 on 15 July; the "lung fields clear" in the notes at 13:50 that day; the 14:00 ward round note with no imaging item; the discharge summary of 21 July with no pending-imaging line; the report text of 27 July and the figure of 45 mm; the emergency reception signature in the bouncer book that same day; the transfer letter in the system on 28 July, the email that had gone out, and the email's opened status. Five further texts. The trust's Radiology Reporting Arrangements of November 2015 contains the sentence "We are…unable to confirm that results have been received by the referrer, or have been acted upon." (p. 25). The same policy sets no reporting time limit (p. 24). The department did not use the critical, urgent and unexpected significant three-tier classification (p. 22). The report contains no "what next" (p. 23). The bouncer process exists only in custom and not in policy, and it has four manual steps, any one of which can fail (pp. 25–26). Two external numbers can be checked. Between 1 April 2017 and 14 May 2018 StEIS held 41 serious incidents of this class, 13 of them from emergency departments (p. 11). CQC records emergency reporting time limits across trusts ranging from 1 hour to 2 working days (p. 30).

    Known only afterwards: whether the letter was printed, posted, or where in the chain it was lost (p. 14); why the cardiology consultant opened the email without reading it (p. 14); that the twelve-day delay came from the backlog after the system change (p. 24); the composition of the 41, of which 27 were cases where serious harm might have been avoided had the patient been informed (p. 11); the trust's internal investigation conclusion, that with her cardiac and respiratory condition as it was, radical treatment may not have been appropriate, and earlier palliative care "might have brought slightly better symptom relief or a small improvement in survival" (p. 17); and that the patient's husband learned of the film from a hospital letter five months after his wife's death (p. 16).

    Stop here. Decide before you turn the page.

    1. What would you do? Write the action, not "pay more attention".
    2. Which item here are you most likely to miss, and why does it look unimportant?
    3. If you may set one checkpoint, when is it and what does it check?

    What happened: The cancer diagnosis was delayed and the patient died. HSIB made 4 safety recommendations, all aimed at the reporting and follow-up closure of unexpected significant findings. Back to the three questions. The commonest answer to the first is "chase the report", but chasing only compresses twelve days to five, and with no ownership mechanism the finding still sits. To the second question, the item most often missed is the national figure of 41 cases: it says the accident belongs to no single hospital. It marks an ownership vacuum in the standard path by which reports come back. The third question lands on closure design: every report with an abnormal finding needs a single named owner and an escalation on timeout, and an empty owner field is the system's red state. Primary source: HSIB, Failures in communication or follow-up of unexpected significant radiological findings, 2019, link in Appendix B, me-005 (archived link, grade A−).

    Options ruled out: The report records, item by item, the routes not taken at the time. Hot reporting, with a report issued as the emergency film is taken: five radiology consultants agreed it was the ideal, but radiologists and reporting radiographers were short, CQC recorded a vacancy rate averaging 14%, and most imaging departments did not consider it realistic (pp. 29, 30 note 24). Telephone handover for every unexpected significant finding: the telephone "definitely gets heard" but leaves no auditable record, and no document required it at the time (p. 32). Radiology arranging the next step directly: it would duplicate CT the emergency department may already have ordered, and coordination costs more while requests are still on paper (p. 35). Writing "what next" into the report: three radiology consultants said reports suggesting lung cancer usually carry a recommendation, and they also considered this report clearly written, the point being whether risk control was triggered, and it was (p. 23). A dedicated results co-ordinator watching unclaimed reports: the report concludes the post is needed, states that it has a resource impact, and notes that most trusts' IT could not support it (p. 56). The NHS telling patients directly: this became recommendation 2019/041, the NHS App could not do it at the time, the time frame under discussion was two to three weeks (p. 60), and the recommendation text is at p. 61. The trust itself took a different route and took it halfway: an electronic acknowledgement system, expected live in August 2019 (p. 28).

    Early signals: Five, each with the reason it was carried past.

    1. The 2015 RCR national audit: 154 radiology departments replied, 88% had a written policy, 17% had an electronic acknowledgement system, and of those with a system only 42% had anyone reviewing the read data regularly; the audit concluded that most departments did not meet the published national guidance (pp. 52–53). The signal sat in a public audit, and the reason it was pushed past was "we have a policy, and we have the bouncer book".
    2. The trust's own Radiology Reporting Arrangements, November 2015 edition, says in its own words that it cannot confirm results were received by the requester or acted upon (pp. 22, 25). A self-declared gap went into an in-force document, and the reason it was pushed past was that it stood under notes rather than defects.
    3. 41 serious incidents, 13 of them from emergency departments (p. 11). Anyone could pull that number with database keywords, 58 retrieved and 17 excluded (p. 12 note 8), and the reason it was pushed past was that each case closed on its own terms, with different causes.
    4. Reporting times stretched markedly after the system change, with a backlog running for months (p. 24). The clinical director called it a system problem being resolved, and the department line stayed at "broadly 48 hours".
    5. After the 2007 NPSA safety practice notice the RCR issued four more standards documents, and the same harm kept happening in England; NHS Resolution recorded 662 radiology claims within two years, 24 of them coded as failure to act on abnormal results, with more than £2.5 million already paid (p. 8, p. 11). These numbers were public, and the reason they were pushed past was that they were a national trend and not a local to-do.

    The point of escalation: The moment the letter went out on 28 July. Until then the report sat with the only department that had a results-handling process: the emergency department had the bouncer book, two deliveries a day at 09:00 and 16:00, a duty results consultant, and an audit trail at the point where reception signed. Once the letter went out, the finding moved to a department with no results-handling process, and the step complied with the guidance then in force. RCEM guidance requires that when a patient is admitted under a non-emergency team, responsibility for review and follow-up be handed explicitly to that team (p. 25), and the report quotes the BMA's judgement on clinical handover, one of the most dangerous procedures in medicine (p. 33). The handover was carried out on 28 July by the emergency consultant on results management, under that guidance and trust policy; the report records no approval step, because the step needed no approval and no signature. The chain runs longer than that: emergency to cardiology, cardiology to CT booking, then respiratory (p. 32). For the next 85 days no status in the system changed because this report had not been handled. Two of the trust's four internal remediation items land on this handover: writing the process for how unexpected significant findings reach the emergency department, and creating a Cancer Imaging and Pathway Co-ordinator (p. 28).

    Failure chain: Technical layer: the mass sat behind the diaphragm, where a non-specialist easily misses it (p. 21); the report was completed on the twelfth day after the examination (p. 13); it states findings only, with no next step and no time frame (p. 23); the system's read status means opened, while the RCR standard requires seen and acknowledged as two separate steps (p. 50); the report travelled as a paper print carried by hand, and any link could drop it (p. 26). Organizational layer: trust policy never says how an unexpected significant finding goes back to the emergency department, and the process lived only in custom (p. 25); receipt had an audit trail and handling did not, so the bouncer book proves the report reached reception and not that anyone took it on (p. 26); the results management consultant's duty stopped at delivery to the receiving team (p. 25); the receiving team had no acknowledgement field and nobody needed to acknowledge (p. 27); the discharge summary carried no pending imaging (p. 13); nationally, 17% of departments had an electronic acknowledgement system (p. 52). Incentive layer: no national standard set a reporting time limit and the trust's policy set none either (pp. 23–24); CQC inspection did not check whether acknowledgement systems were running (p. 4); radiology capacity was held back by radiographer shortage, with a CQC-recorded vacancy rate of 14% (p. 30 note 24), and output was counted in reports completed; the cardiology consultant's inbox ran 50 to 100 items a day (p. 26); the cost of the harm arrived later as claims, 24 in two years and more than £2.5 million (p. 11), and entered no post's current-period metrics.

    The junction broke between the organizational and the incentive layer: the receipt chain closed completely on 27 July, and the handling chain broke at its first hop on 28 July. The arrangement that made "delivered" a qualified output also kept "unhandled" out of anyone's metrics, and neither needed a signature.

    Minimum counterfactual: On 21 July, writing the discharge summary, add one line to it: "Emergency chest X-ray (15/07): report not returned, responsibility passing to cardiology outpatient review." One action, on a document that had to be written that day, at a cost of one line. It needs no new system (the trust's electronic acknowledgement system arrives only in August 2019, p. 28), no budget, no reliance on anyone remembering, and no change in anyone's judgement. The timing is 21 July because the discharge summary is the one document from this admission that crosses both institutions and teams: it goes to the GP practice and to the cardiology clinic, and the clinic is six weeks away. The report records this gap itself (p. 13), and records the clinical consensus that had the report been issued while the patient was an inpatient, the follow-up failure might have been avoided (p. 24). It does not guarantee a cure, since the trust's internal investigation considered she may not have been suitable for radical treatment (p. 17). It guarantees the film enters a list that someone has to open.

    Outcome and sources: On 31 January 2018 the patient died at home (p. 16). The delay was 12 days, the report went unclaimed for 85 days, and the diagnosis was pushed back by about three months; CT on 10 November confirmed bilateral lung cancer, she was told the same day, and treatment was set as palliative (p. 16). Four safety recommendations: 2019/039, that the RCR set out the principles for judging unexpected significant, critical and urgent findings, an alert coding framework and a mandatory trigger list; 2019/040, that the NHSE/I patient safety team ensure organisations implement monitored acknowledgement systems; 2019/041, that NHSX develop a digital way of telling patients results; and 2018/042, that CQC write risk control into the core service framework. There is one further safety observation, that radiology opening CT directly for chest X-rays suspicious of lung cancer is worth evaluating (pp. 5–6, 66–67). The national figure is 41 serious incidents, 13 of them from emergency departments (p. 11). Primary source: HSIB, Investigation report: Failures in communication or follow-up of unexpected significant radiological findings, published 21 October 2022 (recommendation numbers follow the case numbering of 2019/039 to 2019/041 and 2018/042), link in Appendix B, me-005 (archived link, grade A−). The card's page numbers are report PDF pages, 95 in total, and the item-by-item sources are in _evidence/me-005-加厚.md.

    Case five shares a detail with Chapter 30 that is worth setting down. Across the twelve days, nobody broke a rule. Radiology completed the report in queue order, slowly but compliantly; the system delivered it to the requesting department, and that link worked as designed; after the review and the handover nobody owned the follow-up, because the system had no acknowledgement field and nothing flagged the abnormal. Every link ran as designed, and the design left the ownership of the finding with no landing place. Remediation in a case like this points at fields and not at people: owner, timeout, escalation. Add those three fields and the next finding in the same sequence lands. HSIB's four recommendations sit largely on those three fields, and a reader can borrow them directly.

    Case 6 (Public Policy & Administration): The Public-Administration Edition of Side-by-Side Reading (condensed)

    po-008. The National Audit Office of China, audit of central budget execution for 2025 (reported to the Standing Committee of the National People's Congress in June 2026, PDF text verified for this book): the audit found 40.427 billion yuan of corporate income tax, VAT and other taxes levied excessively or collected in advance from 1,954 enterprises; 59.651 billion yuan of inflated fiscal revenue; and 10.935 billion yuan of tax evaded. The case is this chapter's mechanism read backwards. Levying excessive taxes and fees is a workaround at the execution level, and it is invisible in routine upward reporting, where every level's revenue task shows as met. Only the audit channel, which does not pass through the hierarchy (Device 2, national edition), added it up. The decision point is the moment a basic collection unit, facing a revenue task, chooses an enforcement standard. Minimum counterfactual: give tax execution data a sampled direct channel that does not go through hierarchical aggregation. Primary source: National Audit Office, 2026, link in Appendix B, po-008.

    Case six has a second reading. Audit is the attenuation detector that the modern state installs in advance. Its technical features are this chapter's entirely: no hierarchy, in an independent office reporting to the legislature; originals, in extended audit pulling the working papers; side-by-side reading, in final accounts set against the books. Publication is itself a governance act, since it turns the measured attenuation into the starting point for accountability and remediation. A reader in a company can borrow one design principle from the public edition: the detector must have no personnel overlap with the reporting system it tests. The day audit independence is written into the governance documents, one more line appears on attenuation's price list.

    There is a layer beyond the numbers. The audit report lists the three figures separately, excess and advance collection, inflated revenue, evaded tax, and the separation is itself an anti-attenuation way of writing. Merging them into one sentence about collection and management problems involving over a hundred billion yuan would be grammatical and would be informational suicide: three classes of problem map to three remediation paths, and merging blurs all three at once. That is a public lesson for anyone who writes reports. Remediability is the only legitimate reason to keep figures apart, and before any impulse to merge, ask whether the object of remediation can still be found afterwards.

    Case 7 (Aviation): The Engineer's Unease (condensed)

    av-017. Eurocopter EC135P2+, registration JA824H, August 2024, Japan. JTSB serious incident investigation report (2025, verified for this book): the helicopter was cleared to land at a helipad and landed on the runway instead. The detail recorded in the material card is a textbook sample for this chapter. The maintenance engineer riding on board felt something wrong about the landing position and did not say so. The unease was a piece of bad news in perfect condition, and it stopped at the step where saying it would disturb the flying. The upward half of the MUM effect, the sender's unwillingness to report, completed itself inside the cabin. The decision point is the moment of re-confirming the cleared landing point before final approach. Minimum counterfactual: put a fixed mutual confirmation into the approach brief, that the expected landing point matches the clearance, and that unease from any seat is spoken. Primary source: JTSB, AI2025-8-1, 2025, link in Appendix B, av-017.

    The engineer's silence has a context that deserves a word in his favour. The role order in a cabin, who flies and who fixes, is itself a piece of safety design, and what it prevents is interference from outside the role. That is the hard part of designing a channel for bad news: protect the role order and still give every seat's unease an exit. Aviation's mature answer is worth copying. Use an explicit callout phrase, so that anyone in any seat who sees a mismatch says it in a fixed form. Make the consequence of speaking institutional: always recorded, always thanked, never questioned for motive. The phrase lowers the cost of the exit and the institution removes the price of using it. Another industry copying the combination can drop the phraseology and cannot drop the two principles. The exit has to be cheap, and the exit has to be blameless.

    The counter-examples sit in this chapter. In case four the 600 reports all had a registration channel, so an exit existed, and the consequence of registering was an archive nobody cared about, so the exit had no value. In case seven the unease had no fixed phrase, so the exit was expensive, and the consequence of speaking was unknown, so the exit carried liability. That gives the three elements of exit design: it exists, it is cheap, it is blameless. Add a fourth, an echo, which is the feedback ledger in Device 2. A channel missing any one of the four will idle at the first real test.

    The line at the head of this chapter, that every floor up changes the coat, can carry one more note: the coats are changed according to a grammar. The first layer changes the quantifier, so a major hazard becomes some risk. The second changes the verb, so will happen becomes may exist. The third changes the subject, so our unit becomes some units in the industry. The fourth changes the time, so now becomes in the long run. Four steps and the sentence is grammatical, morally spotless and empty. The reader's quick check is to watch those four substitutions. When any of them appears on the way up, put the original back and read the sentence again. The restored sentence makes the receiver uncomfortable, and that discomfort is the first arrival of the real intensity.

    Beyond grammar, the layout of a report takes part in the attenuation. Where the bad news sits, which page, which paragraph, whether bold; how the heading is worded, risk note or serious incident; how it is ordered against the good news, in the sandwich of good, bad, good. All of it is the layout layer. Layout is the hardest to defend because even the wording can be called accurate: it merely placed the bad news where the reading would not be interrupted. The side-by-side table in Device 1 catches layout as well, because the same item is a heading in the original and a footnote in the quarterly report, and the difference in position is the difference in intensity.

    The eight cases run across five domains, chemical, maritime, public policy, manufacturing and healthcare, and produce three host shapes for attenuation. The chain type, cases one, five and eight, where information passes through many links, ownership evaporates and nobody assembles the whole. The paper type, cases two and three, where a document proves delivery while understanding and execution never happened, so the upward word complete is wrong. The granularity type, cases four and six, where everything looks normal at a grain convenient to oneself and problems pile up at another grain. Each host takes a different detection action: for the chain type, find the assembly table and ask where the whole picture sits; for the paper type, test understanding by spot-reading and asking for a playback; for the granularity type, change the grain and look again on a cumulative or an alternative basis. The six signals in Section 16.4 are the common exit for all three, and the four devices in Section 16.5 are matched to the host type.

    Case 8 (Construction & Transport): The Hand the Door Test Missed (condensed)

    ce-006. Ealing Broadway station, London. RAIB report 01/2026 (page verified for this book): a passenger's hand was trapped in a train door and the passenger was dragged along, suffering minor injuries. The investigation found that the door obstacle detection did not identify the trapped hand and the anti-drag measures did not work. The state of the equipment in this case has the form of locally knowable, globally unreachable: the people on the platform saw it, the train system did not know, and the driver departed on the signal. The piece of information that existed stopped at the platform's position and never entered the departure decision chain. The decision point is the visual confirmation before departure. Minimum counterfactual: a mandatory voice confirmation between platform and driver on any obstruction anomaly, with no departure until it is confirmed. Primary source: RAIB, Report 01/2026, 2026, link in Appendix B, ce-006.

    Cases seven and eight side by side are the same mechanism in two industries. In both, the bad news was produced intact at the front line: an engineer's unease in one case, a hand seen in a door in the other. In both it stopped at the last gap before the decision chain. The position of the gap is the same too: between the person closest to departure or landing and the person who makes the decision. So the defence lands in the same place. A forced confirmation at the final gap costs an order of magnitude less than detection installed along the whole chain.

    16.4 What Shows Up Earliest

    Six weak signals, each with the excuse that usually carries it past.

    1. The share of adjectives in reporting material rises and the share of numbers falls. Excuse: "leadership wants the key points."
    2. "Overall under control" and "the trend is improving" appear more often. Excuse: "to steady expectations."
    3. The same event carries different intensity in the minutes of different levels. Excuse: "each level has its own focus."
    4. The front-line originals (work orders, recordings, ledgers) do not match the version that went up. Excuse: "the report was distilled."
    5. A bypass channel exists and has never been used, with zero users. Excuse: "that shows there is no problem."
    6. The top's judgement of conditions at the front line makes the front line laugh. Excuse: "leadership sees the bigger picture."

    Signal three is the operational starting point. It needs no new system, only someone willing to photocopy two sets of minutes onto one sheet of paper.

    Put the six signals back against the cases. Signal one, adjectives up and numbers down, matches case one, where the magnitude of the leak was cut into facets across the parties describing it. Signal two, the rising frequency of "overall under control", matches case three, where the conceptual schedule is the documentary form of control. Signal three, intensity differing by level, matches case six, where the execution-level workaround is invisible as a whole in routine reporting. Signal four, originals not matching the upward version, matches case two, where the training record and the crew's understanding are two versions. Signal five, a bypass channel at zero use, is the negative footnote to case seven, where the engineer's unease never even reached the channel's doorway. Signal six takes this form in case five: the system's belief that follow-up was running normally, set against the national fact of 41 cases.

    The six sort by cost of collection. Signals three and four are cheapest, a photocopy of minutes and a comparison against originals, half a day's work. Signals one and two come next, needing a quarter's material for a word-frequency observation. Signals five and six are dearest, needing interviews and site time. The starting advice is the usual one: begin with the cheapest. Do signal three this quarter, and it alone decides whether the mechanism is running.

    The intensity table deserves a template. Four columns: time, level, verbatim excerpt with no rewriting, and a self-rating of intensity from one to five, where the rating answers "how much more would I worry if I read only this". Each level's version of the same topic takes one row, in time order. There is one discipline in making it: the excerpt must be verbatim, because rewriting re-does the attenuation you are auditing. There is one rule in reading it: do not look for who was wrong, since that leads into Chapter 19, and mark only the change points, the intensity dropping from what to what, at which level. The level where change points cluster is the first site for attenuation governance, and whether that level's receiving end lacks a seat or its sending end lacks protection decides which device to add.

    16.5 How to Defend

    Device 1: reading the minutes side by side. Once a quarter, take one topic's reporting minutes from different levels, lay them out in time order, and mark the points where intensity changes. This is the only method that makes "every step small, the total large" visible. Execution: the comparison is done by the person who reports directly to the top, since he stands outside the attenuation chain, and the result is the first item on that period's meeting agenda, with no pre-screening.

    Device 2: a direct channel for originals. Front-line work orders, incident flash reports and raw customer complaints get a channel straight to a senior reader, unpolished, with a named reader each month who signs the reading log. What the channel gives is existence rather than throughput: writers who know the originals will be read moderate their wording. Capacity design: a senior reader's real monthly capacity is small, so sample rather than pile up, 20 items drawn at random each month, because piling everything up is the same as having none.

    Device 3: an immunity clause for reporting. The system states in writing that a person who reports bad news factually is not held to account for the news itself, and that accountability applies only to concealment and fabrication. Without this clause, what Devices 1 and 2 collect dries up at the source: side-by-side reading will show that every level rewrote, and the first level that rewrote is the one that did not dare write it straight.

    Device 4: quantifier discipline. Internal reporting bans unquantified modifiers, "certain", "rather many", "overall", and every modifier must carry a number or the words "not quantified". The lesson of Qingdao is not that some level said the wrong thing. It is that the response decision was made on an assembly that was never complete. Quantifier discipline cannot abolish that, and it can expose where the problem sits: the paragraph where unquantified modifiers begin is where the attenuation begins.

    The four devices on this chapter's cases. Device 1 on case six: set collection execution data beside the level-by-level aggregates, and the difference is the forty odd billion the audit office measured. Device 2 on case two: management reads one front-line work record a month. Device 3 on case seven: an unease spoken in the cabin goes into the flight record whether it turns out right or wrong, and nobody asks about motive. Device 4 on case one: every status update after the leak carries a magnitude, leak volume, affected area, progress of the work, and where no number exists the words "not quantified" go in its place.

    The failure modes of the four. Side-by-side reading dies of sample bias: only the minutes of material events get read, and the worst attenuation sits in items that looked small at the time; the patch is to draw the topic at random each quarter. The originals channel dies of reading as performance: the signature is there and the reading is not; the patch is for the named reader to tell the front line periodically what he read, since that feedback is the only evidence that reading happened. The immunity clause dies of exceptions: accountability shifts from the message to an improper manner or a wrong moment, and immunity is bypassed; the patch is a written explanation for every bypass. Quantifier discipline dies of number inflation: the modifiers go and the numbers get inflated; the patch is for Device 1's side-by-side reading to check where the numbers came from at the same time.

    The weighting of the four is worth stating. If only one can be installed, install Device 3, immunity, because goods in the channel precede everything else. If two, add Device 1, because once there are goods you need to see the deformation. Three and four follow in that order. The order runs against intuition, since most organizations install side-by-side reading first, and the result is a distortion with nowhere to be reported, which leaves the mechanism where it started. Channel first, detection after.

    One common mistake in staffing a direct channel is worth walking around in advance: making it a full-time inspection post. A dedicated inspector turns finding bad news into a departmental KPI, the department reports noise as signal to meet its volume, and the channel loses credibility fast. A workable design is part-time plus rotation. The duty is carried by front-line staff on rotation, quarterly, whose day job stays on site and who report what they themselves see; rotation keeps the channel from being bound to anyone's career over the long run. The far end, the senior reader, keeps a record too: who read, what was read, and who was given feedback, one page a month. With a ledger at both ends, the channel survives an assessment cycle.

    16.6 Boundaries with Neighbouring Mechanisms

    Against handover rupture (Chapter 30): attenuation is vertical loss between levels, and rupture is horizontal loss between the parties on either side of a handover. Laureline's new procedure, case two here, and the two-ship assumption in ma-008 look like the same class, and the criterion that separates them is direction. What was not passed downward is attenuation; what was not passed sideways is rupture.

    Against why the truth-teller goes first (Chapter 19): this chapter is the channel question, how the message deforms; Chapter 19 is the price question, what happens to the person who speaks. Which to check first. If the channel carries plenty of traffic and it is distorted, fix the channel. If the channel carries nothing, check the price first.

    Against goal displacement (Chapter 12): metric distortion is attenuation that has been structured, since compressing risk into a rating is one institutionalised wording choice. GAO's cross-check of dashboards, case two in Chapter 12, is the public edition of Device 1 here.

    16.7 Five Self-Checks

    1. Take the three sets of minutes from your organization's most recent material event and read them side by side. At which level does the intensity start to change?
    2. How many unquantified modifiers are in the last report you signed?
    3. In your organization, who has read the original complaint or the incident flash report? When did he last verify anything with the front line?
    4. If tomorrow you wrote a piece of bad news into the weekly report exactly as it stands, what would happen? What does that answer tell you?
    5. How old is your judgement of the front line? When did you last spend half a day on site?
