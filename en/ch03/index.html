    Chapter 3 The Five Fields That Record Failure in Earnest

    What this chapter covers: how failure-record institutions are built in aviation, chemicals, maritime, medicine, and nuclear power; the four devices that make a record "in earnest"; and the channels a reader in China can actually reach. When to come back: when you need first-hand failure material for your own industry, or when you have to judge how much an outside accident report deserves to be trusted. Reading time: about 30 minutes.

    Failure knowledge does not accumulate out of conscientiousness. It accumulates out of institutions. Worldwide there are only about five fields that keep failure records at scale, and although none of them ever compared notes with the others, the machinery they grew looks strikingly alike. This chapter takes that machinery apart into four parts, then checks how far each of the five fields has assembled them, and finishes on the channels a reader in China can reach today.

    3.1 What "In Earnest" Means: Four Devices

    A field's failure-record system earns the word "earnest" only if four devices are in place at the same time.

    Device one: mandatory reporting. Statutes must state which events carry a reporting duty and what happens when a report is withheld. Where the duty is optional, the reporting rate tracks individual willingness from month to month, and the sample stops meaning anything.

    Device two: independent investigation. The investigating body answers neither to the party under investigation nor to the enforcement arm that issues penalties. Its product is a statement of what happened and why. Allocating blame is a separate trade. Merge the two and the party under investigation will buy safety with silence.

    Device three: publication in full. Reports must be obtainable whole, numbered, and citable. A field that issues press releases and nothing else has headlines and no body text.

    Device four: liability separation. What is written in a report must, as a matter of principle, stay out of proceedings against individuals, and the statements of those involved must carry no threat of prosecution (gross negligence excepted). Take this one away and the other three collapse together, because nobody writes into a report the details that would put him in prison.

    The four devices lock into one another. Without the second, the fourth has no institutional carrier. Without the third, the output of the first two cannot circulate. Without the first, the sample facing the other three is defective at the source.

    One further criterion belongs in front of the inspection, and it is a criterion about criteria: redundancy. For each device, is a second body keeping a backup copy of the function? Beyond the NTSB there is the FAA's continuing oversight, the insurance industry's investigation teams, and the industry's own voluntary reporting systems. An agency that is the only one doing its job is a candidate for Chapter 22 no matter how good it is. Redundancy varies enormously between fields. Aviation's four devices each stand on several legs; most industries have one leg per device, sometimes one leg across all four. The number of legs is what decides how a record system survives a budget crisis, a political intervention, or the departure of the one person who cared. A reader scoring his own field should add a fifth column to the four: how many legs does each device stand on. The five-column total is the operational definition of the word in this chapter's title.

    The interlock also has a direction, and the direction is worth drawing as a dependency graph. Let liability separation fail and report content self-censors, a failure that propagates downstream from device four. Let publication fail and independent investigation works for nothing, propagating from three to two. Let independent investigation fail and mandatory reporting loses its point, propagating from two to one. The repair order is that graph read backwards: first the holes in mandatory reporting, then the independence of investigation, then publication, and liability separation last. The value of the order is diagnostic. When a field's record system has gone to seed, find the worst device and start there; repair anywhere else has no leverage. The lever is checked with one question: in this field's reports, does the person involved dare write the truth? On that question the first priority is always device four.

    3.2 Aviation

    Aviation is the prototype of this system, and it earned the role through accumulation. Aviation's record institutions were not designed in one sitting; they were bought layer by layer with disasters, and behind each reporting duty and each independent agency sits a famous investigation report. For a designer the stratified history is a free course: each founding document records the correspondence between the accident shape of its moment and the gap it exposed. The stratification continues, with record rules for unmanned aircraft and the airworthiness framework for electric vertical take-off and landing vehicles being written now, which makes new vehicle categories a live sample of institution-building. Anyone pushing for better records at home can treat aviation's history as the most complete set of construction drawings available. The drawings are aviation's; the mechanics of the structure are general.

    At the international level, ICAO Annex 13, Aircraft Accident and Incident Investigation, sets three principles. The state of registry and the state of occurrence both carry an obligation to investigate. Investigation is separated from judicial accountability, and the report is not used to apportion blame. Reports should be published as promptly as possible. Major states have set up independent agencies under Annex 13 and produce a steady output: the NTSB in the United States, the AAIB in the United Kingdom, the BEA in France, the JTSB in Japan, the BFU in Germany, the ANSV in Italy, the AESA in Spain (with the CIAIAC under it), the ATSB in Australia, and the TSB in Canada. Reports are published in full and numbered, as with NTSB AAR-10/01 or AAIB AAR 1/2023, and major investigations open a public docket in which physical evidence, interview records, and transcripts of communications are itemized entry by entry.

    China's civil aviation runs inside that international framework. The Civil Aviation Administration of China organizes accident investigations and publishes reports under the Annex 13 structure. Major accidents appear as progress bulletins: the March 21, 2022 crash of China Eastern flight MU5735, in which all 123 passengers and 9 crew members died, produced a progress bulletin from the CAAC on March 20, 2024. How fast the final report arrives and how complete it is are questions the reader can settle from the case library (see the av-038 entry in Appendix B).

    Aviation produces a second thing that gets overlooked: follow-up. Every NTSB safety recommendation carries a number, the recipient must answer in writing, and whether the recommendation was adopted is a matter of public record. Failure knowledge is not only the analysis. It is also the ledger of what changed after the analysis.

    The ledger has cross-industry value. The adoption-rate curve is a direct indicator of safety governance: sustained high adoption means a working channel from recommendation to practice, and a slide means fatigue or financial strain. The data are public in the NTSB's recommendation status database and on the CSB's recommendation tracking pages, and the reading belongs to Chapter 19's landing-rate logic, where the landing rate is what an organization charges for the truth. The two chapters interlock exactly here. A reader outside aviation should still memorize the mechanism, because it is portable: any regulator's recommendation deserves a number, a named recipient, a response deadline, and a public status.

    The cost of the aviation system is on the record too: investigations run long. Take the January 2, 2024 collision at Tokyo Haneda between a Japan Airlines A350 and a Japan Coast Guard aircraft. Five people died aboard the Coast Guard aircraft and its captain was seriously injured; all 379 people aboard the airliner were evacuated, with one passenger seriously injured and four lightly injured. The JTSB's second interim report, released December 25, 2025, still came without a final report, close to two years after the event (av-037, Appendix B). Deep investigation is priced in years. That is what "in earnest" costs.

    The other half of the price list is that speed is tiered. Aviation's graduated publication is the mature pattern: a preliminary report within days, an interim within months, a final when it is ready, safety recommendations at any time. The effect is to break the information flow into timestamped statements, each declaring what is known and what is not at that moment, which serves public information and investigative rigour at once, two demands usually treated as opposites. The cost of transplanting it is publication discipline, since every interim must separate the confirmed from the pending in its wording. The return is credibility: public patience with progress bulletins comes from bulletins that never claim more than the evidence holds. The Haneda interim reports are a working sample, and a reader can study them as a template.

    3.3 Chemicals and Process Industries

    In the United States the chemical sector's investigative device is the Chemical Safety and Hazard Investigation Board (CSB), created under the Clean Air Act Amendments of 1990, independent of the enforcement agencies OSHA and EPA, and restricted to investigation with no power to penalize. (The CSB began actual operations in 1998; checked against the About page of its website.) Its output comes in two parts: full investigation reports, which include animated reconstructions of the event and are unusually public-facing for an investigation agency, and execution tracking of its safety recommendations.

    The institutional difference from aviation is coverage. Aviation's investigator has jurisdiction over every major accident in the field. Chemical investigation resources concentrate on the worst events, while small and mid-sized releases and near misses are left to company management and local regulators. The UK's HSE system and the continental European arrangements are comparatively complete. In most developing countries, chemical accident records depend on press coverage and full texts are scarce. In China, chemical accident investigation reports are issued by the Ministry of Emergency Management and by provincial and municipal emergency management departments; major accidents get a public report, smaller ones get a bulletin, and completeness varies (Chapter 31 carries the channel details).

    A further dimension of chemical recordkeeping sits inside the fence line: the internal records of a process safety management (PSM) system. A mature PSM system keeps three families of record alive year round, covering process hazard analysis, management of change, and mechanical integrity. Their quality sets the pace at which the company learns, and in an investigation they form a key evidence layer. Internal records and public investigation are complements: the report yields the industry's lesson, the internal record yields this plant's specifics. How well the layers are connected is a gauge of learning capacity, and at most companies they are not connected at all. The report is read and filed, the records stay locked in the system, and nobody does the translation. That post, mapping an industry report's lessons onto the facilities on your own site, has a ready tool in Chapter 35's locating table.

    3.4 Maritime and Transport

    Maritime failure records are built from the rule framework of the International Maritime Organization (IMO) plus the accident investigation bodies of individual states. The UK's MAIB is the model agency: reports published in full, independence from maritime justice, and an investigative stance aimed at safety improvement rather than fault-finding.

    What makes maritime distinctive is fragmented jurisdiction. The state whose flag the ship flies leads the investigation, and the flag state, the coastal state, and the state of the crew's nationality may be three different agencies. Report quality therefore tracks the institutional level of whichever flag happens to be involved. The same accident under a different flag may leave no public full text at all, which is a warning worth carrying: in the maritime field, the absence of a report frequently says nothing about the absence of an accident.

    The industry has a consensus model for this: a lead investigating state with assisting states, jurisdiction concentrated in one body while the others help as needed. How well it works depends on the good faith of the assistance, meaning how fast data are shared and whether access to witnesses is granted, and gaps in that good faith are the largest single reason maritime investigations are slow and incomplete. Hence a cheap reading habit: look twice at a report's acknowledgements. The list of assisting states and technical assistance is the report's collaboration network, and the wider the net, the more complete the perspectives inside.

    A second line of repair runs through the industry's own records. Safety management system rules require ship operators to keep maintenance and event records aboard, which gives a second layer of documentation beneath the flag state's paperwork; the cross-checking value of reading the two layers side by side was set out in Chapter 16. And fragmentation has a search implication. A single accident can leave several documents behind: the lead state's investigation report, the flag state's position paper, the coastal state's administrative record, the insurer's survey report. They fill in different parts of the picture, with the lead report giving the process, the insurance report giving the loss, and the administrative record giving the penalty. The complete search move is document genealogy, pulling the existence of the other documents out of one report's references, and A maritime reader should practise it until it is a habit.

    One more public channel in this field deserves registration: port state control detention records. Which ships were detained and why are public, and the record works as a health check on the state of a hull, in a form that rewards batch analysis.

    3.5 Medicine and Devices

    Medicine's device takes a different shape. What it records is not the accident but the adverse event and the adverse reaction. The FDA's MAUDE database collects medical device adverse event reports, with mandatory reporting duties on manufacturers and on user facilities; the drug side runs on the FDA's Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) and on the European Union's pharmacovigilance system; China's counterpart is the National Medical Products Administration's (NMPA) bulletins and recall announcements together with the drug adverse reaction monitoring system. 〔Verification pending: the NMPA site loads dynamically in JavaScript; annual report figures to be checked once access is in place; this sentence is qualitative.〕

    Score medicine against the four devices. Reporting is mandatory, so device one is present. The investigation side has no aviation-style independent accident body, so device two is missing, with post-market "investigation" replaced by signal detection and safety updates. Publication is half there, since the databases are queryable while individual records vary in quality. Liability separation is present, because the device adverse event reporting rules state explicitly that a report is not a determination of liability (see the language of 21 CFR Part 803, with the manufacturer duty at 21 CFR 803.50, checked against the current eCFR; user facility reporting has its own provision in the same part).

    That structure produces a skew peculiar to medical data. Reporting rates move with how recent an event is and with how hard the causal link is to judge, and the same adverse event can arrive several times through several channels or never arrive at all.

    Duplication and undercount together give the data user an operating rule: aggregate by event rather than by report count, merge the several channel reports of one event, and declare the gap left by under-reporting when you interpret anything. Aggregation needs event identification fields, a composite key of product, time, and symptom; records missing them, typically free text in older systems, must be read by hand, and that reading is the main cost of medical data analysis. Cross-border advice for readers from other fields: whenever you cite a medical figure, state that it counts reports, not cases. The distance between the two numbers is a function of the reporting system, and every loosening or tightening moves the ratio. One line of disclosure blocks nine tenths of the misuse. To Chapter 2's threshold checks add one rule: read medical data as signals, and treat any use as evidence as requiring separate epidemiological verification.

    There is a further direction worth adding, about the gap between signal and evidence: the absence of a signal is itself a signal. A device that has been on the market for years and shows zero reports in MAUDE may be safe, or may reflect an absent reporting habit, since compliance with user facility reporting duties is low worldwide. The correct reading of a zero is uncertainty, which is neither proof of safety nor evidence of a hidden problem, and the action it prompts is active checking: follow-up literature from clinical trials, comparable data from other jurisdictions. Misreading the zero is the common cold of medical data use, taking a quiet database for a clean product. This chapter files it under the medical edition of record skew. The phrase to keep on hand when citing medical data is: what grade of quiet is this quiet?

    3.6 Nuclear Power and Energy

    Nuclear power has the deepest international sharing of any field.

    One detail of that sharing deserves study by other industries: the grading of events. The International Nuclear Event Scale (INES) puts events on a single 0 to 7 scale with explicit criteria at every level. Its governance value lies in the language, since a common scale lets insiders and outsiders talk: the public understands that a 6 outweighs a 5, and the press no longer invents its own explanation. The cost is coarsening, because differences within a level are flattened and two level 1 events can carry wildly different risk. Coarse and fine is a permanent trade-off; coarse serves public communication, fine serves professional analysis, and running both at once is the mature arrangement. Any field building event grading can copy the two-layer structure, coarse outside and fine inside, with INES's public documents as a model for writing the criteria.

    The International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) and the OECD Nuclear Energy Agency jointly operate the Incident Reporting System (IRS). Operating organizations in member states report operating events, and the database is shared among member states. The US Nuclear Regulatory Commission requires licensed operators to submit operating event reports (regulatory location: 10 CFR Part 50.73, the licensee event report system, checked against the current eCFR). The World Association of Nuclear Operators (WANO) runs a further reporting and assessment system inside the industry that is not open to outsiders. Nuclear events also carry an INES rating, published on the 0 to 7 scale.

    The weak point in nuclear's assembly is publication. IRS detail is restricted to member states, WANO is entirely closed, and what the open world can obtain is whatever each national regulator chooses to publish. This is the first sample in the book of a system that is institutionally serious and externally sealed: how deep a reader can go depends on the openness of his own country's regulator.

    The sealed side has an open substitute inside the industry. Some of WANO's technical material is published as ordinary documents, training material and summaries of operating experience analysis, shallower than the database but usable. The way to use it is to borrow across fields: a reader from another industry can read nuclear's public output as a textbook, because nuclear operating experience analysis is among the most rigorously written public material in process industries. The direction of the borrowing is worth naming plainly. Nuclear is missing device three and its internal rigour is the ceiling for the whole industrial world. The gap between inside and outside is a fact of the institution, and the spill of internal material into publications is a ride the reader can take for free.

    3.7 Assembling the Four Devices

    Aviation Full Full Full Full (Annex 13 principles) The prototype; the most complete tracking ledger
    Chemicals Medium (major accidents) Independent in the US through the CSB; uneven elsewhere Medium Medium Small and mid-sized accidents rest on internal records
    Maritime Full Split (follows the flag state) Medium Full Fragmented jurisdiction
    Medicine and devices Full (adverse events) Missing (replaced by signal detection) Medium (individual database records vary) Full Read the data as signals
    Nuclear power Full Full Split (international systems closed; national regulatory documents vary in openness) Full External publication is the weak point

    The first use of the table: find the row for your own industry, or the nearest neighbouring row. The gaps in that row are the discount to apply when you read outside cases.

    The second use: find the device your field is missing and borrow cases from a field that has it. Chemicals has no public sample for small accidents, so a chemical reader borrows maritime individual reports; the mechanism is the same and the parameters are converted on your own desk. The borrowing rule is in Section 3.9 below and in Chapter 23, which requires extrapolation to be declared: name the source industry, state the conversion assumptions, keep the account visible.

    The third use is for institution builders. If you get a seat at the design of a record system, this table is the parts list: copy aviation's full set, de-specify to the budget, and declare what you de-specced rather than hiding it. All three uses rest on one stance. The table grades no field; it tells each reader where he stands. In chemicals, major accidents give you CSB-grade full texts and small ones give you nothing public, so your study of small accidents runs on analogy from aviation or maritime, and how much to discount that analogy is a number you write down yourself.

    3.8 Where China Stands

    A reader in China has five channels to failure records, and they differ sharply in availability and in quality.

    Channel one: accident investigation reports. Under the Regulations on the Reporting, Investigation and Handling of Production Safety Accidents (State Council Decree No. 493), serious and especially serious accidents are investigated at the provincial level or by the State Council. Reports on major chemical and industrial accidents are published by the Ministry of Emergency Management and by provincial departments, with completeness varying from case to case. Against the four devices: mandatory reporting is present; independent investigation is weak, because the investigation team is government-led and staffed from several departments, leaving investigators and the people who allocate blame heavily overlapping; publication is incomplete, with plenty of bulletins and few full texts; liability separation is weak, because reporting and accountability run as one process, and a sequence that assigns blame before it finishes investigating steers every statement toward self-protection. 〔Verification pending: the original State Council gazette, Article 19 onward, needs checking.〕

    A secondary phenomenon of missing liability separation deserves its own entry: language drift. A report written under accountability pressure drifts toward the unfalsifiable. Timestamps lose precision, descriptions of actions turn into characterizations, names turn into job titles. The drift is measurable. Compare the reports of a single institution across years, and the annual change in linguistic precision works as a thermometer of accountability pressure. The measurement has governance value, since the year the language shifts is usually the year the accountability procedure changed, and that correlation points the repair at procedure design rather than at individuals. The use of linguistic evidence in this chapter is institutional diagnosis: take the language's temperature first, then talk about amending the institution.

    A reading exercise for this section: take one published major accident report and work through it section by section, asking which section corresponds to which device. Facts map to device one, analysis to device two, the manner of publication to device three, the accountability section to device four. What comes out is a credibility map of that report, showing which sections can be cited freely and which need a second look, because accountability and facts often carry different weight. Three reports are enough to learn the drawing, and the skill lasts a working lifetime, since any investigation report, Chinese or foreign, can be priced quickly with the same frame. Making that pricing ability common is this chapter's central delivery to Chinese readers: the number of channels is a fact of where you were born, and pricing what they produce is trainable.

    The comparison also gives a company reader a place to start on his own organization. Score your internal reporting system against the four devices. Is internal reporting a duty or a virtue? Where does the internal investigation team sit relative to the department it investigates? How granularly are findings published to the whole staff? What written protection does a reporter have? Most companies come out short on devices two and four, with internal investigation and HR running on one line and no written protection for the person who speaks. Chapter 19 covers external intake and exemption clauses and Chapter 30 covers handover protection, and both carry ready designs. This chapter's only job is the reminder: internal systems and national systems run on the same mechanics, and what a state can learn, a firm can learn.

    Channel two: civil aviation. The CAAC works within the ICAO Annex 13 framework, which makes this the channel that meshes best with the four devices of the five. The MU5735 progress bulletin came through it.

    Channel three: drugs and devices. NMPA recall announcements, bulletins, and the annual report on drug adverse reactions are standardized public outlets. The depth of any single record is limited, but retrieval works.

    Channel four: court judgments. China Judgements Online once offered the largest public body of judgments in the world, and tort, product liability, and labour dispute documents carry a great deal of detail about organizational failure. Availability has changed in recent years. 〔Verification pending: the Supreme People's Court's annual work reports and judicial statistics need checking; this sentence is qualitative.〕

    Channel five: exchange and regulatory documents. CSRC administrative penalty decisions, exchange inquiry letters and regulatory letters, and audit reports cover financial and governance failure at listed companies, in full text, publicly available, in a uniform format.

    Stack the five and the conclusion is this: readers in China have usable full-text channels in civil aviation, in drugs, and in listed-company compliance. In chemicals, public safety, and several other areas, full texts are scarce, bulletins and press paraphrase carry the load, and the grade of the learning material drops below B (grade definitions in Chapter 10).

    One combined technique with the five channels deserves teaching on its own: cross-channel corroboration, where documents about the same matter check one another. A CSRC penalty decision and a court judgment corroborate the detail of financial fraud; an NMPA recall announcement and a judgment corroborate a product quality dispute; a CAAC bulletin and a judgment corroborate an operational dispute. The output is an upgrade in evidence grade: B-grade material from a single channel can support a reconstruction tag once its core facts are corroborated through a second channel, under the rules in Chapter 10 and Appendix C. The cost is one search. The return is material promoted a grade, and corroboration is the retrieval move a Chinese reader should practise until it is automatic. It is the dividend this five-channel structure pays to the careful. The same structure sets the distribution of evidence grades among this book's China cases, and explains why most of them are tagged "reconstructed".

    3.9 Putting the Map to Work

    Three moves.

    Move one: find the nearest primary library for your own industry. That means a specific database and a specific report numbering scheme, not a search engine.

    Move two: read three recent full-text reports from your industry and note, for each, which of the four devices is present and which is missing. The missing one is the extra caution to carry into every page.

    Move three: if none of the five fields is close to your industry, then before you set the strength of any analogy from a neighbouring field, run Chapter 2's three sample checks.

    A fourth move sits outside those three: build the table of Section 3.7 for your own industry, with a column per device, a mark for present or missing, and a count of legs. The criteria are this chapter's and can be copied straight across. Building it is itself a survey of your field's record institutions, with the question list already supplied above, and it costs an afternoon. What it produces is a map of your industry's learning infrastructure as it stands, and the gaps on that map are the agenda for a proposal to your trade association or regulator. An agenda beats a complaint: a proposal that arrives with an assembly table is the only kind taken seriously at an industry meeting.

    The Five Self-Checks

    1. Which of the four devices is missing from your field's accident reporting system? Last year, how much detail did that gap cost in one specific case?
    2. Your industry's most recent major failure: at what address does the full investigation report sit? How many minutes do you need to find it?
    3. Beyond regulatory inspection, does your organization keep its own failure records? How would you estimate the reporting rate?
    4. If only one could survive, the regulatory report or the internal postmortem, which would your organization keep? What follows from that choice?
    5. Of China's five channels, how many have you used? For the ones you have not: did you not know the entrance, or did you search and come away empty?

    A weekend assignment goes with the five questions, one full tour of the channels in half a day. In the morning, run channels one and two: a full text of a major accident report, and an aviation bulletin. In the afternoon, run channels three through five: an NMPA recall announcement, a court judgment, and a CSRC penalty decision. Read every document with the same question in hand: how many of the four devices show in this text. The output is five sets of reading notes plus a quick-reference table of China's record institutions drawn by you, closer to your own use than the table in Section 3.8. A reader who finishes the assignment has converted the map of Chinese failure records from something heard about into something walked. That sense of footing comes before any retrieval work: the map comes first, and the tools only bite once the map is there.
