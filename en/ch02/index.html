    Chapter 2 Three Biases: Recording, Survivorship, Attribution

    What this chapter covers: how the failure sample you are handed has been filtered three times, by institutions, by human nature and by narrative, and the three specific things you will learn wrongly if you study from a filtered sample. When to turn back: when you cite a case to support a decision, when you design a review process, when you meet a number like "industry accident rate". Estimated reading time: 35 minutes.

    Before you read any failure case, settle one question first: where did this sample come from? The sample in your hands was never drawn from the full population of failures. It passed through three filters, and each filter systematically favors a particular kind of failure. The first filter sits in the recording stage, the second in the telling stage, the third in the attribution stage. This chapter takes them apart one at a time, and after each one asks the same question: if you learn from this filtered sample, what exactly do you learn wrong?

    2.1 The Recording Bias: Filing Rules Choose Your Sample for You

    For a failure to leave a written trace, it has to clear a threshold. Institutions set that threshold. They decide how severe an event must be before it is investigated, written up and archived. Failures below the line, however many there are, do not exist in the written world.

    Beyond the filing threshold stands a second gate that nobody advertises: the language gate. Records are written in professional documentary prose, while most first-hand descriptions of failure arrive in speech. Speech does not fit the format, so it is lost or deformed in transcription, and the casualties cluster at the front line. The veteran fitter's way of putting it, the nurse's hunch, the driver's foreboding: all unstructured language, none of it able to fill the boxes on an accident form. The remedy is dual-track recording. Fill the official track as required and store the spoken track separately, as an attachment layer to the file: interview transcripts, the raw wording from the floor, kept verbatim. The cost is storage and tidying. The value lands in future investigations, because a formal document answers compliance questions while a spoken track answers understanding questions, and the understanding layer is what most investigations lack. Dual-track recording compensates for the threshold and the language gate at once, and it is an advance line of defence for the handover rupture in Chapter 30: what the spoken track preserves is the part handovers lose first.

    Aviation has the lowest recording threshold and the most complete coverage of any industry, which makes it a convenient ruler. The U.S. National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) investigates every accident involving scheduled passenger airlines (carriers operating under 14 CFR Part 121). For general aviation accidents (private light aircraft, flying clubs, agricultural and forestry work) it sends an on-scene team to only a share of them, closing the rest in writing. 〔Verification pending: on-scene coverage rates would need checking against NTSB Annual Review volumes; this sentence is a qualitative statement〕 Even in the best-recorded domain on earth, then, the reports you read cluster around the gravest consequences, the most common aircraft types, or the events that happen to be instructive. All forty aviation cases in this book's case library earned a formal investigation. That is what a threshold looks like from the inside.

    China's recording system supplies a second ruler. The Regulations on the Reporting, Investigation and Handling of Production Safety Accidents (State Council Decree No. 493, 2007) sort accidents into four grades by deaths, injuries and direct economic loss: general, relatively serious, serious, particularly serious. Each grade goes to a different tier of government for investigation. Grading is a sensible way to allocate resources, and it doubles as a screen. Investigative capacity follows the grade, so the industry's large volume of sub-serious injuries, damaged equipment and near misses has almost no public written existence. Civil aviation is the exception: the Civil Aviation Administration of China publishes investigation reports and progress bulletins inside the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) Annex 13 framework, which makes it one of the few domains with a full-text outlet. 〔Verification pending: the current text of the Civil Aircraft Incident Investigation Regulations (CCAR-395) needs checking〕

    The shape of the threshold is legible from inside the records too. The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission recall notice for Cub Cadet commercial lawn mowers (Appendix B, mn-010) records that 106 fuel-leak reports had piled up, one of them a fire, before the recall came. The records were never missing; institutional pacing kept them behind the risk. Set that against a foodborne outbreak investigation (Appendix B, ag-001: 33 days from first illness to production halt and recall) and the difference in threshold height between two industries in the same period is plain.

    There is a counter-intuitive corollary here worth spelling out. The threshold is the price tag on a learning opportunity. The mechanism is sample size. In a high-threshold industry, reports on a class of accident are sparse, so confirming a pattern means waiting for the pattern to repeat several times. In a low-threshold industry, the pattern is written into the textbook on its first appearance. Aviation's long run of progress has an underrated cause here: people credit the technology and the culture, while the density of the recording system is the foundation underneath both. For a policy maker the corollary is direct. Every cut in the filing threshold, every near miss pulled into the record, is a trade of administrative cost for learning speed, and inside this book's frame that trade is almost always worth making: records are priced in thousands, accidents in tens of millions. So before you quote any accident rate, ask where the threshold sits.

    A quick table helps fix an industry's position by its recording regime, in four tiers. Tier one, mandatory filing with full-text publication: civil aviation accidents, major hazardous-chemical accidents. Tier two, mandatory filing with restricted publication: most workplace injuries, routine food and drug enforcement. Tier three, voluntary reporting with aggregate publication: near-miss systems, consumer complaint databases. Tier four, no institutional record at all: accidents in small and mid-sized firms, the quiet death of software, accidents at home. The table's use is to calibrate your language. Quote the tier whenever you quote a rate, because a rate without a tier is just a word count. One further corollary: comparing rates across industries is meaningless, since a tier one number and a tier four number are not the same kind of object. What is comparable across industries is whether a mechanism is present or absent, which is precisely what the matrix in Appendix A sets out to show.

    No record below the threshold does not mean no information. The information exists in another form: experience carried in practitioners' bodies. A senior mechanic knows which line has a temper. A nurse knows which combination of orders tends to go wrong. That knowledge leaves with staff turnover and resets to zero at retirement, because it never touched paper. The handover rupture in Chapter 30 is the account of how this sub-threshold knowledge disappears.

    What you learn wrong, in order of the damage it does. One: you treat recording frequency as occurrence frequency. "We never hear of that kind of accident here" usually means "we do not record that kind of accident here". Two: you treat recording quality as safety level. Aviation looks like the industry with the sharpest accident analysis because it has the best recording system, and those two facts are correlated while pointing in different directions. A field that suddenly produces a lot of failure analysis may be getting safer, or may simply have just cleared its filing threshold. Three: you ignore near misses by default. The statistics assembled after the fact show a crowd of near-miss precursors behind every major accident; a precursor that never crosses the threshold never enters the sample, so it never earns a place in the learning material.

    Formal recovery of near misses is the half-answer aviation has produced to the recording bias. The form is the voluntary reporting system: front-line staff report errors and near misses anonymously or under protection, and the report enters the database without penalty. Aviation's confidential reporting systems and flight safety action programmes are the mature models, and healthcare and maritime transport have carried versions across. The voluntary system moved sub-threshold failure into the written world for the first time, shifting the sample from a grading by consequence to a grading by risk: a near miss with small consequences and high risk finally gets a file. The word "half" deserves honesty. Voluntary rates rest on trust, meaning the protection promise and its delivery; break the promise once and the rate falls off a cliff, with the price list in Chapter 19 applying directly. So before you read any near-miss data, check the protection clause and the record of how it has been honoured. The temperature of the data is the temperature of the trust.

    A second route is active inspection: do not wait to be told, walk the site against a list. Safety inspections, process walkthroughs and shadow audits all belong here. Inspection pushes the defence from voluntary to institutional, so it no longer depends on the courage of a reporter; its cost is labour and its coverage is always partial. Its bias is checklist-shaped, finding the hazards on the list and missing new forms that are not on it, which means the list itself has to be updated from the accident library. Together the two routes give an industry a recognisable profile. Voluntary systems supply depth, the fine grain from the floor, and inspection supplies breadth, the corners nobody volunteers. How well those two legs cover the ground measures an industry's recording maturity more truly than any claim about its safety culture.

    2.2 The Survivorship Bias: The Storytellers Have Been Sifted

    The threshold filters events. Survivorship bias filters storytellers. Failure leaves no teller behind as a matter of structure. The failed lose the position, the platform and the credibility that a story needs, and the degree of failure runs in inverse proportion to willingness to tell: the more total the collapse, the fewer people left to describe it. The failure narratives in circulation therefore come mostly from people who are doing reasonably well.

    The filter has an occupational layer. People who stay in an industry after a failure work for someone, and what they can say is bounded by that employer: describing a former employer's failure carries career risk, because the trade is small and the circle is tight. The constraint pushes public telling toward two safe subjects, a former employer that has already folded and cannot retaliate, and episodes unconnected to the present employer that time has laundered. The overlap between the two sets is tiny, so total output is pressed very low. This is one micro-mechanism behind the question Chapter 4 takes up, why organizations seal off failure. The seal is a structure rather than a policy, and every potential teller is running career arithmetic inside it. The arithmetic shows up as scarcity in public content. When a candid failure review does reach you, remember that an author produced it after running that arithmetic: the courage is real, and so is the cost.

    Commercial publishing amplifies the filter. A failure chapter in a CEO memoir is written by the person who was on stage, recalling a peril that has since resolved; the executive who carried out the bad decision and lost the job over it usually has no book contract. Interview samples behave the same way, since founders who agree to sit for a failure interview are the ones with energy and social appetite left after failing. The U.S. data firm CB Insights assembles startup failure analyses from postmortems that founders submit voluntarily, a self-reported sample in which the more painful a case is, the likelier it is to be missing. (A compilation drawn from public postmortems and shutdown notices, self-selected sample; its pages have been checked)

    Software offers a sharper observation window because it has a public postmortem tradition, and Chapter 4 treats that exception on its own terms. The relevant point here is who writes them: companies that were still alive after the incident and still had an engineering team to write the document. A company that died of its architecture never issues a postmortem of the death; its architecture documents go offline with the servers. Even in the most transparent industry, then, the set of public postmortems is a survivor set. It covers the cases that fell ill and recovered, never the cases that died of the illness. The mechanisms behind those two may have nothing in common. One is an immune-system problem, the other organ failure, and only the first is available to you.

    Survivorship has a temporal form as well. Old cases that reach you have been through several rounds of selection, and the ones retold are the ones with drama, a teachable point, reach. The dull lethal cases drop out of circulation in silence. The countermeasure is to go back to the archive: pull every filed report for a given year rather than reading the three that everybody cites. Uniform sampling from the archive gives you an unbiased sample. It costs reading volume, since a year's output may run to dozens of reports. What it returns is the true density of an industry's memory: which patterns are frequent, and which are merely amplified by transmission. The gap between the two is the distortion of narrative, and being able to see that gap is a basic skill of reading reports. The three biases in this chapter are its tutorial.

    What you learn wrong, again in order of damage. One: the catalogue of ways to die systematically omits the most lethal entries. The fast, destructive endings, cash flow rupture, the core team leaving together, a single regulatory veto, are the endings with no time to write a review and the highest shame cost, so they appear least often in circulating knowledge and sit lowest in your own head. Two: you misjudge an industry's transparency. An industry with many postmortem articles may simply have many companies alive to write them; review culture and mortality are separate variables. Three: you learn risk avoidance from survivors. A survivor explaining what they got right is a lottery winner explaining how he picked numbers, methodologically speaking. Extract what the survivor says about what was visible at the time; discard the explanation of why they won.

    A purchasing-level move is available against survivorship: buy death samples. Three categories of public source exist. Regulators publish cancellation and revocation records, courts and registries publish bankruptcy and liquidation filings, industry databases publish shutdown lists. None carries a postmortem and all carry facts: when the firm died, how large it was, what its last public act was. The right use of a death sample is not to learn the cause of death, since nobody tells you, but to calibrate distribution: how many firms in your industry die in a year, at what size, in what pattern. The picture you build from survivor narratives is missing that entire block. Once the distribution is calibrated, every survivor lesson shows its conditions, and the first question becomes whether the dead ever tried the survivor's practice. Mostly there is no record either way, and that absence is itself a reading.

    The archival view of death samples holds one more item that surprises people: a dead company's digital estate outlives it longer than expected. Domain registration records, the delisting date in an app store, archived job postings, the last post on a social account: any of these puts dates on a death. Assembling the timeline means cross-dating public traces, precise enough to answer half the questions of business history, such as when decline began and when the last expansion move was made. The methodological bar is low, public sources plus patience. What is scarce is that nobody does it, since digital archaeology produces neither traffic nor degrees. This chapter carries it as a public toolkit for death-sample collection.

    2.3 The Attribution Bias: Every Review Is Written After the Ending

    The first two biases decide which cases reach you. Attribution decides what shape they are told in. Every review is written after the outcome is known, and knowing the ending changes how the reteller rebuilds the causal chain. Chance gets woven into necessity. Forks get flattened. The fog that was actually there gets compressed into clear error or clear correctness.

    The Challenger accident is the most thoroughly studied sample available. At 11:38 a.m. Eastern time on 28 January 1986, Challenger lifted off from Cape Canaveral, Florida, and broke up about 73 seconds into flight, killing all seven astronauts aboard. The Rogers Commission report opens Volume 1, Chapter 3, "The Accident", with the sentence that the flight began at 11:38 a.m. EST on 28 January 1986, and records that it ended 73 seconds later in an explosive burn of hydrogen and oxygen propellants; the official text is at nasa.gov/history/rogersrep/v1ch3.htm, read and checked for this book on 2026-11-01. On the evening before, 27 January 1986, engineers at the contractor Morton Thiokol recommended in a teleconference against launching in the cold. Their grounds were that O-rings in the solid rocket booster field joints recovered from earlier flights had shown erosion at low temperature, and that evidence linked low temperature to seal failure. Management at the company then changed its position and approved the launch. One sentence from the meeting where the position changed is on the record: take off your engineering hat and put on your management hat (Rogers Commission report, Volume 1, Chapter 5, "Contributing Causes", where Boisjoly's testimony recounts Mason's words to Lund as "take off your engineering hat and put on your management hat"; official text at nasa.gov/history/rogersrep/v1ch5.htm, read and checked for this book on 2026-11-01).

    With the ending known, attribution can be run at several depths. The physical layer is the first: cold robbed the O-rings of their resilience, and hot gas burned through the seal. That layer holds fully, and it is the physical mechanism the Rogers Commission itself established. The organizational layer is the second, and it is the explanation Diane Vaughan gave in The Challenger Launch Decision (University of Chicago Press, 1996). The company and the agency had launched before with erosion visible in the joints, and had succeeded each time; each success was written up as evidence that the deviation sat inside acceptable limits; the decision standard moved gradually under the weight of those successes, and the decision on the evening of 27 January was one routine slide along that track. This layer holds as well.

    The two layers do not conflict, and they train entirely different readers. A reader who stops at the physical layer learns that cold is dangerous, and goes back to his own organization to put a gate on a single parameter called temperature. A reader who takes in the organizational layer goes back to his own ledger with a different question: do we have a deviation indicator that we have already run successfully many times, each time explained as still having margin? One case, and the depth of attribution determines what the learning produces. Which layer reaches you in the market depends on who is telling. Accident bulletins gravitate to the physical layer, management scholars to the organizational layer, participants' memoirs to the luck layer.

    Two further layers complete the set. The incentive layer reads the decision-maker's behaviour against the rewards and penalties in force at the time, what the appraisal system paid for and what it punished, which is the frame of Chapter 17. The structural layer asks whether another person in the same structure would have made the same choice, and the historical cases where the structure stayed put, the personnel changed and the accident repeated are its hard proof. The mnemonic for running all four in order: the physical layer answers how it broke, the organizational layer how it went wrong, the incentive layer why the pull toward proceeding was strong, the structural layer why it happened again. Only a reader who runs all four has earned the whole lesson from one accident; a reader who runs one gets the first page of the same syllabus every time.

    Attribution bias also has an institutional form, and it deserves a straight look: an investigation report is itself written after the fact. The investigators know the ending. Procedure requires them to exhaust hypotheses before converging, yet the pull of narrative is always there. The counterweights are procedural: evidence numbering, a public case file, screening of multiple hypotheses, every conclusion tagged with its evidence grade. NTSB reports attach an evidence chain to each conclusion, so a reader can disagree with a finding and still audit the reasoning, which is the fundamental reason such reports beat news retellings. Residual bias cannot be zeroed. It can be compressed.

    Three steps correct for what remains. Read the structure: the table of contents and the numbering of conclusions. A report whose conclusions are individually numbered and evidence-tagged has had its bias pressed down by procedure; a report that runs as one continuous story lets bias grow freely. Read the dissent: did the board record any disagreement during the investigation? Dissent makes an investigation more credible, and a unanimous conclusion deserves a second look for narrative gravity. Read the follow-up: evidence that arrived after publication. A report whose conclusions were revised keeps its most honest material in the revision record, and an agency willing to revise has earned long-term trust. The three steps cost an extra hour, and they buy a defensible price for any investigative conclusion. A report is neither scripture nor waste paper: it is the best sample available, carrying residual bias.

    What you learn wrong here, in order of damage. One: you overrate "they should have seen it". Played backwards from the ending, every fork is clearly visible; standing at the decision point, information arrives mixed with noise. Two: you fail to acquire field judgment. Reading finished attribution is watching someone else's finished game, and your own play does not improve, which is why every case in this book stops at the decision point, the third clause of the reader's contract in the preface. Three: you flatten complex failure into a single cause. Post hoc attribution tends to converge on one root cause, while a real failure chain usually spans the technical, organizational and incentive layers (the failure-chain model in Chapter 9). A flattened root cause is convenient for briefing and useless for prevention.

    Rebuilding a decision point is a semi-technical drill you can teach yourself on any review document. Step one, sort by time: order the material's information by arrival, separate what came before the decision from what came after, and tag everything after as hindsight. Step two, assign weight: for each pre-decision item, record how reliable it looked, how urgent, and how much noise it was mixed into. Step three, find the forks: list every option that existed at the moment, including the ones killed off in a single sentence. What you hold is a desk reconstruction of the decision point, more accurate the fuller the material; where the material is thin, say so. The drill costs about an hour per document, and it trains the capability Chapter 6 formalizes as what was visible at the time. This chapter gives the household version; Chapter 6 gives the instrument version.

    Against the single-cause briefing habit there is an institutional antidote: conclusions written in layers. The report states its physical-layer conclusions, its organizational-layer conclusions and its incentive-layer conclusions in separate sections, and the summary is not allowed to merge them into one sentence. This is the standard structure of a good investigation report, where findings are numbered one by one and each carries a layer tag and an evidence-strength tag. Move it into internal reviews and the rule becomes concrete: no root-cause field on the conclusions page. Use three columns instead, one each for technical, organizational and incentive, with at least one entry per column. The three-column review costs two extra minutes in the briefing and yields three times the handles in prevention, since one entry per layer is three things that can actually be changed. Between briefing efficiency and preventive effect, this chapter recommends the second every time.

    2.4 What You Actually Hold Once the Three Biases Stack

    A compressed answer first, then the unpacking. Once the three biases stack, what you hold is a collection of failures written for you by survivors, filtered by thresholds, and reassembled after the fact, and your brain will digest it as a full sample, a neutral account and causal truth. The compressed version sets the tone for the rest of this chapter. Every technique here, the threshold table, the three quality checks, the four layers, the desk reconstruction, is resistance to that digestion error. The posture behind the resistance is an admission: bias cannot be cleaned to zero. What can be done is to put a scale on each document so the reader carries it into the reading. Two readers, one with the scale and one without, will split their trust differently over the same document. That is the whole capability this chapter means to hand over.

    All three biases act on every case at once. Run a full check-up on aviation, the lightest case in any industry: recording is mandatory, telling is done by a statutory body, attribution runs under procedural discipline. Even so it carries residuals. Near misses are largely absent, which is the recording bias. Public reviews are written by institutions that still exist, which is the survivorship bias. The report's structure converges on causes, which is the attribution bias. Other industries carry more, never less. Chemicals has a filing regime whose coverage concentrates on major accidents. Software has a review culture whose sample is all survivors. Healthcare event reporting runs at low voluntary rates in every country. Finance scatters its failure record across penalty documents and litigation, and almost nobody assembles it at industry level.

    Here is a sample quality check you can run on any case before reading it, three questions long.

    1. Check the threshold. How did this case enter the record: voluntary report, mandatory filing, media exposure? The threshold tells you which consequence tier it belongs to.
    2. Count the autopsies. What is the teller's stake in the outcome? Does the institution still exist? Was the teller a decision-maker, an executor or a bystander at the time?
    3. Take the attribution apart. Does the account mark the line between visible at the time and reconstructed afterwards? An account that marks nothing defaults to hindsight from beginning to end.

    Run the three and your trust in a case has a scale on it. Fields 6 and 12 of the case card, visible at the time and evidence grade, are these three checks done in advance on the writing side.

    The scale has one last place to go: into other people's hands. The fastest way to internalize the three biases is to explain them to your team, and one telling beats ten readings. The skeleton is in this chapter: one sentence per bias, the four-tier industry table, the three sample checks, the four-layer mnemonic. Four pieces of material fill ninety minutes without strain. For the live exercise, take the team's most recent internal review and run the three checks on it in the room: threshold, teller, layers. The effect shows at once, because the team reads reviews differently afterwards. The cost is ninety minutes, and what it buys is an upgrade in the team's literacy in failure knowledge, the most practical of the gifts the meta-question part gives an organization.

    One more drill on the reader's side consolidates the writing-side work: spot-check the book at random. Pick a card, read fields 1 to 5 only, commit to your own judgment and conclusion, then compare against field 6 and the outcome. The reading works two ways. The farther your judgment sits from the outcome, the stronger the hindsight habit in you. The closer it sits to field 6, the better you have learned to stand at the decision point. One card a month, twelve a year, and the bias becomes muscle memory, which forms no other way than in real judgments; the morning meeting script in Chapter 35 is the practice ground.

    The organizational form of the same immunity is worth writing down: put the three biases into the review template. Every internal review carries three fixed columns, what was this report's recording threshold, what is the teller's stake, how many layers did the attribution take. The cost is one extra page, and the effect is that every review declares its own provenance. A review that declares its bias has a credibility scale; one that declares nothing is discounted to the worst case by default. The columns share their source with the record-keeping regime in Chapter 38, since the credibility of a review is part of the record, and the philosophy extends one step: even the bias gets recorded.

    Five Self-Checks

    1. What is the accident recording threshold in your industry? In the past year, how many events crossed it, and how many did you know about that did not?
    2. Of the failure cases you have cited, how many trace back to a primary full text? If you can name the document and its number, it counts.
    3. Of the reviews your organization ran last year, how many separated information visible at the time from information known only afterwards?
    4. In your industry, who talks publicly about failure, the winners or the losers? Can you put an order of magnitude on the industry's autopsy rate?
    5. Take your team's most recent review and rewrite one causal conclusion in its visible-at-the-time version. Does the conclusion still stand?

    Beyond the five, there is an annual self-audit card for the three biases, kept personally. Question one: of the failure cases you cited in the past year, how many clear the threshold in 2.1, meaning they were formally filed and have a full text? Question two: of the failures you have told, how many were told with the people involved absent, since those present cannot tell it and you are telling it in their place? Question three: of the reviews you wrote, how many drew the line between visible at the time and reconstructed afterwards? The three answers make up your personal bias profile, and every gap in it is an improvement item for the coming year. The tools are all in this chapter: contact with voluntary systems, the purchase of death samples, the four-layer mnemonic, the three-step desk reconstruction. Profile and tools are both in place. What remains is the hours you put on the calendar.
