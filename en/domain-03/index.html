    Nuclear & Energy | nu

    Five columns: record quality, feedback delay, coupling, the three deadliest mechanisms, and what outsiders most easily mislearn. The case library for this domain has not been collected yet, so this page sets the frame first: the institutional picture and the mechanism judgements rest on the institutional analysis of Chapter 3. The empty case slots show up as a wholly empty column in Appendix A and will be filled once the collection is done.

    1 Record Quality

    Institutional depth here is the greatest of any industry, and the public face is split in two. Mandatory reporting and international sharing: the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) and the OECD Nuclear Energy Agency run the Incident Reporting System (IRS) together, and operating events from member states enter a shared pool; the U.S. NRC requires licensees to submit operating event reports (10 CFR Part 50.73). The other half is closed: the reports and assessments of the World Association of Nuclear Operators (WANO) are proprietary, circulating inside the industry and invisible from outside. What the open world can get is each regulator's own documents, NRC inspection reports and event reports, French ASN notices, with the depth of detail set by the country in question; retrieval routes and the direction for filling the gaps are in.

    2 Feedback Delay

    The longest in any industry. Design defects and material ageing return their feedback over decades. Operating events return theirs in months, filtered through several layers of review. Organizational and safety-culture consequences take longer still: the lessons of Chernobyl and Fukushima entered operating practice on a scale of decades. The one short-feedback layer is training and the simulator. This domain has almost no fast feedback, and all of its learning depends on institutional memory (Chapter 30).

    3 Coupling

    Perrow classes nuclear power as the archetype of a system that is high on both interactive complexity and tight coupling: the interactions between subsystems exceed anyone's full understanding, and failures travel faster than any intervention. Seventy years of defence amount to taming that coupling inside multiple physical barriers and defence in depth. Nuclear is therefore the other example of high coupling held down by institutions, standing alongside aviation but with different instruments: aviation through investigation, nuclear through physical barriers and redundancy. Coupling between plants is low, since sites are independent. Coupling across generations is high: designs carry over, standards carry over, and lesson sharing depends on international systems.

    4 The Three Deadliest Mechanisms (from Institutional Analysis, Case Slots Pending)

    The illusion of redundancy (Chapter 22): common-cause failure of multiple defences is the standard shape of a nuclear accident, where common cause means an external event or a systemic design or cultural defect, and the shared-source failure of standby power and emergency systems is the most studied lesson in the field. Handover rupture (Chapter 30): passing operating experience between generations depends on closed industry systems (WANO, INPO), and institutionalizing organizational memory is the lifeline of this domain. Regulatory lag and capture (Chapter 31): licence extensions for design life and the certification of new reactor types keep a standing tension between the rules and the technology. This column will be revised against actual cases once the collection is done.

    5 What Outsiders Most Easily Mislearn

    Mislearning one: reading WANO's closed doors as an absence of failure records. The internal record density here may be the highest in the world; it is simply not published. Concluding that nuclear offers nothing to learn is a double misjudgement.

    Mislearning two: taking the INES scale for a linear measure of severity. It is logarithmic, and it tracks international public impact more than loss.

    Mislearning three: taking the visible layers of defence in depth for the actual line of defence. The common-cause check of Chapter 22 is a life-and-death question in a nuclear setting, and treating it as a compliance exercise is the error.

    6 Three Questions from Readers Outside the Field

    What use is a nuclear case to an industry that is not nuclear? Nuclear is the archetype of defence in depth and conservative decision making. Its redundancy design, its graded event regime and its mechanism for sharing operating experience are all reference frames for other industries (Chapter 3).

    The safety record is so good, is there anything left to learn? Precisely because the recording threshold is so low, with even an INES level 0 entered, this domain holds the thickest near-miss library anywhere. The learning value of near misses is the subject of Chapter 2: a low threshold produces earlier lessons rather than more records.

    What do the event levels mean? INES runs 0 to 7 on a severity scale, 0 having no safety significance and 7 a major release. Every nuclear case in this book sits between 1 and 3, and the lower levels carry the higher density of lessons: they are numerous, fully detailed, and most of them came out of luck rather than defence.

    10 Reservations

    Three limits apply to everything judged on this page. The book's case library samples toward events with rich public records, and the record skew is the subject of Chapter 2. The frequency ordering of mechanisms reflects the distribution inside this library, not the true distribution in the domain. Institutions and technology in the domain keep moving, so this page has a shelf life measured in years, and a reader quoting it should state the year of the quotation. All three limits are the book's general disclaimer, and the threshold check of Chapter 1 applies to the book itself as well.

    11 How the Three Layers Sound in This Domain

    The accent here is that the organizational layer is the thickest. Procedures, tags, watchkeeping, review levels: the density of organizational defence is the highest of any industry, and the INPO and WANO assessment systems are a complete model of organizational audit. Two consequences follow. A break at the organizational layer alone is very unlikely in a nuclear case, since that layer almost always catches it, so real failure almost always needs the incentive layer to cooperate: a budget cut leaves the organizational gate unrepaired for years, and appraisal pressure makes conservative decision making give way. For a reviewer: on the three-layer walkthrough of a nuclear case, the technical breakpoint usually enters only at the last moment, and before that there is a long rope running through the organizational and incentive layers. The far end of the rope is often a budget meeting ten years back.

    13 Closing Address

    One line for the nuclear reader: this industry has already installed the four devices to their limit (Chapter 3), and the real value of its cases for this book is the view of what residual risk looks like once defence density has hit the ceiling. The residual no longer comes from a missing record or an absent independent investigation, since both are there. It comes from the slow loosening of the organizational and incentive layers (Chapter 25 and Chapter 28). The nuclear case tells the reader that an institution is a starting point, and that keeping it running is the work. That is also why this book treats nuclear cases with the most caution, with an evidence grade and an archival source marked on every one.

    Four cases make this one of the thinnest domains in the book, and the thinness is itself a result of success: operating events run into the tens of thousands, major accidents into single digits. The INES grading lets a reader calibrate severity precisely. Level 1, which covers nu-001, nu-002 and nu-003, marks an operating anomaly; level 3 and above is an accident. The value of low-level cases was set out in Chapter 2: they are near misses turned into text. The recording regime of this industry converts what other industries leave unrecorded into a written record, and that is this domain's particular contribution to the book. Few cases do not mean few lessons. Lesson density is lessons divided by cases, and nuclear has the highest lesson density of any domain here.

    14 How the Mechanisms Vary in This Domain

    The nuclear variation on practical drift. Drift here happens almost entirely at the interface between paperwork and action, and the tag is the largest such interface. The two tagging errors in nu-001 and nu-002 share one shape: the logic on the tag sheet was right, the physical match was wrong, the wrong valve or the wrong cabinet was tagged, and the compensatory measures were recorded on paper with nobody going to the site to verify them. How to read it: whether the verification action was independent, a second pair of eyes, is the core detection point for drift in this domain. Independent verification is a written requirement of nuclear safety culture; what varies in practice is the density with which it is executed. The general criterion, that the rule is written so that nobody can follow it, barely holds here, since nuclear has a surplus of rules. The local criterion for drift has to be rewritten as: the rule set is complete and the verification step is omitted by custom.

    The nuclear variation on normalization of deviance. Normalization happens mainly on temporary exemptions for unavailable equipment. The technical specifications of a unit set hard limits on how long key equipment may be unavailable (the 24 hours in nu-001), and going past the limit requires a shutdown or a power reduction, so recovery just inside the limit becomes standard practice: the item comes back at hour 23 and 50 minutes, and the next cycle runs the same way. How to read it: take the distribution of recovery times against the limit, meaning the share of recoveries that land between hour 20 and hour 24. A share that is improbably high says the limit is being used as a target rather than a boundary, which is goal displacement (Chapter 12) mirrored into nuclear. The three-state register needs a fourth column here: how the item was restored, really or on paper.

    The nuclear variation on the invisibility of maintenance. The maintenance ledger here is the thickest in any industry; the thin one is the verification ledger. The lesson of nu-003, samples lost and an unregistered sealed source, shows another kind of maintenance: the ledger for sources and samples is kept by hand, the entry depends on an individual researcher's diligence, and the institution has no stock-count mechanism. How to read it: the frequency of a stock reconciliation audit, meaning the three-way check that the ledger has it, the site has it, and nothing is on site that the ledger lacks, is the right indicator for invisible maintenance in this domain. An audit that reads repair records and never reconciles stock is thick and hollow.
