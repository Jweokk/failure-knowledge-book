    Chapter 13 Narrative before Evidence; Motivated Reasoning

    What this chapter covers: how the conclusion gets ahead of the investigation. An explanation that is not yet mature gets said out loud in full, and from that point it filters the evidence, raises the bar for its opponents, and ties the organization to its own first version of events. Three criteria, four domains on the ground, four defences. When to turn back: before roadshow material is finalised, halfway through an accident-attribution meeting, when a project proposal enters the signature chain, and when you notice you are waiting for "supporting data". Estimated reading time: 50 minutes.

    There is a moment in the prehistory of almost every major failure, and it never appears in the causal chain of the accident report. It is the moment an immature explanation is spoken aloud in full for the first time. Said to investors, said to a superior, written into the summary field of a project proposal, dropped into a work chat. The nature of the explanation changes as it is said. Before that moment it is a hypothesis awaiting a test, and being wrong costs a revision. After it, it is a position, where being wrong costs credibility and testing it reads as provocation. This chapter is about what the organization does after that moment.

    13.1 What It Is

    Definition: narrative before evidence is an inversion in the order of publication. The conclusion goes public, gets committed to, and is written into documents before the evidence is assembled, and from then on every new piece of evidence is filed under the explanation already in place, while opponents carry a heavier burden of proof. The psychological engine is motivated reasoning. A large body of experiments in cognitive psychology shows that people form a position first and select evidence afterwards, and that this process is transparent to the person it happens in, invisible to introspection. Kunda's 1990 review is the standard citation in the field: Kunda, "The Case for Motivated Reasoning", Psychological Bulletin 108(3), 480–498, 1990 (Crossref record checked, DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.108.3.480). Motivated reasoning in a private head is harmless. Public commitment by an organization upgrades it into an institution: an individual who changes his mind has no audience, and an organization that changes its mind does.

    The experimental base is worth a paragraph, because it explains why "stay objective" is useless as a defence. Kunda's review concludes that direction enters at the stage of evaluating evidence: the same data set reads strong to one position and weak to another, and the whole operation presents itself subjectively as "I am simply analysing this rationally". Direction is transparent to the person holding it, which means any defence that relies on individual self-awareness is breached from the inside. That leaves one purpose for an organizational defence: turn the test into an external action that the process cannot skip, which is what Device 1 and Device 2 in this chapter do.

    Commitment has a second layer. Once a narrative enters a written document it acquires an independent life. Documents get forwarded, cited, and used as input to the next document, and each handover wears one layer off the uncertainty qualifiers, the "preliminary estimate", the "to be verified". By the third citation the hypothesis has become a fact. Nobody has to lie for this to happen; the wear occurs in retelling. The defence is document-level grade marking, which Device 1 writes into the page header: the reader can see at any moment which grade the conclusion in his hand belongs to.

    Narratives travel on three carriers, and each needs its own defence. The first is people: the founder's roadshow, the executive's statement, the expert's endorsement, defended by Device 2 (a disproof owner) and Device 3 (symmetric burden). The second is documents: offering documents, project proposals, approval cases, defended by Device 1 (grade marking) and Device 4 (the time gap). The third is metrics: the handful of numbers the narrative has selected will speak for it, as Chapter 12's presentation-layer move describes, and the defence is to pair every selected metric with a control that was not selected. Of the three, the metric carrier is the one most often left undefended. Numbers look more objective than people and more neutral than documents, and they are the most durable form the narrative takes: people go quiet when the meeting ends, documents get archived, and the metric refreshes on the first day of every month.

    The moment named in criterion ① can be seen in advance with one exercise. Draw a timeline for a major conclusion: time on the horizontal axis, four markers on it, being when the conclusion took shape, when it was said outward, when the verification plan started, and when verification finished. A healthy project puts the four points in that order from left to right. A sick one puts the second point ahead of the third, sometimes ahead of the first. The line costs nothing and can be drawn by hand in any meeting. Its value is that it turns narrative before evidence from a retrospective mood into an order-of-events fact that can be pointed at in the room. Being pointed at does not stop a commitment. A commitment that has been pointed at at least knows it owes a verification line.

    Three activating criteria. The mechanism has started if any one of them appears.

    ① The conclusion has already been said outward: to customers, investors, superiors, the public, a regulator, in any form of public commitment. The operative word is outward. What you say to your own people can be taken back; what has gone out becomes an event when it is taken back.

    ② When new evidence arrives, the first question in the discussion is "how do we explain this", not "does it shake the premise". Filing replacing testing is the operational signature of a narrative that has taken over.

    ③ Opposing views are told to "bring more complete evidence", while the proposing side never cleared an equivalent bar when it published its conclusion. An asymmetric burden of proof is the institutional evidence that the narrative now holds status.

    13.2 How It Grows

    Three generating conditions and one amplifier.

    Condition one: a public commitment has already been made. The form does not matter, whether a roadshow, a project proposal, a press release, a statement to a regulator, or a launch date promised to customers. Once the commitment is out, the cost of retreat changes from "revise a conclusion" into "admit the earlier judgment was wrong", and the second is an order of magnitude higher (the mechanism of Chapter 27 plugs in here).

    Condition two: information overload. When the evidence stream is too large, the existing explanation is the only filter available. What reaches a project team in a day (data, rumour, competitor moves, internal complaints) far exceeds anyone's processing bandwidth, and fitting material into a frame that already exists is the cognitively cheap path. An overloaded environment turns the cheap path into the default one.

    Condition three: no independent analytical route. Nowhere in the organization is one person or one post authorised to set the mainstream explanation aside and analyse separately. When that lane is missing, every objection has to ride in the mainstream explanation's car, and the objection is disarmed at the level of wording.

    The amplifier is time pressure. The more an occasion demands an answer immediately, whether the first hours of an accident, a financing window or a policy launch, the more easily the narrative outruns the evidence.

    Two structural observations sit on top of the three conditions and the amplifier. The first is that commitment is a stock. Explanations said outward accumulate: said once at project approval, cited once in the interim report, repeated once in a press release, each repetition adding one to the stock. The larger the stock, the higher the unit cost of changing the story and the weaker the motive to test it. A successful test pays the organization; a failed test is charged to every person who signed along the decision chain. That is why narrative before evidence almost never corrects itself: its direction in time points at the continued inflation of committed stock.

    The second is that the audience sets the hardness of the narrative. A narrative told to internal colleagues can be corrected the same day. One told to investors has to survive until the next earnings season. One told to a regulator and the public makes the correction procedure itself an event. The same explanation will diverge into versions for different audiences, and the divergence becomes a risk of its own: the organization starts managing internal expectations with the external version, and the gap between the two becomes a cost that has to be covered with more narrative. The replacement of customer contract counts by "market potential" in case one below is the moment those two versions merged at the interface of the offering document.

    13.3 The Shape in Four Domains

    Case 1 (Finance): The AI Narrative and Four Thousand Investors

    Case ID: fi-002.

    When and where: offering period December 2019 to December 2024. On 26 August 2026 the SEC filed in the United States District Court for the Southern District of Florida, case number 1:26-cv-25837-RKA, complaint of 15 pages. The company was incorporated in Delaware in July 2018, headquartered in Miami during the offering period, and had its principal place of business in Sunnyvale, California, at the time of filing.

    What and who: GenesisAI Corp., running an AI services marketplace where model developers list and buyers pay a monthly subscription, with the platform taking 30% of transactions concluded directly between buyer and seller (complaint §16, p.4). Founder and then-CEO Archil Cheishvili, recorded in the complaint as 33 years old, CEO, executive and director from August 2018 to January 2025 (§11, p.3). Paragraph 15 of the complaint: every statement made or published by the company was created or approved by Cheishvili. Four offering channels: a first Wefunder round in December 2019; then three Netcapital rounds; a StartEngine round launched in September 2021 and cancelled by the platform; a Reg A offering through the broker Dalmore Group in 2022 (§23, p.7). The instruments were simple agreements for future equity (SAFEs) and stock, and the four crowdfunding rounds ran under provisions of the 2012 JOBS Act that open them to non-accredited investors (§21, p.6).

    What they were doing: in December 2019 the Wefunder offering page carried a chart titled "Revenue Forecast". The revenue line starts at zero in mid-2020 and rises to $250 million by 2024 (§27, p.8). That month Cheishvili put two numbers in an email to a prospective investor: $1 million in 2020 and $8 million in 2021 (§26, p.8). The email attached no customer contract and no letter of intent.

    In February 2020, answering a question in the comment section of the Wefunder page: "We estimate to reach revenue of around $25M in 3 years!" (§29, p.9). The comment section, like the offering page, could be read without registering. In April 2020 another email said the company had "lots of companies and users in the waitlist", with a target of $500,000 that year and $5 million the next (§29, p.9). Three carriers, three dates, three versions of one set of numbers: $25 million in three years, $500,000 that year, $5 million the next, $250 million by 2024. No conversion relates them to one another, and no document ever reconciled them.

    The state underneath, in the same period: the marketplace was still in beta, launched publicly in May 2022 with about 35 models (§17, p.5). No subscription fee was charged before launch, and there was no user agreement or commitment; the complaint's words are "there was no 'waitlist'" (§30, p.9). In October 2021 the company's own StartEngine page said: "[w]e have a beta version of the marketplace up and running…[with] 23 models deployed…" (§17, p.5). That sentence sits in the same year as two upward revisions of the valuation, in April and September 2021.

    The valuation moved once a year. The first round in 2019 implied $7.5 million; the April 2021 second round documents about $47 million; the September 2021 third round about $89 million; the Netcapital page in July 2022 $204 million, more than double the year before (§33, §34, p.10). The solicitation material carried a separate partnership list: slides posted on the website and the crowdfunding page in 2020 and 2021 read "Over 25 partnerships!", and what had actually been signed were non-binding memoranda of understanding, some of them expired by 2021 (§39, §40, p.12). The 2021 offering page also said "over 15 companies willing to join [its] marketplace", and the 2022 offering circular rewrote it as "over 15 agreements with AI companies and individuals who are interested in deploying AI tools" (§40, p.12). Two words for one fact, moving from "willing to join" to "agreements".

    On the promotion side, from 2020 to 2024 the company paid at least $780,000 in advertising to X, Google and Meta, plus one sidewalk lightbox (§20, p.6). In 2023 it built a bulk-email server tool and used it to reach new prospective investors (§19, p.5). In the second half of 2020 the company stopped publishing revenue forecasts (§30, p.9). The $250 million chart stayed up.

    The decision point: four of them.

    The first in December 2019, when the first-round offering page and slides were finalised. The carriers were the "Revenue Forecast" chart on the Wefunder page and the investor email sent that month (§26, §27). The signatory was Cheishvili, and paragraph 15 attributes the creation and approval of every company statement to him alone. On the desk: a marketplace in beta, no customer agreements of any kind, and $250 million as an extrapolated curve. The chart carried a disclaimer, "that this forecast is not guaranteed" (§28, p.9). The disclaimer sat under the chart, the chart stayed on the page, and neither changed for four years.

    The second in April 2020, the email that used the word waitlist. It went out after the first round had closed, its purpose was continued solicitation, the carrier was private email, and the recipients were individual prospective investors (§29, p.9). The same month had a second carrier in the platform's comment section, publicly readable and open to anyone with a screenshot function. What made this step irreversible was the recipient: once the email is sent, the company owes that particular person a particular number.

    The third in April and September 2021, two upward revisions of the valuation. The carriers were two sets of offering documents, and the number went from $47 million to $89 million (§33, p.10). Company revenue that year was zero (§31, p.9). The complaint records the method: the valuation was set by Cheishvili alone; when an investor asked for the basis he answered "comparables"; the comparables he chose had large customer bases, more mature products, or backing from well-known venture firms, and GenesisAI had none of the three (§37, p.12). Paragraph 5 of the complaint says the company had no robust comparable analysis and should have known, before making valuation statements to prospective investors, that a more objective analysis was needed.

    The fourth in July 2022, when the number became $204 million and the carrier moved from an offering document to a public Netcapital page (§34, p.10). Before that the figure had lived only in material behind a registration wall; afterwards it sat on a page anyone could screenshot. Over the same period, February to June 2022, Cheishvili sold 12,000 shares through Reg A for about $51,000 (§23, p.7). The peculiarity of the Reg A channel is that it lets a founder sell shares he already holds, not only new issuance by the company.

    What was visible then: the desk held a checklist that could verify itself. How long the marketplace had been in beta was the company's own development schedule. Whether customer agreements existed took one question to sales. Whether a waitlist existed took one lookup in the system that day. The February 2022 offering circular said "over 1,400 individual registered users and companies", and the complaint defines a registered user as someone who filled in a form with a name, a company and an email as an expression of potential interest, with no payment commitment required, in a system the company did not even track for active use (§36, p.11). The same metric was 1,000 in 2021, 2,000 in a 2022 YouTube solicitation video, and 2,600 in 2023 (§35, §36). Four numbers from four carriers, and the definition was never written down.

    The valuation line was on the desk too: $7.5 million in 2019, $204 million in 2022, with zero revenue in between (§31, §33).

    One more item. In September 2021 StartEngine cancelled the GenesisAI offering (§23, p.7). That was the only interception in the record made by an outside institution. After it, the company did not stop to check a single document.

    Information that only became available afterwards: actual revenue. Zero for 2018 through 2021; about $8,000 in 2022, most of it from an AI conference held in Miami; about $9,000 in 2023; about $40,000 in 2024, mainly from the email server tool rather than the AI marketplace (§31, pp. 9–10). And on 2 January 2025 the company announced it would stop developing the product (§7, p.3).

    Stop point:

    Stop here. Decide before you turn the page.

    1. What would you do? Write the action, not "pay more attention".
    2. Which item here are you most likely to miss, and why does it look unimportant?
    3. If you may set one checkpoint, when is it and what does it check?

    What happened: on 2 January 2025 the company announced it would stop developing the product, and Cheishvili stepped down as CEO and from his other roles (§7, p.3). On 26 August 2026, the day the SEC filed, the settlement terms were published: the defendants did not admit the allegations and consented to a final judgment subject to court approval, a permanent injunction against violating Section 17(a)(2) of the Securities Act of 1933, disgorgement by Cheishvili of $50,000 plus $9,184.53 in prejudgment interest, and a civil penalty of $50,000 (LR 26619). The complaint had sought a three-year officer and director bar (§9, Relief D); the settlement announcement does not list it.

    Back to the three questions. The most common answer to the first is "make the forecast conservative", and the forecast in this case was in fact changed once: in the second half of 2020 the company stopped publishing revenue forecasts, while the chart already published stayed on the offering page for four years. The most commonly missed item under the second question is the September 2021 StartEngine cancellation, the only interception from outside, after which every subsequent offering ran through the same channel structure and the company treated the event as a channel problem rather than a narrative problem. The third question lands in April 2020: had someone been required, before that email went out, to replace "waitlist" with a checkable number and produce the list on the spot, the sentence could not have been written. One threshold does the work, which is being able to produce the list. If the list cannot be produced, the adjective has to go.

    Options ruled out: six.

    1. Replace the revenue forecast curve with signed contract value. Not done. What stayed on the page was the disclaimer "not guaranteed" (§28), treated as proof that the uncertainty had been disclosed. A disclaimer protects the tone and leaves the curve alone.
    2. State publicly in 2020 that no waitlist existed. Not done. The April email that year still used the word, and no correction followed for two years (§29).
    3. Commission an independent review of the valuation method. Not done. The complaint records no robust comparable analysis, a valuation set by the CEO alone, and no written criteria for choosing the comparables (§5, §37).
    4. Disclose the definition of "registered user" to investors. Not done. The definition was written down for the first time in the complaint, having existed inside the company all along (§36).
    5. After the September 2021 channel cancellation, pause the offering and work on the product. Not done. The company kept offering on Netcapital and opened a Reg A channel as well (§23).
    6. Rewrite "partnership" as "memorandum of understanding". Not done. The 2022 offering circular upgraded "willing to join" into "agreements", moving the words against the facts (§40).

    Early signals: seven.

    ① December 2019: the forecast chart starts in mid-2020, and the company held no customer agreements that year (§26, §27). Reason it was pressed past: the note under the chart says forecasts are not guaranteed. ② April 2020: "waitlist" coexists with beta status (§29, §30). Pressed past because interest received during beta was treated as a list. ③ April 2021: the valuation jumps from $7.5 million to $47 million with revenue at zero in the same period (§31, §33). Pressed past because registered users were growing. ④ October 2021: the company's own offering page still says beta and 23 models, in a year when the valuation was raised twice (§17, §33). Pressed past because beta is a technical stage while valuation looks at the market. ⑤ February 2022: the offering circular presents "1,400 registered users" as demand-side evidence (§35). Pressed past because a registration count is a number that can be refreshed every month. ⑥ September 2021: StartEngine cancels the offering (§23). Pressed past by switching platform. ⑦ July 2022: the valuation doubles in a year to $204 million (§34). Pressed past with "comparables".

    Four of the seven are visible at the document layer, two need the version history of platform pages, and one needs a letter from an outside institution. The excuses share a shape: each swaps a checkable quantity for an interpretable one. Registered users is a number that can be refreshed; what registered users mean is an interpretation. A valuation is a signature; the basis of a valuation is an interpretation. A partnership is an intention; the nature of a partnership is an interpretation.

    The point of escalation: the step after the September 2021 channel cancellation. The company kept offering on Netcapital, wrote $204 million onto Netcapital's public page in July 2022, and ran a Reg A through Dalmore the same year (§23, §34). $204 million was not a new fact; it was the same valuation method restated on a new carrier. The carrier changed, and with it the cost of retreat: withdrawing a number from an offering document is an edit, and withdrawing a number from a public page is a retraction. In the same year the founder sold 12,000 shares through Reg A (§23), a sale inside the same offering channel and at the same time as the $204 million pricing. By then the valuation was no longer a hypothesis open to internal discussion. It was a price already sold to more than four thousand people, and every additional investor added one more listener to any retreat.

    The Reg A round had a second consequence in the shareholder register. The complaint describes the investor base as "predominantly retail investors" (§21, p.6), thousands of them scattered where they cannot check each other. A concentrated board demands an explanation once. Thousands of dispersed investors will not ask at the same time, and any one who does can be answered with the same paragraph. Part of why escalation works is that the audience is dispersed.

    Failure chain: technical layer. The marketplace only launched in May 2022, and the company's eventual revenue came from an email server tool built in 2023, about $40,000 in 2024 (§19, §31). There is no transition band between what the product could do and what the narrative said: the narrative described a marketplace and the revenue came from a bulk-mailing tool. Nothing failed in the technical layer; what failed was the exchange rate between the technical layer and the narrative.

    Organizational layer: every outward statement was created or approved by one person (§15). No second reviewer touched the valuation, the registered user had no definition, and the nature of the cooperation agreements had no legal standard distinguishing an MOU from a partnership. Paragraph 40 of the complaint notes that some of the companies that signed MOUs did put their tools on the platform, which gives the word "partnership" a handhold in fact without giving it one in contract. The handhold's use is that when the question comes, a real page can be pointed at.

    Incentive layer: Reg CF opened the rounds to non-accredited investors, four crowdfunding rounds plus one Reg A; from 2020 to 2024 at least $780,000 went into advertising, and the traffic it bought landed directly on offering pages (§20); each round's valuation sat above the last and supplied the reference for the next one's pricing. Cheap issuance, a dispersed audience and small tickets together slowed the feedback loop that asks whether anyone is challenging this.

    The break at the joint sits between the organizational and incentive layers: checking the phrase "registered user" costs an afternoon, and raising a valuation costs one signature. The first appears in nobody's appraisal. The second does.

    Minimum counterfactual: before the April 2020 email went out, a person who does not report to the CEO replaces "waitlist" in the outward text with two checkable numbers, beta registrations and paying customers, and requires the underlying list to be produced on the spot. One action, landing after the first round and before the second, with no luck required: the list is either in the system or it is not. If it is not, the sentence cannot be written that day, and the valuation narrative that follows loses one of its supports. The cost is one email and one query. The return is an adjective removed permanently from the outward text.

    Outcome and sources: announcement of 2 January 2025 that product development would stop; settlement of 26 August 2026, a permanent injunction plus $50,000 in disgorgement, $9,184.53 in interest and a $50,000 penalty, subject to court approval. Primary sources: SEC, Litigation Release No. 26619, 2026-08-26; SEC v. GenesisAI Corp. and Archil Cheishvili, No. 1:26-cv-25837-RKA (S.D. Fla.), Complaint (15 pages), link in Appendix B, fi-002.

    One detail in this case deserves to stop on the enforcement language. The SEC characterised the conduct as "negligently misrepresenting". The choice cuts both ways for a reader of this book. Read lightly, it means the regulator did not need to prove an intention to lie, only that the gap between statement and fact was reachable, so narrative optimism carries a price by itself. Read heavily, it means motivated reasoning is no excuse: a founder who believed his own narrative still met the legal consequence. Both readings arrive at the same lesson. The truth of a narrative lives inside the person; the liability of a narrative lives outside him; the distance between those two positions is the width at which this mechanism requires an organization to dig in beforehand.

    Case 2 (Public): The Replication Narrative and Its Precedent

    Case ID: po-006.

    When and where: in January 2021 the UK government and EDF opened formal negotiations on Sizewell C; construction started on site in April 2024; the final investment decision (FID) came in July 2025; financial close in November 2025. The audit report was published on 20 May 2026, signed by the Comptroller and Auditor General on 12 May and laid before the House of Commons on 18 May, numbered HC 33, session 2026-27. The site is at Sizewell in Suffolk, England; the decision-makers sat in Whitehall, London.

    What and who: the Department for Energy Security and Net Zero (DESNZ, including its predecessors the Department of Energy and Climate Change and the Department for Business, Energy and Industrial Strategy) as sponsor department; the project company Sizewell C Ltd (SZC Co.). Equity: DESNZ 44.9%, EDF 12.5%, La Caisse 20%, Centrica 15%, Amber Infrastructure 7.6%, with total equity capped at £8.5 billion in nominal terms; debt capped at £36.6 billion from the National Wealth Fund plus £5 billion of commercial loans (NAO p.5). The auditor is the National Audit Office, signed by the Comptroller and Auditor General Gareth Davies. The accountable bodies are the department that approved FID and the Treasury, not the construction site.

    What they were doing: the original text of the replication argument is at paragraph 1.11 of the NAO report. By the time SZC was approved, HPC had completed more than 99% of its detailed civil design and 85% of its detailed mechanical design; for comparison, when EDF took FID on HPC in 2016 it had only outline design and about 10% of detailed design, and the Finnish and French projects were no more mature at their investors' decisions (p.24). About 85% of the above-ground design could be copied straight from HPC. The reactor design already met UK nuclear safety regulatory requirements. Key components continued to come from inside the EDF group: Framatome reactors, Arabelle turbines (§1.12, p.26).

    The shape of the argument is worth seeing clearly. The NAO summarises the position of DESNZ and the company as follows: copying HPC's design and using the teams that built HPC reduces schedule and cost risk; SZC's cost and schedule estimates are far more mature than those of the HPC, French and Finnish sponsors at the time they decided; the company considers about 60% of its cost estimate to have "high or very high" certainty (§8, p.7). All three statements are true. "Risk is reduced" is derived from them, and sits outside them.

    Three cost and schedule lines, in 2024-25 prices: the company's project baseline £38.2 billion with all units in service by July 2039; the DESNZ low regulatory threshold £40.5 billion by October 2039; the high threshold £47.7 billion by August 2043, with government and investors committed to fund up to that cap (NAO p.5, §1.15, p.26). Three lines correspond to three probability ranges, and once written into the company's licence they become contractual parameters.

    Returns and bills: investor post-tax equity return of 10.8% to 13.0% in nominal terms, which the NAO judges above the level common in other utility sectors, including a "nuclear premium" (p.13, §18). On the consumer side, a typical household bill rises by £4 in 2025-26 and climbs to a peak of £19 to £21 a year in the first decade of operation, in 2024-25 prices; consumers began paying through their bills from November 2025 (p.5, Summary §3).

    Money in before the decision: from 2017 to FID, DESNZ injected £5 billion, taking its holding to 90%; external consultancy fees over the same period came close to £77 million (§1.8, p.21). In January 2022 it paid EDF a £100 million option fee (Figure 2 note 1, p.23). In November 2022 it injected £679 million to become a 50% shareholder and arranged CGN's exit (§1.8).

    The decision point: four of them.

    The first in January 2021, when formal negotiations opened with an initial target of financial close by the end of March 2023 (§1.7, p.21). The carriers were the negotiating mandate and the timetable. On the desk: the overruns and delays of the four precedent EPRs were all public, with Finland and France each more than ten years late at four times the original estimate (§1.10, p.24). Starting negotiations altered every option after it. Once a party is in exclusive negotiation, whether the project should be built leaves the table and whether the price can be closed takes its place.

    The second in 2022, when the regulated asset base (RAB) model was legislated (§1.6, p.19). This moved the location of the risk: construction-period costs would be carried by consumer bills rather than by the developer alone. The carrier was the Nuclear Energy (Financing) Act. Once the Act passed, the audience for the approval argument widened from investors to billpayers, and billpayers have no procedure for voting down an individual expenditure.

    The third in April 2024, when construction started on site, fifteen months before FID. The NAO records that continued injections during the negotiations "helped keep the start of construction on schedule for April 2024", preserving the overlap with HPC's schedule so that people, supply chain and equipment were not dispersed; the report's own words are that DESNZ "funded the project at risk accepting it could lose its investment" (§1.9, p.22). This is escalation in this chapter's sense, and the card takes it apart separately below.

    The fourth in July 2025, when DESNZ and the Treasury took FID together, 4.5 years after negotiations opened and at least 28 months later than planned (§1.7, p.21). That month DESNZ wrote the two regulatory thresholds into the company's licence (§1.15, p.26). Financial close came in November 2025, and in the same month £1.6 billion went to HPC for access to its cost, schedule and experience information (§1.13, p.26). The payment was designed to offset exactly the £3 per megawatt hour price reduction agreed in the 2016 HPC deal (§1.14, p.26). The name of the official who signed off FID is not stated in public records.

    The order of the four nodes is worth holding on to. Opening negotiations decided who to talk to. Legislation decided who carries construction risk. The start of construction decided the direction of the evidence. FID decided which numbers went into the contract. By FID the first three had narrowed the options to price terms, and the only question left on the approval table was where to set the three cost lines.

    What was visible then: Figure 3 in the NAO report is a table that fits on a negotiating table. Olkiluoto 3, construction started 2005, originally due in service 2009, actual 2023, 14 years late, cost from €2.3 billion to €9.6 billion in 2024 prices, an overrun of about 320%. Flamanville 3, started 2007, originally 2012, no earlier than 2026, 14 years late, cost from €3.2–3.3 billion to €13.2 billion in 2015 prices, an overrun of 300% to 310%. Taishan 1 and 2, five years late each. HPC unit 1, started 2018, originally in service 2025, revised to 2030–2031, five to seven years late, cost from £17.9 billion to £35–36 billion in 2015 prices, an overrun of about 100% (p.25; EDF annual report figures of February 2026). The same passage mentions Vogtle and VC Summer in the United States, which shows overruns are not confined to the EPR design (§1.10).

    The second item on the desk was the NAO's own June 2017 report on HPC (HC 40). Same audit institution, same subject, previous conclusion, and already public when negotiations opened in 2021.

    The third was the company's self-assessment of cost certainty. Figure 5 of the NAO report, dated August 2025, splits the £38.2 billion estimate into seven cost areas. The three marked "very high" certainty total 48%, and the least certain 15% is non-replicable site works (pp. 29–30). The strength of the outward argument came from the 85% that can be replicated, and the risk is concentrated in the remaining 15%.

    Information available only afterwards: SZC's own cost and schedule. The project expects to generate from 2038, the three cost lines correspond to in-service dates of July 2039, October 2039 and August 2043, and the actual figures cannot be checked until after those dates. The NAO's concluding sentence reads: "no nuclear power station of its type has so far been built without delays or cost increases." (Summary §21, p.15)

    Stop point:

    Stop here. Decide before you turn the page.

    1. What would you do? Write the action, not "pay more attention".
    2. Which item here are you most likely to miss, and why does it look unimportant?

    What happened: the project reached financial close in November 2025 and is proceeding as planned, with consumers paying through their bills from November 2025. The NAO did not overturn the project. It wrote the gap between the narrative and the precedents up as an audit finding, in wording that says the path "depends on some significant assumptions", that investor returns "look high", and that the extent to which investors are incentivised to control costs is "unclear" (§19, §22). The report makes two recommendations: any department considering a similar delivery model should be able to explain why so much risk has to be borne by taxpayers and consumers, and demonstrate that investor returns are commensurate with the risk actually borne; and DESNZ and the company should improve oversight of cost, schedule and private investors (Summary §23, §24, pp. 14–16).

    Back to the three questions. The most common answer to the first is "wait until the HPC data is more complete", and the NAO records the other side of that: the delay did give the company time to improve its estimates and its supply chain arrangements (§1.9). The reason holds, and it is a different operation from comparing precedent overruns item by item. The first improves an estimate. The second tests an argument. The most commonly missed item under the second question is one row of Figure 5: the least certain 15% is exactly the non-replicable site works, while the strength of the outward argument came from the 85% that replicates. The third question lands before the April 2024 start of construction, and the checkpoint is one table: take each precedent in Figure 3, break its overrun down by cause, and state item by item which arrangement SZC uses to avoid it.

    Options ruled out: five.

    1. Submit an item-by-item comparison of the causes of overrun at comparable projects before FID. Not done. The approval argument is built with similarities first and cost estimates second, and the overrun history is covered in one sentence in paragraph 7 of the report, that other nuclear stations have also suffered large overruns, and not treated as evidence requiring a response item by item (§7, §8).
    2. Prepare a credible alternative. Not done. The NAO's words are that DESNZ "did not develop a credible 'plan B' to its negotiations, despite being asked to do so by the major projects review group and the Infrastructure and Projects Authority recommending this" (§2.4, p.38).
    3. Bring the £1.6 billion HPC information access payment into the project baseline. Not done. The baseline explicitly excludes it, and it is one of the main differences between the £40.5 billion threshold and the £38.2 billion baseline (§1.15, p.26).
    4. Raise investors' risk exposure in exchange for a lower return. Not done. The NAO judges investors' cost-control incentive at no more than about 1.6 percentage points when costs rise, and writes that "whether these incentives are sufficient cannot be judged from the transaction structure alone" (§19, p.13).
    5. Push the start of consumer payment until after commissioning. Not done. Consumers have been paying since November 2025 (p.5).

    Early signals: six.

    ① January 2021, when negotiations opened: the overrun and delay record of the four precedent EPRs was already fully public (Figure 3). Pressed past because SZC replicates HPC and is not a first-of-a-kind build. ② June 2017: the NAO's HPC report had already identified problems in the way risk was transferred. Pressed past because the new financing model had replaced the old structure. ③ Late 2022: large nuclear projects under development had fallen from four in early 2017 to SZC alone (Figure 1, pp. 18–20). Pressed past because with one option left the negotiating leverage was gone, and the NAO's formulation is that taxpayers and consumers had to accept more risk (§10). ④ April 2024: construction starts fifteen months before FID, recorded by the NAO as "at risk". Pressed past to preserve the schedule overlap with HPC and avoid a break in skills and supply chain. ⑤ July 2025, at FID: the alternative required by the Major Projects Review Group and the Infrastructure and Projects Authority still did not exist. Pressed past because resetting the project would cause significant delay and delay would erode the benefits of replication, a causal chain the NAO records (§2.4). ⑥ Investor return of 10.8% to 13.0%, above the normal utility level. Pressed past because private capital had to be attracted.

    Three of the six come from the NAO's own earlier report and the front half of the same report. Signals were not in short supply. What was missing was a column in the approval table to write them into.

    The point of escalation: the April 2024 start of construction. At that point the object of the argument changes from a document to a site: fifteen months, £5 billion, a supply chain already scheduled, a reactor building with its base slab poured. Any later evidence against the replication narrative has to pass through the fact of construction already under way. The carrier of this approval was DESNZ's continuous injection of money during the negotiations, £679 million for equity in November 2022 and £5 billion cumulative by November 2025; the name of the signatory is not stated in public records. The NAO's assessment of this stretch runs both ways: the money kept the start date at April 2024 and preserved the overlap with HPC, and the same money went out with no guarantee that a deal would be struck.

    A second consequence of starting is that the direction of the evidence got locked. After the start, the project team had to prove that the station could be built to this schedule, and no longer whether it should be built at all. The two need different evidence. The first needs progress photographs, supply contracts and labour curves. The second needs a list of the causes of precedent overruns. The first batch generates itself every day. The second was never requested.

    Failure chain: technical layer. The construction history of the EPR points the same way at four sites (Figure 3), and HPC itself overran by about 100%. SZC's response was to take the replicable part of the design to completion. Figure 5 shows the most certain 48% made up of three items, spend already incurred, nuclear equipment under signed fixed-price contracts, and bulk materials replicable from HPC, while the least certain 15% is non-replicable site works. The technical break sits on the site, and the site is the piece that cannot be copied.

    Organizational layer: the approval argument is built around replication, and the overrun history was never required to answer item by item; the alternative was absent, though both opinions requiring one are written into the report; two regulatory thresholds went into the licence, fixing uncertainty as three numbers. Once in the licence, the thresholds are contractual parameters rather than assumptions awaiting discussion. The failure at this layer is one of argumentative form: a conclusion that could only be supported by similarity was written into a contract that can only be tested by those three numbers.

    Incentive layer: political commitment (energy security, jobs, net zero) and consumers already paying turn the cost of retreat from a fiscal question into a question of political credibility; an investor return of 10.8% to 13.0% coexists with a cost-control incentive of about 1.6 percentage points (§18, §19). High return and weak incentive together make cost control a secondary objective for investors, with getting the contract as the primary one.

    The break at the joint sits between the technical and organizational layers: precedent overrun data never entered the evidence column of the approval table, because it was filed under "first-of-a-kind problems" and SZC described itself as not first-of-a-kind. Once the classification is made, the same data moves from evidence to background.

    Minimum counterfactual: before the April 2024 start of construction, require the project side to submit a table that breaks down each precedent EPR in Figure 3 by cause of overrun and delay, states item by item which design, contract or organizational arrangement SZC uses to avoid it, and marks in red any item with no corresponding arrangement. One action, before the start, with no luck required: every cause of overrun is in public audit reports, and the person filling the table needs no new data, only existing data placed in a table that has to be completed. The value is in the columns rather than the answers. Columns that demand item-by-item correspondence force a correspondence to be written. Where none exists the row stays empty, and an empty row is easier to see than any argument.

    Outcome and sources: financial close in November 2025, project proceeding; consumers paying from November 2025; the NAO wrote the gap between the replication narrative and the precedents into an audit finding, with two recommendations on oversight and on the proportionality of returns. Primary source: National Audit Office, Sizewell C, HC 33, Session 2026-27, 20 May 2026 (signed by the Comptroller and Auditor General on 12 May 2026), link in Appendix B, po-006.

    This case is worth one dissection of the replication narrative as a form. The bookkeeping is asymmetric: similarity (same reactor type, reusable design) gets written up as the conclusion, and difference (site geology, supply-chain generation, contractor mix, regulatory version) stays in invisible default values. The NAO sentence about no such station having been built without delay or cost increase is sharp because it tests the other side of similarity. If replication really holds, the overruns should replicate too; the argument replicated the success assumptions and left the failure data out. That gives a general probe for any narrative of this kind. Ask the proponent which parameters of the precedent are being replicated, and which parts of the precedent's full data set, including the bad data, fall inside the similarity claim. Similarity that cannot be answered for is narrative similarity. Similarity that can be checked item by item is engineering similarity.

    The same dissection has a cross-generation sample in case one of Chapter 11 (mi-001). The shuttle era's "in-family" classification amounted to organizing twenty years of foam-shedding history into a narrative that supported flight: every shedding with no consequence was narrated as evidence of system reliability, and the opposite reading, that shedding had never been eliminated and only never cashed in, waited until after STS-107 to be written into an official conclusion. Same facts, two narratives, and the difference is only who holds the organization's attention and its power to classify. The lesson for a reader is that the ground here is the organization of facts rather than their quantity, and what an auditor can do is periodically re-check the alignment between the narrative and a raw list, an event-by-event table of the kind at CAIB p.124.

    Case 3 (Drugs and Devices): Into Distribution before Clearance (condensed)

    ph-005. Adept Medical's ear ventilation T-tube, 9mm. FDA device recall record Z-0966-2025, whose API record has been read for this book: no 510(k) clearance, 6,280 units recalled (2,500 in the United States, 3,780 outside it), distributed to Canada, Australia and New Zealand. The decision point is the internal node at which distribution began while clearance was still pending. Minimum counterfactual: before the first shipment abroad, the quality and regulatory team confirms the 510(k) status and records it in the release record. Primary source: FDA, Z-0966-2025, 2025, link in Appendix B, ph-005.

    Cases three and four read together as two process forms of assumption used in place of evidence. The assumption in case three is procedural: clearance in progress, shipping first, the distribution step running ahead of the regulatory status. The assumption in case four is technical: the safety of a raw-milk process rests on induction from batches that went well, until a state laboratory's finished-product test breaks it. In both, the assumption performs the function of evidence, and no line on the release sheet says that the basis here is an assumption. The action asked of the reader is cheap. In the release, shipping and go-live checklists you handle, find which line rests on an assumption, attach one measurement or one wait to it, and that line moves from the narrative list back to the evidence list.

    Case 4 (Agriculture): A Process Assumption Standing in for a Safety Conclusion (condensed)

    ag-003. Twin Sisters Creamery's Whatcom Blue raw-milk cheese. FDA enforcement report event 97880 / H-0193-2026, whose API record has been read for this book: a Class I recall of 3,438.2 pounds for potential E. coli O103:H2 contamination. Raw-milk cheese is not pasteurised, and its safety rests on a process assumption; the state agriculture department's finished-product testing broke the assumption.     Case 5 (Startups): The $103 Million Automation Narrative (full card)

    Case ID: st-003.

    When and where: founded 2014, wind-down announced June 2020. Headquartered in Austin, Texas, with an outsourced office in the Philippines. The exact date of the wind-down, the date of the board resolution, and the signing dates of the funding rounds are not stated on the Failory page.

    What and who: ScaleFactor, a bookkeeping and finance software company serving small and medium enterprises. One founder, Kurt Rathmann, also CEO; between 100 and 250 employees; 7 funding rounds totalling $103 million from 17 investors (Failory names Bessemer, Coatue, and Austin's Techstars, written "Tech Stars" in the original). This card is evidence grade D: a single media account of a party's own retrospective, with no primary documents, no round-by-round dates and no breakdown of amounts. Every node involving an internal meeting, resolution or signatory is marked "not stated in public records" and is not filled in by inference.

    What they were doing: in 2017 the company launched its first software product, built on top of QuickBooks and Xero, doing bookkeeping workflow orchestration. Failory's words are "built on top of QuickBooks and Xero". The front end was an automated dashboard covering five services: bookkeeping, tax filing, financial forecasting, bill payment and payroll. The delivery cadence promised outward was real-time reporting, set against the monthly close of a traditional bookkeeping firm. Pricing was about $6,000 a year, with the top tier at $30,000.

    Behind the interface sat another structure. The bookkeeping was done by accounting staff in the Austin office plus an outsourced team in the Philippines. Reports still went out monthly. Their job title was not accountant but "customer service officer". Their cost did not sit in cost of goods sold. Failory's description of this: "creative accounting on their own numbers to boost the perception of growth and to give themselves a more tech-like sales margin."

    What customers saw was the interface, and not who produced the reports behind it. Five services were packaged into one dashboard at $6,000 to $30,000 a year with "real time" at the centre of the sales pitch. At delivery the promise was downgraded to monthly, and monthly is the rhythm of a bookkeeping firm rather than a software product. That distance between promise and delivery showed up on the customer side as late reports, and inside the company as an unwritten definition that entered no document.

    The customer side left specific evidence. Accounts handed to customers contained errors they had to correct themselves. Lindsey Reinders's business lost $17,000 to an error that went unnoticed for months; when she asked for compensation she was offered a partial refund conditional on not making her customer experience public. Failory quotes her: "If you're one of the investors that gave these clowns $100 million...You should know they've flushed it down the toilet with poor product and poor service."

    Then 2020, and the pandemic. Failory's formulation is that the pandemic "allegedly halved" the company's $7 million in ARR. In June of that year the company announced it was winding down.

    The decision point: four of them.

    The first in 2017, when the first software product launched. The carriers were the product itself and the pricing page, the date is precise only to the year, and the page text no longer exists. On the desk: the product stood on two third-party accounting systems, delivery was still monthly, and people were in the loop. This step changed the company's outward identity from a bookkeeping service into software, and pricing, hiring and fundraising all followed software-company terms afterwards. The exact launch date and the person who approved it are not stated in public records.

    The second was calling the accounting staff customer service and moving that labour out of cost of goods sold. The carriers were the organization chart and the P&L line items, both internal and both printable. The consequence is measurable: gross margin looked better, and the share of labour vanished from the statements. The date of the change, the person who approved it, and whether a finance lead objected are not stated in public records.

    The third was the pivot to a matching platform. Failory gives one sentence as the reason, and the sentence is material for this chapter: "raised money like a tech startup, so they needed to develop a scalable tech product rather than to refine and grow their service business." Product form was decided by the terms of the fundraising, and the order is identifiable here: valuation logic first, product form second. The specific timing, the decision meeting and the approver are not stated in public records.

    The fourth came in June 2020, with the announcement of the wind-down. Failory records that the investors decided to stop funding and that the pandemic was the outward attribution; the CEO's quote is: "Business owners went into fight or flight mode. You don't necessarily need all the planning tools, high end gadgets. You just get back to the simple 'pen and paper.'" The resolution documents and the signatories are not stated in public records.

    Two of the four nodes have dates, one to the year and one to the month. That is the hard constraint of a D-grade source, and it sets how to read this card: the actions and their form can be checked, the moment and the signature cannot.

    What was visible then: four things on the desk, all inside the company's own systems, with no outside adviser required.

    1. The actual date the monthly reports went out. The gap against the "real time" promise is timestamped in the system.
    2. The labour hours each customer account consumed. They sit in the rota and the timesheets.
    3. The composition of errors and the rework. It sits in the tickets, the $17,000 one included.
    4. The absence of customer-service labour from the cost of goods sold line. The P&L shows it without any audit.

    A set of ratios could also have been computed on the spot, and the card does one division here, with every figure taken from numbers Failory has already published. Pre-pandemic ARR about $7 million; 100 to 250 employees; annual fee $6,000 to $30,000. Put the three together: ARR per head about $28,000 to $70,000; a customer count implied by the annual fee of about 233 to 1,167; and therefore about 1 to 12 customer accounts per employee. A product that genuinely automated bookkeeping would serve an order of magnitude more accounts per head. The division is a check that could have been run at the time rather than an accusation: divide two numbers from your own statements and see which business the answer looks like.

    Information available only afterwards: after the wind-down the company returned remaining capital to investors, in an amount not disclosed, and employees received 12 weeks of severance, with a small team retained to serve existing customers and help them migrate (Failory). Also the overall judgment of former customers and former employees, that the promised automation was "almost non-existent" and delivery depended on people.

    Stop point:

    Stop here. Decide before you turn the page.

    1. What would you do? Write the action, not "pay more attention".
    2. Which item here are you most likely to miss, and why does it look unimportant?
    3. If you may set one checkpoint, when is it and what does it check?

    What happened: the company wound down in June 2020, having raised $103 million, with pre-pandemic ARR of about $7 million. Remaining capital was returned to investors in an amount not disclosed, and 100 to 250 employees received 12 weeks of severance (Failory).

    Back to the three questions. The most common answer to the first is "take a down round and extend the runway", and that road was in fact walked here: first the pivot to a matching platform, then a platform that never produced traffic, then investors stopping funding. A down round solves cash and leaves the division standing. The most commonly missed item under the second question is the job title. Calling accountants customer service is the cheapest rewrite in this narrative and the hardest for an outsider to see, and it changed two tables at once, the people on the organization chart and the cost on the P&L. The third question lands in the first quarter after the 2017 launch: had someone measured the share of labour hours once at that point, the pricing basis for the following rounds would have been different. The checkpoint costs one week of counting.

    Options ruled out: six.

    1. Treat the service business as a service business, refine it and grow it. Failory records this path as rejected, with the reason given in the fundraising terms: money raised as a tech company demanded a scalable tech product.
    2. Disclose to investors that the "customer service" staff behind the curtain were accountants. This was tested once, and Failory writes that "some investors were turned away after revealing the 'customer service staff' behind the curtains"; once the price of disclosure had been seen, the remaining 17 invested.
    3. Compensate the customer affected by the error in full and handle it openly. Not done. What was offered was a partial refund conditional on confidentiality.
    4. Price by account or by hour, and disclose the gross margin structure. Not stated in public records whether this was discussed.
    5. Make the service business's unit economics positive before pivoting to a platform. Not stated in public records whether this was discussed. The checkable fact is that the pivot happened while the service business had not produced a profitable structure.
    6. Cut the annual fee and narrow the customer base into a small, stable bookkeeping firm. Not stated in public records whether this was discussed. The checkable fact is pricing from $6,000 up to $30,000, a band that corresponds to full-service outsourcing for an SME rather than to self-serve software.

    Of the six, the first two have source support, and the last four have only the fact side without the decision side. That line is the boundary of a D-grade source: it can tell you what the company did, and rarely what it considered.

    Early signals: six.

    ① 2017: the product was built on QuickBooks and Xero, orchestrated the workflow, and replaced very little. Pressed past to get the workflow running on existing systems first. ② Reports went out monthly, against the "real time" promise. Pressed past for a reason not stated in public records. ③ The accounting staff were titled customer service officer on the organization chart. Pressed past because what is presented outward is a software company. ④ Customer-service labour was left out of cost of goods sold. Pressed past to bring gross margin close to a software company's. ⑤ A $17,000 customer loss, plus a refund conditional on confidentiality. Pressed past by handling it case by case. ⑥ A team of 100 to 250 against $7 million in ARR. Pressed past with the observation that SaaS companies are all labour-heavy early on.

    The first five of the six are visible at the document layer, and the sixth needs one division. Of the two hardest signals, one was renamed and one was reclassified. Neither requires a lie, only a different option chosen while filling in a form. A signal does not predict the outcome; it asks the people in the room to answer one question while they are still there, which is how this number was calculated.

    The point of escalation: the pivot to a matching platform. This restated a labour-intensive service as a platform and kept fundraising priced at tech-company multiples. 7 rounds and 17 investors, each one endorsing the classification that this was software. By the time the platform also failed to produce traffic, the company had no service business to retreat to, because customer relationships, pricing and staffing had all been rewritten in the previous step. Failory's judgment of this stretch is that the company "needed to develop a scalable tech product rather than to refine and grow their service business", a sentence that served as the reason at the time and describes the mechanism of escalation. The specific date and the approver are not stated in public records.

    This step also changed what was measured. As a service business the company could count accounts, hours per account and retention; as a platform it could count registered sellers, transaction volume and retention. Once the metrics changed, the earlier set stopped appearing in the monthly material. No number was falsified. The company changed which numbers it reported, and at the moment of the change the quantities that could have falsified the original narrative left the field of view. This is the same machine as the presentation-layer move of Chapter 12.

    Failure chain: technical layer. The automation was not complete enough to support a claim of no humans. Failory records the software as "very glitchy and mistake-prone", which added to the accountants' workload; delivery still depended on people and the cadence was still monthly. The technical problem sits in the role the software played: it orchestrated the workflow in this business, and the share of the work it replaced was small.

    Organizational layer: two definitions were rewritten at once, the job roster and the cost of goods sold line, and the result is that the share of labour, the number best able to falsify the narrative, appears in neither the internal statements nor the outward material. The company had no measurement post independent of the growth narrative, and no second person put a written opinion on record about either definition (not stated in public records whether anyone raised one). The failure here is the removal of evidence from the statements rather than its concealment: the line items remained and the people remained, and they stopped sitting next to each other.

    Incentive layer: tech-company multiples reward the classification "scalable", and a service company's gross margin structure depresses a valuation. The price of disclosure was measured once, when an investor left because of it, and non-disclosure became the default afterwards. 7 rounds of fundraising hardened that default into process.

    The break at the joint sits between the organizational and incentive layers: rewriting a definition costs nothing, funding arrives quickly, and the truth has to wait until customer complaints accumulate enough to be visible from outside. By the time it was visible, the company was already a platform.

    Minimum counterfactual: in the first full quarter after the 2017 launch, draw a random sample of customer accounts, and have someone who does not report to the sales line measure the share of labour hours from receipt of documents to issued report, then write it into that quarter's investor update. One action, between two funding rounds, with no luck required: the hours are in the rota and the documents are in the system. Once the number sits in an investor update it becomes a monthly comparable series, and once the series exists every later claim about automation has to align with it.

    The action lands in the investor update for a reason that concerns readers. The readers of a weekly report rise and fall with the narrative. The readers of an investor update set the price of the next round. The same number read by the first group is read as normal for an early stage; read by the second it enters the valuation discussion. Where the checkpoint sits decides whether it does anything, more than what it checks.

    Outcome and sources: wind-down in June 2020; $103 million raised; pre-pandemic ARR of about $7 million; remaining capital returned to investors in an amount not disclosed; 12 weeks of severance. Source: Failory Cemetery, "Why Did ScaleFactor Fail?", link in Appendix B, st-003. Evidence grade D: a single media account of a party's own retrospective, with no primary documents and no round-by-round dates; every internal decision node on this card is marked "not stated in public records"; ARR per head and accounts per employee are divisions this card performed on disclosed numbers, and are labelled as such in the text.

    Case 6 (Finance): A Final Judgment on Narrative Detached from Assets (condensed)

    fi-005. American Patriot Brands and two of its executives. SEC litigation (Litigation Release No. 26587, whose text has been read for this book): on 10 July 2026 the Central District of California entered summary judgment finding liability for securities offering fraud; joint and several disgorgement of $17,786,703 plus $6,202,777 in interest, a company penalty of $4,729,004, $6,399,792 in total against CEO Robert Y. Lee, and officer and director bars against two executives. Unlike the settlement in case one, this is a litigated final judgment: the court made a court-level finding on the disconnect between the offering narrative and the underlying assets. The angle for this chapter is that the scale of the narrative, a grand picture of agricultural assets, was disconnected from the real state of those assets, and the disconnect left checkable space in the offering documents. Minimum counterfactual: reconcile the description of asset status in the offering documents against the underlying contracts, item by item. Primary source: SEC, LR-26587, 2026, link in Appendix B, fi-005.

    Six cases side by side complete the exit list for narrative before evidence: crowdfunding offering documents (fi-002), an approval argument (po-006), a device distribution release (ph-005), a process assumption (ag-003), a funding-round narrative (st-003), and an asset narrative as found by a court (fi-005). What the six exits share is that once a commitment is complete, evidence is demoted from the basis of a decision to material for its defence. Every organization has a narrative, so the reader's stocktake is a hunt for commitment exits: list every channel through which your organization makes an outward commitment, attach a grade gate to each, and this chapter's defences have somewhere to stand.

    13.4 What Shows Up Earliest

    Eight weak signals, each with the reason it is usually pressed past.

    1. The conclusion is published before a verification plan exists: the launch date comes first and the experiment schedule second. Pressed past with "the market window will not wait".
    2. "We have already confirmed that" starts appearing in the minutes, and the carrier of the confirmation cannot be found. Reason: "it was said out loud."
    3. When new data arrives the first question is "how do we explain it", and nobody asks whether it shakes the premise. Reason: "keep the discussion efficient."
    4. The person questioning is asked for ten times the evidence, and the person supporting only has to restate the position. Reason: "don't re-litigate."
    5. The wording of diligence, test and pilot reports begins aligning with the established conclusion: one fact gets characterised more and more consistently across reports. Reason: "the team has reached consensus."
    6. "The person playing devil's advocate doesn't understand the business" becomes the standard classification in the room, and the person classifying never tests his own business judgment. Reason: "division of expertise."
    7. Appendices thicken while the body thins: evidence is moved out of sight rather than into the argument. Reason: "leadership only reads the conclusion."
    8. The original wording of an earlier version's conclusion is edited silently, with no revision record. Reason: "improving the phrasing." (Compare the register of definition changes in Chapter 38: revisions to a narrative need a trail as well.)

    Put the eight back against this chapter's cases. Signal one, publication before a verification plan, is fi-002: when the offering documents were finalised, the revenue expectation had no verification arrangement attached. Signal three, new data explained and never tested, is po-006: the overrun history of comparable projects reached the approval table as a footnote and entered the discussion as "we adopted a new model". Signal four, an asymmetric burden, takes this form in po-006: people questioning the replication strategy were asked first to prove that replication did not hold, while the strategy itself was never asked to prove anything. Signal five, report wording drifting toward the conclusion, is st-003: the growth narrative pulled the characterisation of ticket composition and retention data toward "market education in progress". Signal eight, silent edits, is most common in fundraising, where the forecast numbers in one round's documents get rewritten in the next round's with no revision record.

    Re-sorted by cost of discovery: signals two, five, seven and eight are findable at the document layer; one and three need meeting observation; four and six need a record of symmetry across meetings. Half can be caught at the document layer, which is why Device 1 builds grade marking into the document template. Make the defence that depends most on documents the most mechanical action, because a mechanical action does not depend on the actor's alertness.

    The eight share a blind spot worth writing down. All of them watch externally visible acts, whether publication, minutes, reports or classification, while the earliest work of narrative before evidence happens inside cognition and leaves no external trace. By the time any of the eight appears, the mechanism has reached the commitment stage. The only way to move the detection window earlier is to watch the moment in criterion ① of section 13.1, when the explanation is first said outward. The only position in an organization that can watch that moment is process: put a verification plan field into the template for outward-commitment documents, so that a blank field blocks signature. A template is more reliable than vigilance, for the same reason Device 1 is mechanical.

    13.5 How to Defend Against It

    Device 1: graded release of conclusions. Split outward expression into three grades, hypothesis (marked explicitly as unverified), working conclusion (with an evidence list and disconfirming conditions), and finding (with an independent review record). Only the third may support a commitment. Implementation: the three labels go into the header of the document template rather than into the author's conscientiousness. Signature: a finding-grade document needs an independent reviewer, and the reviewer may not be the person who first proposed the conclusion.

    Device 2: appoint a disproof owner. Every major conclusion gets one person whose job description includes overturning it, with budget, data access and a no-blame guarantee. The comparison question from the GenesisAI case is this: who was authorised to check the gap between the company's actual revenue and the revenue the narrative implied? The disproof owner's annual review is not "I checked and found nothing wrong". It is a report of the disproofs found during the year and which of them were adopted.

    Device 3: a symmetric burden rule. Write the meeting rule in advance: proponents and challengers face the same evidentiary threshold. The chair enforces it, and violations go into the minutes. The effect is that every triggering of criterion ③ leaves a trace.

    Device 4: the time-gap clause. Early accident attribution, finalised offering documents and project commitments all wait overnight. The clause is procedural in form: the sign button on an outward-commitment document must be more than twelve hours from the last edit, enforced by the system.

    Each of the four has one direct use on this chapter's cases. Device 1 on fi-002: a crowdfunding offering document is an outward commitment and may only be supported by finding-grade material, and finding grade requires a reviewer's signature from someone other than the original proponent, which closes the path where a founder believes his own narrative. Device 2 on st-003: the disproof owner's annual review topic is the real composition of the automation completion rate, with data access and a no-blame guarantee; had that post reported once, about a year before the wind-down, the share of tickets would have reached the board pack. Device 3 on po-006: the approval hearing rules are written in advance so that the side arguing for replication and the side questioning it face the same threshold, with the precedent overrun comparison table submitted by the proposing side. Device 4 applies to the shared setting of cases one and five: the sign button on offering documents and commitment documents is forced overnight. The value of that mechanical action is the overnight window it gives motivated reasoning. No new evidence arrives by the next morning, and the only thing that has changed is the distance between the signer and the narrative.

    The failure modes of the four devices need writing down as well. Graded release dies in the formalisation of the labels: the header says hypothesis while the prose runs in finding-grade tone, and readers are more sensitive to tone than to labels. The disproof owner dies of an orphaned post: budget without data access, a review without an audience, the annual counter-argument written and archived. Symmetric burden dies when the chair stops enforcing: the rule is in the meeting policy, the chair breaks it once with no consequence, and from then on the rule is symmetric only on paper. The time-gap clause dies in signature outside the system: the button in the workflow waits overnight while the real commitment goes out immediately in the work chat. All four failure modes share one feature, in that the act of bypassing the device is small, local and unrecorded. The countermeasure is one meta-rule rather than more devices: every exception to a device is registered, and the exception list is reviewed quarterly.

    The meta-rule has its own way of dying. The exception list becomes an exception budget: when "three exceptions this quarter" gets read as "nine left in this quarter's budget", registration turns from a defence into a quota. The fix is to make sure the list is read by someone with no stake in the commitment, and to compile it per commitment rather than per organization. The number of exceptions under one conclusion grade carries more information than the organization's total. The first points at a narrative that is currently out of control. The second only shows that the organization is busy.

    The joints to other chapters can be gathered here. Section 13.1 describes how commitment rewrites the standing of evidence; Chapter 11 describes how the absence of consequence rewrites the standing of a deviation; Chapter 12 describes how a metric rewrites the standing of a goal. All three run on one engine: organizations need certainty, and certainty can be manufactured. Manufactured certainty is cheap, immediate and allocatable, and its bill arrives at the tail. The one action the book gives the reader is this: whenever certainty arrives too easily, find out where its bill is being sent.

    One cross-chapter addition beyond the four devices: give every narrative a control metric. For each key quantitative claim in an outward commitment, whether a revenue expectation, a schedule commitment, a cost commitment or a capacity commitment, register at the time of setting an independently measurable control quantity and the party responsible for measuring it, and put the commitment's due date and the control measurement date on the same line. This moves Chapter 12's pairing discipline onto the narrative: on the day the narrative comes due, the control number appears on the same table without being asked for. The register is an early-warning instrument in itself. A commitment whose control quantity has never been measured is automatically downgraded to unverified hypothesis, on the symmetry principle of criterion ③.

    13.6 Boundaries with Neighbouring Mechanisms

    Against normalization of deviance (Chapter 11): normalization works at the archival layer, where a deviation gets filed as acceptable, and this chapter works at the expressive layer, where a conclusion gets published early. A case can show both, with the deviation first archived internally as acceptable and then described outward as safe. Both are present in Sizewell's replication narrative: precedent overruns were archived as manageable, and the replication hypothesis was published as low risk.

    Against sunk cost and escalation (Chapter 27): escalation explains input, meaning why the organization continues, and this chapter explains expectation, meaning why it is optimistic. Narrative first is often the preparatory step for escalation, because once the story is out, exit becomes broken faith. Division of criteria: if the review table shows an alignment problem in benefit estimates, go to Chapter 27; if it shows a problem in the order in which conclusions were published, stay here.

    Against the slack of success (Chapter 20): slack is the shrinkage of defensive investment in a period without accidents, and this chapter is the inflation of belief in a period without counter-evidence. They share a time condition, which is the absence of bad news, and a remedy, which is one independent sceptical post with a supply line attached.

    13.7 Five Self-Checks

    1. The last outward commitment you made about a project's expectations: is the verification plan finished? If it is not, which of the three grades does the commitment belong to?
    2. Who on your team is authorised to overturn the mainstream conclusion? When did that person last use the authority, and who paid the cost of using it?
    3. The last time you questioned a superior's conclusion, what evidentiary threshold were you held to? Did the proposing side pass the same threshold at the time?
    4. The judgment you are most confident of right now: can you write it in the form "this evidence would make me abandon it"? If you cannot, what does this chapter call it?
    5. Look back at the first communiqué after the most recent accident in your industry: how long passed between the attribution and the investigation report, how many times did the narrative change in between, and where is the record of the changes?
