    Chapter 37 Design Review Question List

    What this chapter covers: 130 review questions in groups, a general set plus the twenty-four mechanisms, each one carrying a note on what it means when you cannot answer. Use it with the locator table in Chapter 35: pick the groups your situation hits, then take the questions one at a time. When to come back to it: design reviews, decision meetings, due-diligence interviews, and any time you are setting an exam for a team below you. Reading time: about 50 minutes.

    The question list is a toolbox. It comes off the shelf when needed and goes back, and nobody reads it through in one sitting. There are two occasions for taking it out. The first is a design review: ten minutes before the meeting, the questions for the mechanisms in play are copied into the agenda, and the review changes from a presentation with an audience into a pass down a list. The second is a self-check, one person walking their own system through the questions one at a time. The output of a self-check is the list of questions that could not be answered, and that list is the finding. A question nobody in the design ever considered is itself a gap.

    Every question is worded for an answer out of knowledge: can you answer this, as distinct from have you done this. The first form points at knowledge, the second at process. Process on file with knowledge missing is the most common failure shape in this book, and it is practical drift (Chapter 15) seen through a review question.

    The list has a second use, in training. Hand a newcomer the questions and send them to interview the veterans. The newcomer learns the structure and logic of the system; the veterans get asked things they never considered. Both sides gain. The barrier is as low as a barrier gets, because nothing has to be taught to anybody. Hand over the list. The list teaches by itself.

    Three rules, and they fit on three lines. Rule one: questions are used in groups. Take the mechanism groups the locator table hits, add the general group, and stop at twenty to thirty questions for one review; reading the whole table is an editing act, not a review act. Rule two: the arrow after each question gives the meaning of a failure to answer, and that meaning goes into the minutes, where it does more work than a follow-up question. Rule three: answers must land on dated evidence, and a verbal assurance counts as no answer.

    Where the library came from is worth stating once. Not one of the 130 questions came out of theoretical reasoning. All of them were traced back from the decision-point field of this book's case cards: what material lay on the table at that decision point, and which question nobody asked out loud. That unasked question was then rewritten into a form you can put to yourself today. On the Alaska 261 card, the question lying next to the decision point was "whose inspection record is the extension based on" (the information source was the operator alone), and it became the prototype for question 3 of the Chapter 11 group. On the Travel + Leisure card it was "who independently reviewed the change in the counting rule", which became question 7 of the general group. On the Grafton card it was "once the alarm sounds, who has to stop what they are doing", which is question 2 of the Chapter 16 group. This provenance tells you what kind of document you are holding: an index to the decision points of a case collection, with a site behind every question that paid real tuition. It also explains the wording. Almost every organization in those cases had processes and had documents. What they lacked was somebody at the decision point asking the question and demanding an answer.

    The relation to the trigger criteria needs drawing as well. A mechanism chapter's criteria answer whether the mechanism is running. A question answers whether your organization holds any knowledge about that mechanism. A criterion hit requires evidence. A question answered with "I do not know" requires only the admission. In a self-check the two tools divide the work: questions screen for black holes in knowledge, criteria confirm whether the mechanism is actually operating. Questions first, criteria second, because screening a black hole costs far less than collecting evidence, and the cheaper instrument belongs in front.

    The General Ten (run this group first in any review)

    1. At which second does this action become irreversible? → No answer means the decision-maker has not yet met the irreversible point (Chapter 7).
    2. Who holds the last second-confirmation slot before that point, and what does that person check? → No answer means the defence is nominal.
    3. Which of the items the decision rests on carry a timestamp? → None of them means the decision stands on retelling (Chapter 6).
    4. If every dollar committed were written off tomorrow, who loses most? → No answer means nobody has costed the exit (Chapter 27).
    5. Who holds the unilateral right to stop this, and in which document is it written? → No answer means the right does not exist (Chapter 18).
    6. Was the last anomaly of this class attributed in the register? → No answer means the anomaly was explained away (Chapter 8, rung one).
    7. Who independently reviewed the last change in the counting rule? → No answer means the ruler is being swapped (Chapter 12; Chapter 8, rung five).
    8. When was the critical dependency list (third parties, suppliers, single systems) last updated? → No list means the dependencies are invisible (Chapters 21 and 33).
    9. What happened, professionally, to the last person who stopped a project? → No answer means stopping is a career risk (Chapters 19 and 27).
    10. Where does the person who pays for today's decision in three years sit now? → No answer means horizon mismatch is running (Chapter 28).

    Normalization of Deviance (Chapter 11)

    1. How many times has a deviation of this class occurred, and on what basis was each occurrence judged to have no consequence? → No register means normalization is already running.
    2. In "there is still enough margin", is the margin a number or an adjective? → An adjective means the standard is softening.
    3. When was the inspection standard last relaxed, and who approved it? → No answer means the relaxation was done in the dark.
    4. How was the last question a newcomer raised about the old practice handled? → "It has always been done this way" means criterion ③ is hit.
    5. Which release action can be completed without writing down why it is acceptable this time? → If one exists, release leaves no trace.

    Goal Displacement; Goodhart's Law (Chapter 12)

    1. How is this metric being optimized right now? Name three actual workarounds. → Cannot name three means you have not been on the floor.
    2. How long is the footnote to the metric's definition, and who has the authority to interpret it? → A swelling footnote means the definition is drifting.
    3. What happened afterwards to the last person who said the metric was distorted? → If they paid a price, criterion ③ is hit.
    4. Which observation channel that cannot be scored is still running, and who reads it? → None means the organization has only the ruler left.
    5. Has anyone written the list of differences between this metric and the goal it stands for? → Never written means the substitution runs unmonitored.

    Narrative before Evidence (Chapter 13)

    1. How many independent explanatory paths did the current conclusion pass through before it was fixed? → One means there is no competing hypothesis.
    2. Whose job description includes "responsible for overturning this"? → Nobody means refutation has no post.
    3. Is the hypothesis written in a form that can be overturned, with the evidence that would make you drop it named? → Cannot write that means it cannot be falsified.
    4. How much evidence must an objection produce before it counts? → A higher bar than the proponent faces means the burden is asymmetric.
    5. When did the early conclusion first enter a formal document? → Before the data was complete means the narrative closed its books early.

    Where Intuitive Expertise Breaks Down (Chapter 14)

    1. Does the experience this judgment rests on still hold in the current environment of rules, technology and people? → No mandatory check means running exposed.
    2. How often do the rules of this environment change? → Fast change plus a practice built on experience puts you in the failure zone.
    3. How long is the feedback loop on this class of decision, and who can afford to wait for it? → Longer than six months means no learning loop.
    4. When did someone last answer data with "I have done this for twenty years"? → If it happened, criterion ① is hit.
    5. In a new field, is the first move analogy or measurement? → Analogy means intuition is out of bounds.

    Practical Drift (Chapter 15)

    1. When was the diff between the manual and actual practice last run? → Never means drift is invisible.
    2. Are newcomers trained from the manual or from a senior hand? → The senior hand means drift is passing down the generations.
    3. Which rule can nobody in fact follow? → No answer means nobody dares admit it.
    4. What is the immediate consequence of breaking that procedure? → None means drift has a place to grow.
    5. Who approves the informed violation, and under what name? → Oral custom means a violation with no owner.

    Information Attenuation up the Hierarchy (Chapter 16)

    1. Where do the front-line version and the decision layer's version differ, item by item? → No answer means attenuation has passed the midpoint.
    2. What channel carries information around the hierarchy, and who is responsible for reading it? → No channel means you hear retellings only.
    3. Is the ratio of adjectives to numbers in reporting moving which way? → More adjectives means packaging in progress.
    4. How many layers does bad news cross from the front line to the decision, and what does each layer delete? → If you cannot say what is deleted, every layer deletes.
    5. What happened to the last person who reported over their manager's head? → If they were dealt with, the channel is dead.

    Misaligned Incentives (Chapter 17)

    1. Have the performance schemes of each layer been read side by side? Which clause is the angle between them? → Never read side by side means the misalignment is invisible.
    2. Whose account carries the externality of this layer's decision? → Nobody's means the cost is discharged for free.
    3. Who is responsible for the overall result, and does that person hold real authority or only the blame seat? → No authority means decoration.
    4. Between the decision-maker's tenure and the cycle of the consequence, which is longer? → The shorter tenure means rational neglect.
    5. How dense are "per the contract" and "per the rules" in the minutes? → High density means behaviour is taking cover in the contract.

    Diffusion of Responsibility (Chapter 18)

    1. In whose contract or job description does the stop authority sit, and at which clause? → No clause means stopping depends on courage.
    2. How many people are on the signature chain, and what exactly does each one sign? → If you cannot say what each one signs, the signatures are idling.
    3. What is the organization's first question after an accident? → "Who signed" in place of "where did it break" confirms a culture of diffusion.
    4. How many layers of subcontracting, and who holds inspection rights at each layer? → If you cannot count the layers, it is a black box.
    5. For a resolution the committee passed, who executes, who supervises, who closes it out? → All three in one seat means nobody owns the resolution.

    Why the Truth-Teller Goes First (Chapter 19)

    1. What is the three-year usage record of the internal reporting or dissent channel? → Zero use means the problems are present and nobody says so.
    2. Where is the person who last raised a sharp question now? → Gone or marginalized means criterion ② is hit.
    3. Who signs the performance review unilaterally? → The direct manager alone means the alarm is wired to the manager's mood.
    4. Which questions have become bound to which leader's reputation? → Cannot list them means the binding runs deep.
    5. How many of the organization's alarms (independent oversight, rotating audit, external assessment) still sound? → All silent means the only alarm left is the accident.

    The Slack of Success (Chapter 20)

    1. Which inputs have shown no visible output value for a long time? Is there a list? → No list means they are being read as surplus.
    2. Has "this has never caused a problem" been used as grounds for a cut? → If it has, criterion ② is hit.
    3. After the last cut to defensive spending, how long until an accident appeared? → Within two years means criterion ③ is hit.
    4. In the finance ledger, does "nothing happened" count as output or as no output? → No output means it will be cut inside the budget cycle.
    5. Who was last rewarded for "nothing happened"? → Nobody means this work runs on self-amusement.

    Hidden Coupling (Chapter 21)

    1. When was the real dependency graph last drawn, by whom, and has anyone checked it? → No graph means the coupling is invisible.
    2. Which systems share something "unimportant" (power, accounts, database tables, domains, people)? → Cannot list three means the sharing has never been examined.
    3. Where is the post-mortem of the last cause-unknown outage filed? → Not filed means the clues are evaporating.
    4. Can "who does this outage affect" be answered automatically? → If not, the blast radius is guessed.
    5. Who holds the list of acquisitions, outsourced pieces and legacy connections? → No owner marks the deepest coupling.

    The Illusion of Redundancy (Chapter 22)

    1. What is the list of items the backup shares with the primary system (same vendor, same drawings, same crew)? → No list means common origin has never been faced.
    2. When was failover last rehearsed at peak load? → Never means the rehearsal is a performance.
    3. Has the redundancy been verified on its own, by turning the primary off? → Afraid to turn it off means the redundancy is unproven.
    4. Has anyone walked through the scenario where both systems fail at once? → Never considered means common-cause failure runs exposed.
    5. Does the integrator's contract carry a redundancy liability clause? → Not written means you bought the redundancy on the drawing.

    Extrapolating Past the Validated Domain (Chapter 23)

    1. Which page of the scale-up plan holds the section "what changed from the pilot"? → No such section means the extrapolation was never declared.
    2. Does the difference list say, item by item, why each difference does not affect the conclusion? → Hand-waved means the differences were never digested.
    3. Has "we validated this at A" been used as grounds for skipping checks? → If so, criterion ③ is hit.
    4. Who listed the differences in the new environment, and when? → Nobody did means differences are found by accident.
    5. Is the valid boundary of the extrapolation written into the final conclusion? → Not written means the next extrapolation follows.

    Crossing a Scale Threshold (Chapter 24)

    1. Where is the re-validation record for the core assumptions at the last order-of-magnitude jump? → No record means the assumptions run exposed.
    2. Is coordination cost measured, in meetings, management layers and interface documents? → Unmeasured means the non-linearity is invisible.
    3. Which practices had already changed character at twice the scale? → No answer means the change is unknowable.
    4. Why was the extra management layer added? → For communication means a threshold signal.
    5. Is there a record of veterans saying "it was not like this before"? → No record means the organization is losing its control group.

    Margin Erosion (Chapter 25)

    1. Where is the design margin register, and who maintains it? → No register means the cuts cannot accumulate.
    2. Has anyone read the list of past cuts as a whole? → Per-event reading only means nobody holds the total.
    3. What is the quantified basis for "still enough"? → No number means criterion ③ is hit.
    4. Who would stop the fifth cut? → Nobody means a straight slide.
    5. Can the original design value of the margin still be found? → If it cannot, the reference point is gone.

    Automation and Skill Decay (Chapter 26)

    1. What is the mandatory frequency of manual operation, and when was it last recorded? → No frequency means the skill is decaying on its own.
    2. What share of training is takeover scenarios? → A low share with high automation means the takeover lands at the hardest moment.
    3. When did "the system handles it" become a reason not to practise? → If it has, criterion ③ is hit.
    4. The manual assumes actions people know; how do you verify they still know them? → No verification means the assumption is expiring.
    5. Is the threshold for leaving automatic mode written down, and rehearsed? → Neither means the takeover is improvised.

    Sunk Cost and Escalation (Chapter 27)

    1. Which section of the project charter holds the exit condition, and who adjudicates it? → Not written means the only exit is a coup.
    2. When did "we have already put in this much" last appear as a main argument, and in which meeting? → Frequent appearance means escalation is running.
    3. Who is the external adjudicator, and do they have a stake in the project? → None means you judge yourself.
    4. Who last adjusted the re-forecast assumptions in the optimistic direction? → One-sided adjustment means the model serves the conclusion.
    5. What happened, career-wise, to the last person who argued for exit? → Moved aside means exit has a price.

    Horizon Mismatch (Chapter 28)

    1. Does the budget have a line for future cost? → If not, the far cost never enters the account.
    2. Has anyone drawn the decision-maker's tenure and the consequence cycle on one chart? → Never drawn means the mismatch is never faced.
    3. Who signs off on the cross-period result? → Nobody does means a commons.
    4. How does a long-dated liability show up in the current-period statement? → If it does not, the ledger is forgetting on people's behalf.
    5. Does the bill list the next incumbent will inherit exist? → If it does not, handover is where blame gets passed.

    The Invisibility of Maintenance (Chapter 29)

    1. Where is the three-year curve of maintenance as a share of budget? → Falling year on year with a constant asset count means the criterion is hit.
    2. Is the maintenance register auditable, in what was done, when, and against which standard? → Not auditable means soft spending.
    3. In the maintenance ledger, does "nothing happened" count as output? → If it does not, you lose every budget review.
    4. Who signed the last decision to cut maintenance? → A signer who did not know the consequence means delayed settlement.
    5. When was the maintenance standard last updated against the vendor manual? → A version cycle behind means the standard is expiring.

    Handover Rupture (Chapter 30)

    1. Does documentation exist for why key processes are done this way? → If only the how exists, the judgment is being lost.
    2. Does the handover list carry a column for what has gone wrong here before? → If it does not, the lessons leave with the person.
    3. After the veteran leaves, what channel carries questions beginning with why? → No channel means the rupture is a fact.
    4. Can you list the places where the documentation states conclusions only? → If you cannot, the blind spots are unknown.
    5. What answer did the last newcomer get when asking why? → "It has always been like this" means criterion ③ is hit.

    Regulatory Lag and Capture (Chapter 31)

    1. What share of the regulator's information comes from the regulated? → A dominant share means the structural condition for capture is present.
    2. What is the lag between the last rule update and the change in technology? → A generation means lag is now normal.
    3. Has anyone computed the ratio of penalty to profit from violation? → Below 1 means the fine is a membership fee.
    4. What is the record of people moving between regulator and regulated? → Frequent movement both ways means the revolving door turns.
    5. Who drafted the key clause? → The regulated party means drafting power has leaked out.

    Compliant and Unsafe (Chapter 32)

    1. Who owns the gaps between inspection items? → Nobody means the gaps are free.
    2. After a compliance check, has anyone asked whether the compliant items taken together are safe? → Never asked means nobody owns the whole.
    3. Are safety meeting agendas built on violations or on risk? → Violations only means a Safety-I loop.
    4. Has a compliance certificate ever stood in for a safety judgment? → If so, criterion ③ is hit.
    5. Is the list of exceptions and waivers getting longer or shorter? → Longer means the rules are failing while the paper stays clean.

    Cascading Failure (Chapter 33)

    1. Does the risk register carry a line for third-party dependency? → If not, someone else's risk is not risk.
    2. Do you know who your key supplier's supplier is? → Not knowing means two layers down is a black box.
    3. Has the isolation and degradation plan been rehearsed? → No rehearsal means a cascade takes everything.
    4. Has anyone estimated the exit cost of a single-source supplier? → Not estimated means exit capacity is evaporating.
    5. Has a third party's accident ever entered your own post-mortem? → Never means the learning radius stops at your wall.

    Path Lock-in (Chapter 34)

    1. When was the last exercise on what you would choose if you started from zero today? → Never means locked in without knowing it.
    2. How much has the current estimate of migration cost risen against three years ago? → Rising without review means the lock is deepening.
    3. When did an alternative last appear in a selection review? → Long ago means the evaluation has become ritual.
    4. Does anyone book the benefits of the lock-in, in compatibility, network effect and scale? → Booking the pain only means the exercise will be distorted.
    5. Is the trigger condition for breaking the lock written down? → No trigger means the lock has no keyhole.

    How to Run the Review Meeting

    The pass has a fixed method, in four steps.

    Step one, grouping, ten minutes, done by the chair beforehand. Take the mechanism groups the locator table hits, add the general group, land on 20 to 30 questions, and copy them into the agenda. Under each question leave three lines: answer, evidence (date plus source), verdict (answerable, black, refused).

    Step two, the pass, each respondent time-limited. Questions are read out one at a time and the designated respondent answers. The answer must carry a date and a source, and an adjective counts as no answer. Ninety seconds per question: no dated evidence inside ninety seconds is recorded as black, with no debate, no explanation, no filling of the gap. The count of black questions is the main output of the review.

    Step three, disposition of the black questions, fifteen minutes. Each black question gets three things settled on the spot: what evidence is missing, who supplies it by which date, who reads it once supplied. A black question that cannot be disposed of this way, because the evidence does not exist and nobody can produce it, is upgraded to a design gap and goes into the action column of the conclusion page (Chapter 36).

    Step four, minutes, five minutes. One page: the black question list plus the disposition commitments. The reviewer of the minutes is chosen by the same standard as the conclusion page in Chapter 36.

    The part of this method most often dropped is the time limit and the discipline of the black call. Without a time limit the review slides back into a briefing. Let explanations in and adjectives swamp the numbers again. Ninety seconds and the hardness of the black mark are the whole secret of turning the list from a document into a tool.

    Here is a redacted specimen of the minutes, three black questions in their real format, with the details invented.

    Q8 (dependency list update): black. Missing: the automatic dependency list does not cover the three legacy-connection systems. Supply: architecture group to produce the legacy connection list within two weeks. Review: brought by default to the next review. Q9 (career outcome of the person who stopped it): black. Missing: no record. Disposition: no evidence to be supplied; upgraded to a design issue, with a stop-authority clause written into the project charter. Q7 (review of the definition change): answerable. Evidence: definition register, 2026-09-14, independent review signature by the risk interface owner.

    Three entries, three dispositions: one that can be evidenced (a name and a date), one that cannot (upgraded to a design issue), one green (source given). Green questions go into the minutes too, for a reason that surfaces six months later. In a post-mortem you need to know which pieces of knowledge were lit at the time, and a lamp that was lit is also a coordinate for reconstructing what nobody knew then.

    Due Diligence and the Outside View

    In an outsider's hands the list is a different instrument. Investment due diligence, an audit entrance, an acquisition assessment, supplier qualification: each can put the questions and count the distribution of black. Two things differ from the internal self-check.

    An outsider has no obligation to smooth things over, so black is black and the count comes out cleaner. Keep raw counts: how many of the general ten went black, how many went black in each hit mechanism group, and whether the black questions could be assigned an evidence owner on the spot. Those three numbers draw a profile of the organization's knowledge stock that is uglier and harder to fake than any qualitative paragraph in a due-diligence report.

    The outsider also adds three questions the internal self-check does not use. When was your last internal self-check, and does the black list still exist? An organization that has never self-checked will come back near-total black from outside. After the last external review, how many black questions were evidenced? The completion rate is a direct readout of learning capacity. Is there any question considered awkward to ask inside? If there is, list them: the awkward questions are a map of the relationships.

    The output is a two-page list: distribution of black, evidence commitments, date of the next review. Writing the review date onto the output is the last step that makes the method take effect.

    Maintaining the Question Library

    The library is not a finished document. Three rules keep it alive.

    Growth comes from post-mortems. After each one (Chapter 38), ask whether this accident contained a question that, asked at the decision point, would have changed the direction. If it did, rewrite it as a question, add it to the library, and tag the source case and the date. The library then grows on the organization's tuition, and the tuition is not wasted.

    Retire and de-rate. A question green for three consecutive reviews, with everyone answering and evidence complete, is demoted to a spot-check question. Green for six consecutive reviews, it leaves the standing set and joins the re-green file. The library stays sharp this way; a green question left in the standing set only dilutes the density of the pass.

    Keep a version number and a change log. Every addition, deletion or edit gets one line: date, question number, action, source. The log turns "how long since your list was updated" into a question with an answer, and that question is the first of the three external ones.

    The owner belongs in quality or safety, the same administrator who holds the deviation register (Chapter 11, Device 1) and the post-mortem archive (Chapter 38). Three registers under one keeper, reconciled once a quarter, and the cross-reading is where the insight comes from.

    Quick Recipes

    Three recipes for the common occasions, to save the time spent choosing groups.

    Recipe A, new system launch review: the full general ten plus groups 21 (Hidden Coupling), 22 (The Illusion of Redundancy), 26 (Automation and Skill Decay) and 30 (Handover Rupture), 30 questions. A launch replaces yesterday's set of coupling relationships wholesale, so the weight falls on dependency and takeover.

    Recipe B, organizational expansion or acquisition review: the full general ten plus groups 16 (Information Attenuation), 18 (Diffusion of Responsibility), 24 (Crossing a Scale Threshold) and 33 (Cascading Failure). Expansion lengthens the reporting chain, lengthens the signature chain and widens the dependency surface, and these four groups sit on exactly those three things.

    Recipe C, budget cut review: the full general ten plus groups 20 (The Slack of Success), 25 (Margin Erosion), 29 (The Invisibility of Maintenance) and 28 (Horizon Mismatch). A cut rests on somebody's assumption that these inputs produce nothing, and these four groups check that assumption's books line by line.

    A recipe is a starting point. If the locator table hits a mechanism outside the recipe, add that group. Recipes are revised every six months against post-mortem data, and the mechanisms that actually occurred in this organization always take priority in the next revision.

    Three Ways a Pass Fails

    Failure one: the pass becomes a performance. Respondents receive the questions in advance and prepare answers, and the review turns into a re-reading of the briefing. Test it by swapping the respondent on two questions without notice; if the person swapped in cannot answer, the pass is acting. Countermeasure: name respondents on the spot, with no advance notice.

    Failure two: question fatigue. After a year of use every question has a template answer, the pass runs shorter, black grows rarer, and the organization acquires the feeling of rapid progress. Test it by rewriting one question, same meaning with all the wording changed, and watch whether black returns. A return means the earlier green was memorization. Countermeasure: the de-rating and the rewording rotation in rule two.

    Failure three: black questions never settle. Black marks pile up, everyone nods at the disposition step, and nobody supplies the evidence afterwards. Test it by spot-checking the previous round's black questions, to see whether the promised evidence exists and carries a date. Countermeasure: the disposition commitments go straight into the action column of the conclusion page, sharing its reviewer, so the question list rides the checking mechanism Chapter 36 already built instead of raising a second one.

    The common antidote to all three is one thing: keep the list in contact with reality, by swapping people, rewording, and spot-checking commitments. Like any register, a question library that stops touching reality degrades into a printed document.

    Five of the general questions carry the opening of a session, so they get their own notes; the texture of the first few minutes sets the quality of the whole meeting.

    Q1, which second is irreversible: force the specific. "Launch day" is not an answer; "the second the switchover takes effect" is. The function of this question is to drag the meeting from the conceptual layer to the physical layer, and every question after it benefits from the pull. A failure to answer usually means the decision-maker has not broken the decision down to the action layer, and at that point stopping to take the decision apart is worth more than continuing the pass.

    Q3, which items carry timestamps: listen for the age of the evidence. If every timestamped answer predates the last change in the environment, the answer amounts to nothing. Follow with one question: what has changed in the environment since this material was produced? If it changed and the material was not updated, black.

    Q5, where the stop authority is written: ask for the original text, not the retelling. A retold stop authority evaporates at the first conflict; a written one does not. "There is a consensus on this" counts as black, because a consensus that never enters a document does not exist. That is the oral version of the Chapter 18 criterion.

    Q8, when the dependency list was updated: let the system answer for itself. The best answer is a list generated automatically, with the generation time at the link; second best is a manual list with an update date. A manual list untouched for more than a quarter counts as black.

    Q10, where the person who pays in three years sits: this one is a thermometer, measuring the horizon awareness in the room. A cold answer, whether nobody can answer or the whole room laughs, is itself the heaviest line in the minutes. A cold room is not a deduction. After the cold, turn the question into one action on the conclusion page, a one-page handover forecast written for whoever takes over in three years, and the thermometer has become construction.

    Training Use: A Three-Week Plan

    The raw material for the plan is the list and nothing else.

    Week one: the newcomer takes the general ten and interviews three veterans, half an hour each, recording only the answers and the hesitation inside them. Output: a three-way comparison across the ten. What the newcomer learns is the real distribution of knowledge in this organization, which questions have stock underneath and which are empty.

    Week two: the newcomer takes two mechanism groups, the two most relevant to the post, about ten questions, and has to find one real example in this organization for each or confirm there is none. Questions with no example are tagged organizational blank and confirmed with a mentor. Output: a mechanism-group list with local footnotes. What the newcomer learns is how to hang an abstract mechanism on a concrete site.

    Week three: the newcomer sits in on a real review with one task, counting the black questions and the disposition commitments in that session. Afterwards, 300 words of observation for the chair. What the newcomer learns is the method: the time limit, the black mark, the disposition. From week four the newcomer can time any review, and after three more rounds can serve as respondent.

    Total cost, three half-days of interviews and one session sat in. Output, one usable review participant. Traditional training gives newcomers knowledge; this plan gives them questions. In failure prevention, questions are the scarcer resource (the knowledge-inflation argument in Chapter 4), and scarce training resources belong on the scarcer side.

    Links to the Tools in Chapters 38 and 39

    The upstream and downstream relations are worth setting out. Upstream sits the locator table in Chapter 35, which decides which groups you take. Get that step wrong and the whole pass idles, so outside recipes A, B and C, a hit mechanism group always outranks recipe habit.

    Two downstream. The first: black questions feed the evidence layer of the Chapter 38 post-mortem. What a post-mortem lacks most is a snapshot of knowledge at the decision point, and the black list is the negative of that snapshot, the record of what nobody knew then. With the black list pulled up, reconstructing what was not known at the time has a basis, and hindsight contamination is blocked one layer further. The second: the pass is the twin of the Chapter 39 rehearsal. A rehearsal works backwards from a failure that already happened; the pass works forwards from the question of whether you can answer. Both probe the same dark region of knowledge, and the two outputs can check each other. If every hazard the rehearsal turned up can be ruled out by a green answer to some question, the rehearsal is going through the motions. If the pass comes back all green and the rehearsal finds three real hazards, the green answers are watered. Compare the two outputs side by side once a year, and the list has a two-way reference for its calibration.

    Remote and Cross-Team Adaptation

    Three points for remote reviews. Keep cameras on during the answering step, because the black call depends on raw information in faces and hesitations, and a text-only channel translates "cannot answer" into "will reply later", costing a grade of accuracy. Project the minutes and the black list live in a shared document so everyone watches the black count grow; visibility is what holds the discipline of the pass together (the meeting version of the sticky-note principle in Chapter 36). For teams across time zones, split the pass in two: the question and answer block sits in the overlap, with the time discipline unchanged, and the disposition block runs asynchronously, each black question claimed in the document with the claim timestamped. A joint review across teams adds one step: each side runs the general ten alone before the joint session, and comparing the two black distributions exposes the knowledge gap better than a joint pass. Two teams' different answers to the same question are a risk portrait of the interface zone, the Chapter 33 territory projected onto questions.

    Self-check runs on a different rhythm from a meeting: one person, one list. Three rhythms for three depths.

    Monthly quick check, fifteen minutes: the general ten only, answered aloud, with the questions you cannot answer written down. The point is familiarity; questions atrophy like muscle without use.

    Quarterly self-check, ninety minutes: the groups the locator table hits plus the general group, answered in writing, each answer with a date and a source, producing a black list. That black list goes straight into the action column of the Chapter 36 conclusion page, or joins the team review's black pool. No separate pile.

    Annual comparison, half a day: lay the four quarterly black lists side by side and read three trajectories. Black in all four quarters is a structural blind spot, upgraded to a design issue. Black turning green is evidence of learning, written into the annual review. Green turning black is recent decay, handled first. This comparison carries the most information of anything in self-check: one black list tells you where the gaps are, four tell you which way the organization is drifting.

    The most common break point is month three. The first two months are fresh; in the third a project squeezes the check out. The countermeasure is the same one the locator table's implementation schedule uses: put it on the agenda, first Monday of a fixed month, no exceptions.

    One hidden return is worth writing down. A person's answer history against the list is their own growth record in failure knowledge. Turn back twelve months in the annual comparison and most people find their own optimism, and that finding is the self version of the Chapter 6 swap test.

    One Question in Three Role Versions

    Different levels need different wording for the same suspicion. Take question 5 of the general group, the stop authority, as the example; rewrite the rest by the same method.

    Engineer's version: in the part of the work I hold, when I think something is wrong, what is the shortest path to stopping it, and who will listen? If the answer contains telling my manager and my manager telling his manager, the path is longer than the danger radius of the step, which is the field form of the Chapter 18 criterion.

    Manager's version: how many hops does the stop signal travel from the person closest to the danger in my team to me, and does it decay at each hop? The question forces the manager to count his own reporting chain, and Chapters 16 and 18 merge here.

    Executive's version: when was the last instance of a stop coming from below in this organization, and what happened to that person afterwards? The age of the instance and the fate of the person, taken together, are the whole evidence for whether the stop authority is real.

    All three have the same structure: the suspicion does not change, and the addressee and the shape of the answer follow the power position. One rule governs the rewrite. The role version must not blunt the suspicion. Softening "who will listen" into "everyone needs to be aware of this" kills the question on the rewriting desk.

    The Cost and the Return, Roughly

    A rough account for readers still hesitating. On the cost side, one review pass including grouping and minutes runs about two hours; the quarterly self-check ninety minutes; criteria cards and list maintenance about an hour a quarter. That totals roughly forty work-hours a year for a ten-person team, one person's week.

    On the return side, two references. The direct loss of any grade-A case in this book runs from a $950,000 fine to eleven deaths, and the reader can convert the proportion of that class the list might have blocked into their own organization's units. The account stops there; more arithmetic would be slick. The list still fails to stop a great many accidents. It stops the class where nobody ever asked, and that class appears often enough in post-mortem records to justify the week.

    There is an intangible side too. The pass changes how meetings talk. Adjectives retire on their own in front of a demand for dated evidence, and the change spills into meetings where no question list is present. Spillover cannot be booked, but teams that have used the list report it: after one pass, "it should be fine" buys a good deal less in that team.

    Backing One Group, Question by Question

    The Normalization of Deviance group serves as the demonstration: five questions, each with a case from this book standing behind it, so you can see where each question came from.

    Q1, how many times and on what basis each was judged consequence-free, stands on Challenger and Columbia: the evaluation history of O-ring erosion at Marshall, in the transcription layer of CAIB and of the House investigation materials, and the per-flight no-disaster table for foam shedding (CAIB p.124). What the two cases share is that a history of no consequence was taken as proof of no risk. The question puts that substitution back on the table and runs it again.

    Q2, margin as number or adjective, stands on a language phenomenon shared across several chapters: from "the margin is still sufficient" to "still sufficient" to "sufficient", the evaporation history of three words. The function of the question is to make the organization write the number back. No number and no source counts as no margin (the review version of Device 2 in Chapter 11).

    Q3, who approved the relaxation, stands on av-034: the extension of lubrication and end-gap inspection intervals was applied for by the operator and approved by the FAA, on a single information source. The question presses exactly on the information make-up of the approval chain, where an operator's application plus an operator's data means nobody checked.

    Q4, how a newcomer's doubt was handled, stands on the review room in mi-001 and on the veteran narrative phenomenon that several cases share. A newcomer's question is a free blind-spot survey, and the way it is handled, answered or restated as custom, measures the organization's cognitive openness directly.

    Q5, which release can be completed without a written reason, stands on nu-002, where nobody verified after the placard went up. The question forces out the list of waived releases. If the list exists, it is a map of the gaps. If it does not, the waivers are being granted in the dark. Both results need handling.

    The demonstration can be repeated on any group. Find a case in this book for each question, searchable by mechanism in the case index of Appendix B, then find a local instance to land it on. The book's case proves the question is worth asking; your instance proves whether you are asking it in time. Put the two side by side and the question is actually installed.

    The Closing Question

    1. Of all the questions above, how many can you answer with dated evidence inside three minutes? → Fewer than five means the organization's stock of knowledge about failure is thinner than its memory of success. The cause sits in the system rather than in you (Chapter 4), but the window to change it is in your hands (Chapters 38 and 39).

    The closing question deserves a second read. It presses the weight of all 130 into one number: how many answers you can produce with dated evidence in three minutes. Organizationally that number measures the thickness of the knowledge stock. Personally it is more direct, because it is what you carry out of the room. Every output of the pass eventually converts into movement in this number, and comparing the number quarter to quarter is more honest than comparing any briefing's adjectives. The list closes here. The 130 questions are the tackle; the closing question is the scale; a catch only counts once it has been weighed. Put the scale on the platform every quarter, on the procedure already written in the self-check section. The first reading is usually unflattering, and that is normal, because the list begins by admitting the dark area is larger than you thought.
