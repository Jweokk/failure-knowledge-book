    Chapter 11 Normalization of Deviance (Vaughan, 1996)

    What this chapter covers: how a deviation turns into standard practice on the strength of "nothing happened last time"; three trigger criteria, the shape the mechanism takes in four domains, eight weak signals and the devices that block it. When to come back to it: when someone answers a challenge with "we have always done it this way"; when you are about to approve a request to relax a standard, extend an interval or waive an inspection. Reading time: about 55 minutes.

    11.1 What It Is

    Definition: the first appearance of a deviation is handled as an anomaly. As it recurs and produces no consequence, it is reinterpreted as acceptable normal; the explanation stops entering the register; the inspection and release criteria move quietly. Diane Vaughan named the mechanism while studying the Challenger launch decision (Vaughan, The Challenger Launch Decision, University of Chicago Press, 1996). The coldest part of her account is this. Normalization does not happen in the explanation. It happens in the filing. Every deviant event is explained by the organization, which says why this one did no harm, and then filed, which says which category it belongs to. Explanations are public and arguable. Filings are quiet and not arguable. The substantive move of normalization is to shift a deviation out of the drawer marked "to be eliminated" and into the drawer marked "known, acceptable", and the basis for the filing is the outcome: nothing happened.

    Three trigger criteria. If any one of them is present, the mechanism is already running.

    ① A deviation of the same class has appeared twice or more, and none of the occurrences produced a consequence. ② Each occurrence was explained as "there is still margin", "a one-off", "contained", and none of the explanations generated a register entry. ③ An inspection standard, a limit or an interval was lowered or relaxed and written into the process, or practice took the place of process.

    Normalization runs through three phases, each with its own archival signature. In the anomaly phase the deviation appears for the first time, enters the register, the entry stays open, and the handling work is organized around removing the cause. In the case phase the deviation appears a second and a third time, each occurrence is explained on its own terms (margin, coincidence, special conditions), the register entries are closed one by one, and no combined statistic across entries is ever produced. In the routine phase the appearance of the deviation no longer triggers a register action at all. Handling is fixed in the process or in habit, and "we did it this way before" becomes the oral register. What separates the three phases is attention. The anomaly phase consumes attention, the case phase sustains it, the routine phase releases it. The mechanism's end product is a reallocation of attention: the attention that used to watch the deviation is moved elsewhere by the organization, while the deviation keeps running where it was.

    People assume normalization needs an explicit decision to tolerate something. In most cases no such decision can be found. The "in family" clause in section 11.3, the alarms that close in seconds, the noise explanation: each is a structure that only becomes visible when a large number of small handling actions are strung together and read backwards. Standing at the moment any one of those actions was taken, the person saw a reasonable piece of work. That is the argument for the three-state register in section 11.5. Reasonableness seen from one seat cannot be re-checked by addition. Only the register can do that.

    Why "no consequence" carries almost no evidential weight needs saying once, plainly. A run with a deviation that lands safely proves very little. It proves this run did not hit the tail, and nobody knows where the tail sits. Stack fifty safe landings and the evidence grows far more slowly than intuition suggests, because the runs are correlated with one another (the same people, the same practice, the same tempo) and the information they carry overlaps heavily. An organization that reads a consequence-free period as proof of safety is reading an intuitive count of independent trials. The real trials are neither independent nor repeated often enough to separate hypothesis A from hypothesis B. That is also why weak signal 8 in section 11.4 is so hard to refute. The challenger cannot produce more data, because the data only exists after the consequence. Normalization wins arguments in a peculiar way. It does not need to win. It needs to last until the data arrives.

    The arithmetic core is worth setting out, because it explains why "we have always done it this way" is an understandable error rather than a stupid one. The deviation has appeared n times with no consequence, and two hypotheses are on the table. Under A the margin really is sufficient, and the absence of consequence is the structure's doing. Under B the margin is not sufficient, and the absence of consequence is luck. The organization usually holds no data that discriminates between them. Discrimination requires knowing the tail of the distribution, and the tail is precisely what has not happened yet. Normalization is the default to A when the data cannot discriminate. The cost of that default is set by the compounding of exposures. Every run with the deviation is one exposure. Under B the accident probability rises with the number of exposures; under A it stays constant. The installment-type irreversibility of Chapter 7 is this arithmetic in the form it takes at a release decision.

    11.2 How It Grows

    Four generating conditions, corresponding to structure, incentive, time scale and information.

    Structural: the deviation is produced at the front line, and the authority to remove it sits far away. Stopping a line, changing a supplier, redesigning one detail: high cost, long process. Writing an explanation: low cost, finished on the spot. When the gap between the cost of removing and the cost of explaining is wide enough, the organization chooses explaining again and again.

    Incentive: the person who explains the deviation does not carry the deviation's tail risk. The manager who signs the relaxation is usually transferred before the consequence lands (Chapter 28), or the deviation itself yields a current-period benefit in output, on-time rate or labor hours. Where the person who sees the deviation is also the person who benefits from it (Chapter 17), the explanation drifts toward hypothesis A on its own.

    Time scale: the consequence is delayed. Delay breaks the learning loop. Months or years sit between the deviation and the consequence, and by the time the consequence arrives, the record of the deviation has already been overwritten with "explained". Delay has a side effect. A stretch of no consequences is itself evidence, to the organization, that hypothesis A holds. Success becomes the fuel of deviation; the slack of success in Chapter 20 is the sister mechanism.

    Information: no cumulative register. Each deviation is assessed on its own, from the material of that one event plus the memory of the people involved. A list of historical deviations does not exist, or exists with every entry marked "explained". With no independent review, the hypothesis-A explanation has nothing to be confronted with.

    There is one amplifier: years in the trade. Personal experience is not built like an organizational register. "I did this for twenty years and nothing happened" treats a personal exposure count as data, and its sampling bias is the microscopic version of this chapter's mechanism. It also sits on the border with Chapter 14: intuition is most confident inside its own failure zone.

    One archival property of the mechanism needs adding after the generating conditions. The status of a register entry decides who reads it later, and with how much attention. An entry marked "explained" presents itself in every later search, every handover, every audit as a settled matter. The reader assumes it no longer needs judgment, only awareness. Chapter 30, handover rupture, meets this chapter exactly here. The entries on a handover sheet marked "legacy item, dealt with" are how normalization travels between departments.

    The consequence-free period has a second contaminating effect: it rewrites the baseline for future assessment. Every run with the deviation produces a record that says the run was normal. Those records enter the experience base, and later risk assessments use them as their template. The sample the assessor sees has already been screened of bad outcomes. This is the survivorship bias of Chapter 2 reproduced inside the organization. The filing action rewrites the organization's definition of normal every day, and the rewriting raises no alarm.

    Among the four generating conditions, the structural one is the most often misread, as "the front line is a long way from headquarters". Distance is the shell. The core is a difference in standing between two kinds of knowledge. Design knowledge is written into documents; operating knowledge is scattered among people. Documents can be searched, cited and audited. Knowledge held in people cannot, so every collision between the two ends with the document winning, and the real operating practice survives outside the documents under the name of custom. Normalization is that survival, filed. Before custom produces a consequence, the organization's official language has no word for it. The front-line representative signature clause in the device list of section 11.5 is aimed at this gap in standing. Its job is to give operating knowledge the same right to be cited as a document.

    11.3 The Pattern in Four Domains

    The six cases below come from four domains. Following the book's convention, each stops at the decision point: you decide first, then turn the page for the outcome. Case numbers match Appendix B. Cases one, two and five are given as full cards, each backed by a grade-A full report from an independent investigation. The other three stay condensed.

    Case 1 (Spaceflight): The "In-Family" Classification of a Foam Strike

    mi-001.

    When and where: 16 January 2003, launch at 10:39 a.m. local time; 1 February 2003, entry interface at 8:44:09 a.m., loss of communication with the ground at 8:59 a.m. Locations: Kennedy Space Center, Florida (launch and imagery interpretation); Marshall Space Flight Center, Huntsville, Alabama (external tank project, flight readiness review); Johnson Space Center, Houston, Texas (debris assessment team, mission evaluation room, mission management team).

    What and who: The NASA Space Shuttle Program office (SSP); the Marshall external tank project (ET Project); the Johnson engineering directorate and structures division; engineers from United Space Alliance and Boeing; the Debris Assessment Team (DAT, led by two co-chairs, one from NASA and one from United Space Alliance); the Mission Evaluation Room (MER, the project office's engineering support group); the Mission Management Team (MMT, chaired by an appointee of Johnson); and the Program Requirements Control Board (PRCB), the body that signs the flight readiness review. The STS-107 crew numbered 7 and spent 16 days on orbit.

    What they were doing: On 7 October 2002, STS-112 launched and a piece of foam roughly 4×5×12 inches came off the left bipod ramp of the external tank, tearing the insulation on the solid rocket booster attach ring (CAIB p.126, figure 6.1-5). On 31 October, at Marshall, the PRCB heard the external tank project manager, Jerry Smelser, at the STS-113 flight readiness review. His briefing was titled "STS-112/ET-115 Bipod Ramp Foam Loss" and ran four pages (CAIB p.126). That loss had occurred three months before Columbia's last launch (CAIB p.124, figure 6.1-3). The briefing gave three flight rationale: the foam came off the -Y bipod ramp and exposed the bipod fitting; the current fitting had not changed since STS-54 (ET-51); and across more than a hundred external tanks only 3 significant bipod foam losses had been recorded (CAIB p.126). At the same review the NASA headquarters safety office submitted a report estimating a 99% probability that no further foam would come off that area (CAIB p.127). The review's conclusion characterized the foam loss as an "acceptable risk" rather than as "not a flight safety issue", and the STS-113 certificate of flight readiness was signed (CAIB p.127). STS-113 launched at night in November; no imagery was obtained, so whether foam came off again could not be determined (CAIB p.127). The judgment rested on a long ledger. After the first flight in 1981, 300 tiles were replaced. Of the 79 missions with imagery, 65 captured foam loss, and the orbiter's lower surface averaged 143 dings after return (CAIB p.123). STS-107, meanwhile, flew as a 16-day science mission with no robotic arm, and RCC panels 1 through 11 on the left wing leading edge could not be seen from inside the cabin (CAIB p.147).

    The decision point: ① 31 October 2002, Marshall, STS-113 flight readiness review. On the table: the foam dimensions and damage photographs from STS-112, the three flight rationale from the external tank project, and the 99% estimate from the headquarters safety office. The Image Working Group had earlier recommended that the STS-112 loss be recorded as an In-Flight Anomaly; the recommendation was not adopted at the review (CAIB p.126). CAIB's re-check found the first and third rationale incorrect. The bipod ramp design had been changed at ET-76, and the 99% calculation pooled the left and right bipods and set the start of its sample after STS-112, which left out STS-7, STS-32R and STS-50 (CAIB p.127). The meeting's conclusion was written as "acceptable risk", and the certificate was signed (CAIB p.127).

    ② 22 January 2003, in the morning, Houston: the imaging request is withdrawn. At 8:09 the MER log records that "Engelauf wanted to know who was having the Air Force look at the vehicle" (CAIB p.154). At 8:30 NASA's Department of Defense liaison called US Strategic Command to withdraw the imaging request; less than 90 minutes separated initiation from withdrawal (CAIB p.154). After the withdrawal, a 9:33 email from Ham to Austin and Roe was still asking the same question in another form: could anyone state that no external tank foam loss could cause a flight safety problem, on grounds of density (CAIB p.155). At 10:15 Dittemore replied that someone first had to confirm that foam density could not strike a tile hard enough to affect the orbiter (CAIB p.156). At 10:37 the log records the conclusion: "Shriver, Ham, Roe have indicated they do not need Air Force look at vehicle" (CAIB p.157).

    ③ 24 January 2003, 7 a.m., Houston: the debris assessment team briefed the MER. That day the MER manager gave the MMT an oral summary with no data. The MMT classified the impact as a "turnaround issue" and stopped pursuing imagery (CAIB p.39).

    What was visible then: On 17 January at 9:30 EST, image analysts at Marshall saw the impact at 81.9 seconds on film, and within an hour Kennedy confirmed it on higher-resolution film (CAIB p.141). Only three ground cameras could see the impact at all: one lost tracking during ascent, one was out of focus from improper lens maintenance, and one captured only the upper surface of the left wing (CAIB p.141). At 10:58 CST the same day, the MER log recorded debris "from the external tank forward bipod area, traveling down the left side, striking the left wing leading edge near the fuselage" (CAIB p.142). At 15:15 Bob Page, chair of the Image Working Group, telephoned Wayne Hale and Lambert Austin: Boeing was computing trajectory, velocity, angle and energy (CAIB p.142). At 16:28 the log records that "USA and Boeing are not working the debris problem over the weekend, waiting for film on Monday" (CAIB p.143). On 18 January Boeing produced two size estimates, 20×20×2 inches and 20×16×6 inches, with a velocity of about 750 feet per second and an incidence angle under 20 degrees (CAIB p.144). At 11:39 on 20 January the log records debris "about 20 inches, plus or minus 10 inches, similar in size to the STS-112 event" (CAIB p.147). At 9:26 on 21 January, Schomburg wrote by email: "TPS took a hit, probably not a problem" (CAIB p.150). The debris assessment team held its first formal meeting the same day (CAIB p.152). At 9:01 on 22 January, Air Force Lieutenant Colonel Lee emailed McCormack: "Austin called yesterday, requesting DoD high-resolution photos of the shuttle on orbit" (CAIB p.152). That same day Rocha wrote an email that was never sent: "in my poor technical judgment, SSP and the orbiter project not requesting additional imaging is wrong, close to irresponsible" (CAIB p.158). At 23:13 on 23 January, Stich emailed the crew: "the experts have looked at the high-speed photography, there is no concern for RCC or tile, and absolutely no concern for entry" (CAIB p.160). At 12:15 on 28 January, the safety and quality assurance seat's log mentioned debris for the second time, asking whether anyone from SR&QA had been involved in the decision that the ascent debris strike was safe (CAIB p.167). Information that became available only afterwards: the foam struck RCC panels 5 through 9, panel 8 was breached, hot gas entered the wing leading edge spar, and the aluminum structure melted. None of this appeared before the debris reconstruction that followed 1 February.

    Stop here. Decide before you turn the page.

    You are in that seat now, with the materials above in front of you.

    1. What would you do? Write the action, not "pay more attention".
    2. Which item here are you most likely to miss, and why does it look unimportant?
    3. If you may set one checkpoint, when is it and what does it check?

    What happened: At 8:44:09 on 1 February 2003 the orbiter entered the atmosphere. At 8:59 communication with the ground was lost, and the vehicle broke up over Texas and Louisiana. All seven astronauts died. During the search a Bell 407 helicopter crashed in Angelina National Forest, killing the contract pilot and a Texas Forest Service employee and injuring 3 (CAIB p.47). Ground searchers passed 3,000 within two weeks and 4,000 within a month (CAIB p.46). Back to the three questions. The most common answer to the first is "ask for the imagery again". CAIB lists it as the 4th of eight missed opportunities: the request was withdrawn within 90 minutes, and the person who withdrew it was not an engineer (CAIB p.154, p.168). The item most often missed on the second question is the four slides of 31 October. They described a design that had already been changed as unchanged, and that sentence was the pivot of the entire flight rationale (CAIB p.127). The third question lands on 17 January. The Image Working Group had classified the strike as "out of family" that day, and under project procedure an event of that class should have been handled as a Tiger Team. What was formed was a DAT, which is not bound by the Tiger Team checklist, so the event belonged nowhere in the procedure (CAIB p.143).

    Options ruled out: Have the crew photograph the left wing with a handheld camera. Rocha asked on 17 January whether the crew could inspect the wing; no response is recorded (CAIB p.168). Columbia had no robotic arm, RCC panels 1 through 11 were not visible from the cabin, and a visual check required a spacewalk (CAIB p.147). Request Department of Defense imaging. It was initiated on 22 January and withdrawn within 90 minutes (CAIB p.154). Change the entry trajectory to reduce heating. The engineering analysis concluded that no alternative trajectory would significantly reduce the heat load on that area (CAIB p.161). Upgrade the DAT to a Tiger Team. The event had been classified out of family, but that procedure was never run; the team was left "organizationally adrift" with only a date to report: 24 January (CAIB p.143). Extend the mission and wait for on-orbit imagery. Not found in any meeting record. Declare the strike a flight safety issue outright. CAIB records the actual requirement as the reverse: engineers had to prove it unsafe before management would move, while the standing expectation was to prove it safe (CAIB p.170).

    Early signals: ① 18 June 1983, STS-7: the left bipod ramp lost a 19×12 inch piece of foam, leaving 25 shallow divots forward of the bipod strut and 40 divots on the lower external tank. The post-flight PRCB recorded it as an in-flight anomaly and closed it at the next review on the grounds that the orbiter thermal protection system had been repaired. Neither the closure document nor the flight readiness certificate mentioned the cause of the foam loss (CAIB p.124). Suppression reason: it was logged under procedure, so it counted as handled. ② 9 January 1990, STS-32R: 5 divots in intertank foam with diameters from 6 to 28 inches, 111 strikes on the orbiter's lower surface with 13 over 1 inch. The anomaly was closed at the STS-36 review as a "local foam void", vent holes were subsequently drilled in the foam, and the mechanism has never been confirmed (CAIB p.125). ③ 2 December 1988, STS-27R: 707 divots, 298 of them over 1 inch, one tile knocked off and structure exposed. After the ablator was replaced, the problem counted as closed before the next review (CAIB p.128). The same investigation recommended that the project "actively solicit design improvements to eliminate the source of debris or reduce the potential for damage"; 14 years later there was still no corresponding action (CAIB p.129). ④ 25 June 1992, STS-50: the third bipod foam loss, characterized in Hazard Report 37 as an "accepted risk", with the external tank project writing at the same time that it was "not a flight safety issue" (CAIB p.125). ⑤ 19 November 1997, STS-87: thermal protection damage prompted NASA to run 9 flight tests to settle the foam loss question. The fix did not work, and the in-flight anomaly was closed after STS-101 as an "accepted risk" (CAIB p.129). ⑥ 7 October 2002, STS-112: the fourth, with foam of 4×5×12 inches tearing the booster attach ring insulation. The review of 31 October passed it with two lines, "design unchanged" and "99% no loss" (CAIB pp. 126–127). The suppression reasons collapse into one sentence: this is a class of event already logged, already explained, with no consequence on record. CAIB's count is 7 left bipod losses in 113 flights, about 10% of the 72 flights with usable imagery (CAIB p.124).

    The point of escalation: 31 October 2002, Marshall, where the STS-113 flight readiness review wrote "acceptable risk" into its conclusion and the certificate of flight readiness was signed shortly after (CAIB p.127). CAIB's judgment is that this decision "made it, and possibly encouraged it, easier" for members of the MMT to use the same reasoning when they assessed the STS-107 foam strike (CAIB p.126). STS-113 then launched at night with no imagery, and in November the same rationale was forwarded by Paul Shack to Rocha by email as a reference (CAIB p.151). By 17 January, an engineer arguing "this one is different" first had to overturn the conclusion of the project's most rigorous review, held a month earlier.

    Failure chain: Technical layer: foam separated from the left bipod ramp at 81.7 seconds and struck RCC panels 5 through 9 at 625 to 840 feet per second at 81.9 seconds; panel 8 was penetrated and hot gas entered the wing leading edge spar. There is a design background underneath this. The thermal protection system was designed to withstand impacts below 0.006 foot-pounds, and the external tank requirement was that it produce no debris; both requirements were breached on the first flight in 1981, when 300 tiles were replaced (CAIB p.123). Organizational layer: the strike was filed as "in family", meaning within known experience, considered understood, and not a flight safety issue. The classification rested on the absence of past consequences, not on the energy and location of this strike (CAIB p.124). The same filing broke once, on 17 January, when the strike was reclassified as out of family, but the Tiger Team procedure that out-of-family should trigger was never started (CAIB p.143). Foam damage monitoring was judged inadequate by the project itself, a fuller record would have helped identify trends, and the improvement never landed (CAIB p.129). Incentive layer: STS-107 was a long-scheduled 16-day science mission, imaging would consume science time and require interagency channels, and the trade on 22 January was to stop a 90-minute request (CAIB pp. 154–155). The break sits between the organizational and incentive layers. The filing clause exists precisely so that anomaly signals can be dissolved quickly under schedule and resource pressure, and what it saves management is the cost of one more look.

    Minimum counterfactual: At 8:30 on 22 January 2003, do not withdraw the Department of Defense imaging request that had already been initiated. Let it run through the procedure Strategic Command was already following. One action, ten days before the breakup. The cost was a few hours of science observing time, and it required no luck and no new technical judgment, because that morning the request had already reached the stage of identifying available imaging assets (CAIB p.153). CAIB lists it as the 4th of eight missed opportunities (CAIB p.168).

    Outcome and sources: Seven astronauts died. Two search personnel died in a helicopter crash and 3 were injured. The orbiter broke up, and debris was scattered across Texas and Louisiana. CAIB found that the accident "was not a random event" and listed the way the foam loss was filed as an organizational cause (CAIB p.10, p.124). Primary source: Columbia Accident Investigation Board, Report Volume I, August 2003, NASA official PDF, link at Appendix B mi-001.

    Case 2 (Chemicals): Thirty-Two Alarms

    ch-001.

    When and where: 10 November 2014, a Monday, a mis-dilution shutdown; 12 November, an attempted restart; the morning of 14 November, the hydrate plug confirmed and a handling plan settled; 15 November, high-pressure alarm inside the building at 2:51 a.m., valves opened from 3:01 to 3:13 a.m., the last victim found between 11:15 and 11:55 a.m. Location: La Porte, Texas, the Lannate insecticide unit of the DuPont La Porte plant. The point of failure was the second-floor drain area and the third-floor vent header collection level of a multi-storey enclosed building; the control room stood outside the building.

    What and who: E. I. du Pont de Nemours and Company, La Porte plant, insecticides business unit (IBU), Lannate (methomyl) unit. The shift was nights: control board operator, shift supervisor (who also served as Process Coordinator, responsible in an emergency for giving the rescue team process information), and field operator. Three plants on the site shared one emergency response team (ERT) of about 60 people, managed by DuPont (CSB p.20, footnote). The same building also handled chlorine and methyl isocyanate (MIC), both more toxic than methyl mercaptan under site standards (CSB p.13, footnote; p.28). Methyl mercaptan was detected with borrowed hydrogen sulfide detectors, with the alarm set at 25 ppm for methyl mercaptan (CSB p.14, footnote).

    What they were doing: On 10 November a tank truck of 50% acetaldoxime (AAO) was being unloaded when a water dilution valve that should have been interlocked shut stayed fully open, because a jumper installed during the spring 2014 turnaround had not been removed. Water entered the tank, the AAO was diluted, and the unit shut down (CSB p.9, footnote; p.13). Water entered the methyl mercaptan feed line during the shutdown. Houston temperatures held below 55 Fahrenheit from 11 November, averaging about 40 Fahrenheit over the first 24 hours, hydrates formed in the line, and the restart on 12 November failed (CSB p.13, footnote; p.102). On the morning of 14 November the unit technical team met the day-shift supervisor, concluded that water had entered and formed hydrates, and decided to heat the line under its insulation with hot water hoses until the hydrates decomposed. The team also recognized that heating would expand the methyl mercaptan and that it needed somewhere to relieve, so operators opened three valves between the methyl mercaptan feed line and the vent header; at the time of the incident one was fully open and one was slightly open (CSB pp. 99–100, pp. 102–103). None of this work had a process hazard analysis, a written procedure or a management-of-change review (CSB p.100). Execution began at the tank and feed pump section and moved along the feed line toward the reactor system, with pressure gauges on the three connecting valves used to locate the plug and track progress (CSB p.103). In the early hours of 15 November the hot water melted the hydrate, and liquid methyl mercaptan passed through those three valves into the vent header, a line built for vent gas and designed never to carry liquid (CSB p.104). At 2:51 a high-pressure alarm came from equipment inside the building. The board operator treated it as condensate accumulation, as custom dictated, and sent the shift supervisor and operator 1 to drain the third floor (CSB p.15). Third-floor low-point draining had been written into the daily work instructions since 14 January 2014, once per shift (CSB p.108).

The line has low points, there is no engineering measure such as a knockout drum, and accumulated liquid could only be removed by hand from the low points (CSB p.106, p.108). CSB records that this was not considered in design; no individual signature for project approval or acceptance appears in the report. Over the following three years, several pieces of equipment connected to that header had repeated high-pressure events, and the fixed response was manual draining (CSB p.107).

    ② March 2010: a corrective action closed. A second-party PSM audit in 2009 found that the site held no documentation showing the building ventilation system had ever been tested for supply effectiveness, and recommended a periodic test procedure. The site configured an automatic work-order generator in the computerized maintenance system and closed the corrective action on that basis in March 2010, without assigning an owner (CSB pp. 97–98). The first test should have run in November 2010 and was actually delayed to 2012. The dry end was measured for airflow but never analyzed and had no acceptance criteria. The wet end, the side that failed in 2014, was never tested at all (CSB p.98).

    ③ 14 January 2014: the daily work instruction. Area management required operators, in the daily instruction book, to drain accumulated liquid from the third-floor vent header "low point" once per shift. A paragraph was added later: attach a hose and drain the liquid under an open floor safety shower until it stops flowing (CSB p.108). The instruction came with no management of change, no dedicated procedure, no line-breaking permit, and no requirement for additional protection (CSB p.108, p.111). The instruction book had no per-item signature block, and CSB names the approving source only as "area management" (CSB p.108).

    ④ The morning of 14 November 2014: the meeting of the unit technical team and the day-shift supervisor. The meeting settled on external hot water heating with three valves connected to the vent header, with no management of change and no written procedure. The hot water heating also exceeded the heat input design value of the system's thermal expansion relief valve (CSB p.104).

    What was visible then: 32 methyl mercaptan alarms at the control panel over 17 hours (CSB p.14). The detector range was 0 to 25 ppm and the alarm was set at 25 ppm, which is 2.5 times the OSHA limit of 10 ppm and 50 times the NIOSH recommended value of 0.5 ppm; readings appeared only on the DCS and never entered the historian (CSB p.15, footnote). The high-pressure alarm at 2:51 on 15 November arrived with the "condensate" custom attached to it (CSB p.15). The odor threshold of methyl mercaptan is about 2 ppb, one five-thousandth of the OSHA permissible exposure limit of 10 ppm, so smelling it says nothing about a low concentration, and prolonged exposure brings olfactory fatigue (CSB p.14, footnote). Two shift suggestion meetings, in February and October 2014, both recorded the low-point accumulation and both proposed changing the piping to eliminate the low point (CSB p.112). A management-of-change review started weeks before the incident stated in its own text that third-floor draining "was not a good practice, because the line is sometimes nitrogen-purged, which could release nitrogen inside the building" (CSB pp. 108–109). The only written note on the shift turnover record was one line: "Unplugging M[e]SH Header." (CSB p.113, footnote). An "urgent" work order had been opened on 20 October after another roof fan stopped with an abnormal noise, and a second fan had been down since June (CSB p.85). The ventilation system had no scrubber or incinerator of its own; exhaust went straight to atmosphere (CSB p.97, footnote). The 2009 audit finding and the March 2010 closure were on file (CSB pp. 97–98). Information that became available only afterwards: the concentration gradient on each floor, the release rate and the dispersion, all reconstructed by CSB modeling, with a total of about 24,000 pounds (CSB p.7, p.24).

    One detail in this case deserves a second look from the chapter's angle. The maintenance work order for the failed exhaust fan sat in the system for nearly a month with the status "urgent", and CSB's text uses that word in English, where it means handling within two to three days. The work-order system was not wrong, and the person who set the priority had a reason: ventilation plays no part in the reaction, and a stopped fan does not affect output. The gap sits between the status language and the handling. "Urgent" reads, in this chapter's archival sense, as the maintenance edition of "explained": a known defect formally logged as urgent, with nobody asking why it had not been handled within two or three days. What arrived was that defect becoming the condition that turned a release into a poisoning on the night of the incident. Device 1's three-state register, ported to a work-order system, is a one-line patch. A defect in equipment that carries a safety function may only be given the status "remediation by date", and the date is set by a party whose appraisal does not depend on output. An "urgent" with no time standard is an adjective.

    Case 3 (Aviation Maintenance): Interval Extensions, Approved One Signature at a Time (condensed)

    av-034. Alaska Airlines 261, an MD-83 (N963AS), 31 January 2000, 2.7 miles north of Anacapa Island in the Pacific. The NTSB final report (AAR-02/01; this book downloaded and read the PDF): the lubrication interval and the end-play check interval for the horizontal stabilizer jackscrew assembly had been extended at the company's request with FAA approval (executive summary p.4, verified). The report found that the extensions allowed inadequate lubrication to develop into excessive wear, that the jackscrew nut threads failed in flight, and that the 2 pilots, 3 cabin crew and 83 passengers on board all died. The decision point is the moment the FAA signed the extension. Minimum counterfactual: do not extend the end-play check interval, and check at the original interval. Primary source: NTSB, AAR-02/01, 2002, link at Appendix B av-034.

    The condensed version is worth a second look because it shows normalization's most ordinary carrier: a relaxation document with unimpeachable wording. The application came from the operator, the approval from the regulator, both acting inside their own authority, and every step is compliant when read alone. The mechanism leaves one trace. Almost all the information the approval rested on came from the applicant's own records. NTSB's executive summary sets out the path between the extension and the development of wear plainly; in hindsight, the separate reasonableness of each extension request adds up to putting the wear process of the jackscrew nut inside a sparser inspection window. Devices 2 and 4 in section 11.5 are built to this shape: numbers must be measured, and a signature must have a second pen.

    Case 5 (Aero Engine): The Low-Amplitude Indications of 2016 (full card)

    av-006.

    When and where: 20 February 2021, departure from Denver International at 13:04 local time, failure in the climb at 12,500 feet at 13:09, return and landing at 13:28, about 24 minutes from takeoff to touchdown (the flight was bound for Honolulu); the failure occurred above Broomfield, Colorado. The inspection that matters happened in April 2016, at the Pratt & Whitney overhaul shop (NTSB pp. 19–20).

    What and who: United Airlines flight 328, a Boeing 777-222 (N772UA, manufactured September 1995, 400 seats, maximum takeoff weight 545,000 pounds), with two Pratt & Whitney PW4077 engines rated at 77,000 pounds of thrust each. The right engine was manufactured in 1995 and installed on the aircraft in August 2016; its most recent inspection before the event was a continuing airworthiness inspection on 16 January 2021, with airframe total time 96,814.41 hours (NTSB p.9). The maintenance program was United's FAA-approved engine maintenance program, built on the Pratt & Whitney PW4000 112-inch maintenance planning document (NTSB p.19). Fan blade overhaul was done at the manufacturer's overhaul shop and included fluorescent penetrant inspection, visual inspection and thermal acoustic imaging (TAI) (NTSB p.19).

    What they were doing: The aircraft was climbing at 12,500 feet at about 280 knots, with moderate turbulence forecast between 13,000 and 23,000 feet, and the crew increased thrust to shorten the time in it. Five to seven seconds after the thrust increase, the cockpit voice recorder captured a thump; the flight data recorder showed an uncommanded shutdown of the right engine, followed by a fire warning (NTSB p.6). The engine had accumulated 2,979 cycles and 12,384 hours since overhaul, and 15,262 cycles and 81,768 hours since new (NTSB p.9). The blade that failed was number 19, fractured transversely across the full span at the root platform plane (NTSB p.14). That blade had been through two overhauls, one in 2014 and one in 2016; the April 2016 TAI left two low-amplitude indications on the record (NTSB pp. 19–20). The overhaul interval in force at the time was 6,500 cycles (NTSB p.19). The blade failed at less than half the interval, and the next TAI was scheduled at 6,500 cycles (NTSB p.3). TAI works like this. The blade airfoil is coated with a thermally radiative paint, a transducer at the root vibrates the whole blade, the faying surfaces on either side of a crack rub at high frequency and generate heat, an infrared camera reads the temperature signature at the surface, and software amplifies it for an inspector to interpret (NTSB p.19). Inspectors do meet suspicious indications and have to judge whether a real crack or a benign cause produced them, the benign ones being paint debonding at the surface or sand trapped in the internal cavity, neither of which justifies removing the blade (NTSB pp. 19–20).

    The decision point: ① April 2016, the overhaul shop, the TAI interpretation. The software flagged two low-amplitude indications on the convex side, in the mid-chord flowpath region, and both fell inside the fatigue origin area that later fractured. The inspector classified them as extraneous, judging them to come from camera sensor noise or from loose particles inside the cavity (NTSB p.20). Under nondestructive inspection procedure NDIP 1065, then in force, an indication of that amplitude required one of two actions: strip the coating, re-spray and run a second TAI, or escalate the ambiguous indication to a team review. The report states that "there was no evidence that either of these reviews occurred", and the blade was released for continued service (NTSB p.3, p.20). The interpretation left one word behind, extraneous, and no second opinion (NTSB p.20).

    ② 13 February 2018: another PW4077 on a United 777-222, over the Pacific, released a fan blade in the same way, NTSB case DCA18IA092. NDIP 1065 then went through revisions B, C and D, which added process controls and sharpened the illustrations of acceptable and unacceptable indications (NTSB p.20). The same passage records that after the 2018 event the April 2016 thermal data were pulled for review, that "the reviewers accepted the previous interpretation", and that the blade stayed in service (NTSB p.20).

    ③ The 6,500-cycle overhaul interval itself. The figure comes from the manufacturer's maintenance planning document, applies uniformly to PW4000 112-inch stage 1 fan blades, and was executed through United's maintenance program with FAA approval; NTSB lists the manufacturer's insufficient inspection interval frequency among the contributing causes (NTSB p.4, p.19). After the event the TAI interval went from 6,500 cycles to 1,000, one sixth of what it had been (NTSB p.4).

    What was visible then: The hierarchy of the maintenance program: United's engine maintenance program approved by the FAA, resting on the PW4000 112-inch maintenance planning document, under which a blade continues in service as long as it passes the prescribed inspections (NTSB p.19); this set of blades had been overhauled in 2014 and again in 2016, both at the manufacturer's shop (NTSB p.19). The April 2016 TAI images, and the two low-amplitude indications the software flagged on its own (NTSB p.20). The NDIP 1065 criteria then in force, under which an ambiguous indication is either redone or escalated to a team review (NTSB p.20). The cycle counts for that blade since new and since overhaul, and the 6,500-cycle interval set by the maintenance planning document. The work cards from the 2014 and 2016 overhauls, covering fluorescent penetrant, visual and TAI, with April 2016 the second shop visit at which this set of blades received TAI. The procedure requiring that an indication an inspector cannot clearly interpret be referred, with the images and the blade, to a process engineer (NTSB p.20). Information that became available later: the fracture originated on the internal cavity radius surface about 6.6 inches above the blade root and grew from 0.063 inch to 0.199 inch over about 3,150 cycles of stable low-cycle fatigue; the two low-amplitude indications sat "very close" to that origin, and the report concludes they "most likely" are the images of the initiating fatigue crack (NTSB p.18, p.20). Two manufacturing reasons for the short blade life also only hold after metallurgical analysis: carbon contamination on the cavity surface accounts for about two thirds of the total difference, and a contour discontinuity left by machining for about one third, the latter raising local steady stress by 30% and cutting fatigue life by 50% (NTSB p.4, pp. 18–19).

Primary source: NTSB, Aviation Investigation Final Report DCA21FA085, published 7 September 2023, link at Appendix B av-006.

    Case 6 (Nuclear Power): Tagging With No Verification (condensed)

    nu-002. Unit 3 of the Gravelines nuclear plant, France. The ASNR event notification (October 2026, this book read the page): a tagging error disabled an existing compensatory measure, the affected equipment was unavailable for 7 hours 45 minutes, and the event was rated INES 1. Tagging is one of the most frequent routine actions on a site: performed several times a day, with no consequences for years, its execution carried by custom. From this chapter's angle the decision point is the verification step after the tag is applied. No second pair of eyes checks the correspondence between the tag and the compensatory measure, and the error walks into an undefended interval. Minimum counterfactual: make independent verification mandatory once tagging is complete. Primary source: ASNR, 2026, link at Appendix B nu-002.

    Set the six cases side by side and the common structure appears. None of them lacked a procedure. Spaceflight had a classification clause, chemicals had an SCBA response clause, aviation had established inspection intervals, finance had an alert handling process, nuclear power had a tagging system. What all six lacked is the same thing: a mechanism that forces the procedure and reality to meet. Foam came off, alarms sounded, signals arrived, alerts piled up, tags were hung, and none of those meetings produced a review action, because the entry's status, the alarm's calibration and the signal's handling had already supplied the answer "no further thought needed". Normalization is that "no further thought needed" accumulating item by item in the archive. The signal list and the defenses in the two sections that follow are aimed entirely at that accumulation.

    One more observation belongs at the end of the case set. In all six cases the procedure text was in place before the event, and a paper compliance check would have looked at it and seen nothing. A checklist verifies that a procedure exists. The three-state register verifies that the procedure met reality. Separating those two jobs is the first operational distinction this chapter offers.

    11.4 What Can Be Seen Earliest

    Weak signals, each with the reason usually used to push it aside. The list runs from earliest to latest.

    1. The register of same-class deviations is missing, or every entry in it carries the status "explained". The reason it is pushed aside: "we have already dealt with that."
    2. The number of anomaly reports falls while the number of problems raised at the front line stays the same. Reason: "safety is improving." (A falling report count is more likely to mean the reporters gave up.)
    3. The wording of an inspection standard or an acceptance clause is rewritten from "must" to "in principle". Reason: "for workability."
    4. "We did it this way before" enters meeting minutes for the first time, as a basis for a decision. Reason: "respect for practical experience."
    5. A request to relax a limit, an interval or a threshold comes from the user, and the review is carried out by the user's usual collaborator. Reason: "standards should fit reality."
    6. A newcomer's question about existing practice is answered with a statement of custom rather than a statement of grounds. Reason: "old hands training newcomers is the best training there is."
    7. The same problem has two or more formal explanations in circulation, and they do not cite each other. Reason: "a difference of wording, nothing more."
    8. "No consequence so far" is used as safety evidence for the first time, rather than as a statement that the data are insufficient. Reason: "let the results speak."

    Signal 8 deserves its own paragraph. It marks the fully formed stage. By the time it appears, the mechanism's output is a system of argument with "nothing happened" as its evidence, and any later challenge has to refute that system before it reaches the facts. Most misuse of black swan rhetoric (Chapter 41) grows on this foundation.

    Put the eight signals back against the cases in this chapter and their landing points show. Signal 1, a missing register or one where everything reads "explained", lands on fi-013: the independent test had already written the alert backlog up as a finding, and the entry never moved into a remediation status. Signal 4, "we did it this way before" written into minutes, lands on mi-001: the STS-113 review formally recorded the foam loss flight rationale as safe to fly (CAIB p.126). Signal 5, an application by the user reviewed by a collaborator, lands on av-034: the lubrication and end-play check intervals were extended on the company's application with FAA approval, on a single information source (NTSB AAR-02/01, executive summary p.4). Signal 8, absence of consequence used as safety evidence, lands in two places at once. In mi-001 it is twenty years of foam loss without a catastrophe read as the system working, and the per-flight table at CAIB p.124 is the ledger of that argument. In av-006 it is every normal inspection result read as support for the explanation, once the anomaly had been explained and closed.

    There is an order of use worth writing down. Check the register first, then talk to people. Half the eight signals (1, 3, 5 and 8) can be confirmed from the register alone; the other half need interviews and site observation. Run it the other way round and the interviewee's "explained" narrative arrives first and explains away the fork that was sitting in plain sight on the register. The order of the audit is itself part of the defense.

    Who runs the audit matters too. The best candidates often sit outside the organization chart: internal audit, a third-party assessor, or a newcomer less than six months in post. What those three share is the absence of stock memory about the "explained" archive. They ask the questions that a stock reader has stopped asking. That also explains why the same register gets quieter the older the organization is: every reader has already accepted the "explained" entries. The list of questions a newcomer raises in the first year is usually the best index of what the register actually contains.

    11.5 How to Prevent It

    The four devices share one principle: take the filing action out of the hands of the explanation and give it to the register and the signature.

    Device 1, the three-state deviation register. An entry may carry one of three statuses only: eliminated (with evidence of elimination), remediation by date (with a date and an owner), running at risk (with a risk assessment and a signature). A fourth status, "explained", is prohibited. Explanations may go in the remarks field, but the status must land in one of the three. Device 1 in Chapter 8 is the same register used in an escalation setting.

    Device 2, margin quantified. Any statement that "there is still margin" must be written as a number together with its data source, and the source may not be the consequence-free history itself. Evidence for hypothesis B cannot be used to prove hypothesis A. A margin statement with no numeric source counts as no margin.

    Device 3, the exposure audit. Every running-at-risk item carries two numbers: the current exposure count, and the exposure increment added by each release. The audit has one question. Is the operation of this deviation a finite exposure that we authorized, or an infinite one? This is the register form of the installment-type irreversibility in Chapter 7.

    Device 4, two signatures for any relaxation. Approval to relax a standard, extend an interval or waive an inspection requires two signatures: the head of the requesting party, and a representative of the risk-bearing party who has no interest in the deviation and whose appraisal is not affected by current output. The FAA approval in the Alaska 261 case could not act as independent gatekeeping because its information source was single. The two-signature rule pins the information source down: the material required for approval must include inspection or test records from outside the user.

    The weak signals of normalization share one cross-industry property. None of them needs technical expertise to see. All of them need a register. An auditor with no industry experience who is handed a deviation register can count the share of entries marked "explained" and make a reliable judgment about how far the mechanism has run. Defending against normalization therefore does not require industry experts. It requires an honest register and someone willing to read it.

    The signature requirements across the four devices add up like this. The register is maintained by a safety engineer, not a production engineer. Margin figures are written by the user's engineer and reviewed by the safety engineer. Exposure increment reports go directly to the technical director. Relaxation approvals carry the signature of both the user and the risk-bearing party. What the four roles share is the absence of any interest in current output, and that is the organizational base the whole defense stands on.

    Each device has one most direct application in the cases of this chapter. Device 1 on fi-013: on the day the independent test report arrives, the alert backlog entry must land in one of the three states; on the facts it can only go to "remediation by date", with a date and an owner attached, and the quarterly audit after that looks at one thing only, whether this entry has closed. Device 2 on av-034: a statement in an extension request that lubrication remains adequate must carry a measured jackscrew lubrication condition and measured end play; without the measurement the request returns automatically. Device 3 on av-006: had the 2016 anomaly been registered as "remediation by date", the entry would have rolled into every inspection with a review deadline attached, and the blade that broke in 2021 would have had several chances to be looked at again. Device 4 on mi-001: had the STS-113 review run on two signatures, the second pen signing the safe-to-fly rationale would have had to come from a party with no interest in the launch schedule. CAIB calls that review a "key decision" (p.126), which shows how completely the second pen was missing.

    None of the four devices can be installed once the drift has matured. Registers, numbers, audits and second signatures are slow variables, and they work only if the organization configures them during the consequence-free period. Install them as the consequence approaches and the mechanism has already passed into the routine phase; what the defenses then face is the whole "explained" archive. That is why these items belong in a standing budget rather than in a special remediation project. Slow variables need a standing carrier.

    The failure modes of the four devices are worth naming, because each one has a dignified way to die. The three-state register dies when a "remediation by date" comes due and nobody asks: the entry rolls over, and after a few rolls it is indistinguishable from "explained". Margin quantification dies in the degradation of its source: the first figure is measured, the second is copied from the first, the third is an experienced guess, and nobody notices that the wording in the source column has moved from "measured" to "estimated". The exposure audit dies at the manual step: logging the increment depends on the releaser jotting it down, and when things get busy only the total keeps rising. The two-signature rule dies in the relationalization of the second pen: the risk-bearing representative sits with the user long enough that the independent check before signing shrinks into a courtesy initial. One antidote covers all four. Each quarter, draw one closed entry at random and re-audit it backwards, publish the draw, and put the result on the first page of the next quarterly report. Devices do not stay honest by themselves. Someone has to poke them.

    11.6 Boundaries with Adjacent Mechanisms

    Against practical drift (Chapter 15). Drift describes the fork between practice and rule, which is the question of how the work is done. Normalization describes the fork being redefined as acceptable, which is the question of how it is ruled on. Drift can run without normalization: drift that a register is watching stays in "remediation by date". Normalization usually takes drift as its raw material, but it needs the second step, explain and file. Where both are present, find the fork point first, then find out who filed it.

    Against margin erosion (Chapter 25). Eating into margin is an active reduction, a cut, and it leaves a register to check. Normalization is an interpretive slide, carried out in speech, and it usually leaves no register. A single approval to extend an interval is both a cut and a normalization, and the carrier decides which chapter owns it. What lands on paper belongs to Chapter 25. What lives in the tone of a meeting and in custom belongs here.

    Against narrative before evidence (Chapter 13). Narrative operates after the conclusion is public: commitments made outward, sides taken inward. Normalization operates in the internal archive layer: filing, classification. The two usually appear as a pair, normalization inside first and narrative outside afterwards. The discriminator is audience. An action filed inward belongs to this chapter. An action promised outward belongs to Chapter 13.

    11.7 Five Self-Checks

    1. In your process's deviation register, how many entries carry the status "explained"? Pick one. Is its deviation still running now?
    2. Where were you the last time you said "we have always done it this way"? Was there anything in the other person's objection that deserved a register entry?
    3. Of the relaxations you have signed (intervals, limits, waivers), how many rested on material that included records from outside the user?
    4. In your organization, who can state the current cumulative exposure count for a given running-at-risk item? If nobody can, in whose hands is the exposure accumulating?
    5. Take a customary practice you have run for more than two years with no consequence, and walk it through criteria ①②③. If all three hit, what does this chapter call it?
