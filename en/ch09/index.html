    Chapter 9 The Failure Chain: Three Layers and the Breaks at Their Boundaries

    What this chapter covers: how the technical, organizational and incentive layers are divided, how to recognize the breaks where they meet, and how to use the test that says an event is a failure only when all three layers break, while a single broken layer is a fault. When to turn back: when you are reviewing an accident, reviewing a system's defence-in-depth design, or working out which layer your own improvement measures land on. Estimated reading time: 45 minutes.

    Any failure with weight to it can be taken apart into three layers: what the physics and the system were doing, what the people and the procedures were doing, and what the structure of interest was rewarding. Each layer can break alone. Where the break sits decides what kind of event you have.

    The classification also decides who gets told. A fault, meaning one layer broken, can be handled inside the maintenance department. A failure, meaning several layers broken, has to be escalated to governance. Mix the two paths and governance drowns in faults while the real failures inside the flood get diluted. The criterion for choosing the path is the test in this chapter: two or more layers broken, report it as a failure. Filing a failure as a fault has its own cost. Deliberate downgrading is Chapter 18's subject; unintentional downgrading usually comes from people who cannot layer an event at all, and the criterion below is written for them.

    The walkthrough in section 9.4 carries a precondition. At least four of the nine cells must be fillable with evidence. Below four, the exercise degrades into three-layer guessing, and its output is a collection list rather than an analysis: which evidence columns are empty, what can be recovered, what cannot and should be logged as a checkpoint. Walk the case when collection is done. The threshold comes from Chapter 10's evidence grades, at least two dated pieces at grade B or above. Cases that fall short go into the matrix first, under Chapter 5's downgrade rule.

    Fill the table as a small team. Three people, one from a technical background, one from process, one from management, each filling a copy alone before the copies are merged. A single filler carries a single-layer bias: the technically trained one leaves the organizational row thin, the manager writes the incentive row in the language of intentions. The disagreements at the merge are the most valuable cells. Put them in the notes row instead of resolving them by seniority; disagreement means the organization holds no single model of what happened, and that is a diagnosis.

    One stance before the method. The three layers are an instrument, not an ontology. The claim is not that the world is built in three storeys, but that sorting causes by the currency you would have to pay to remove them gives analysis a handle. A tool is judged by whether it works, meaning whether it locates a place to intervene, not by whether it is true. The stance removes a burden: you need not settle which layer a phenomenon philosophically belongs to. Where attribution is disputed, run the hypothetical intervention both ways and keep whichever reading changes the outcome.

    9.1 How the Three Layers Are Divided

    The dividing line is not the object of study. It is the intervention currency: the variable whose change would eliminate this class of event. Whichever layer holds that variable is the layer the event belongs to.

    Technical layer. The currency is physical: swap a part, change code, add a sensor, move a parameter. Events here are failures of components, materials, algorithms and physical processes. A hydraulic line punched through, thread wear, a battery in thermal runaway, a pitot tube with its intake blocked. The list has a second use: the search radius of your currency is the radius of your authority. A frontline engineer can reach parts and parameters, never assessment schemes or contracts. So write the currency list into the job description, in a column of its own beside the duties. That column feeds Chapter 35's positioning table, whose first parameter reads it.

    Organizational layer. The currency is process and information: change the checklist, change what a handover contains, change the reporting route, change the shape of the meeting, change the training. Events here are information that did not arrive, responsibilities nobody claimed, process forked away from practice, training detached from the real scenario.

    Incentive layer. The currency is contracts and assessment: change the KPI, change the pay structure, change the tenure, change the liability rules. Events here are local rational choices summing into collective loss (Chapter 17's territory), present benefit set against distant cost (Chapter 28), and assessed indicators diverging from the real goal (Chapter 12).

    A technical break needs one further question before you write it down: primary or induced? Primary means an unknown failure mode in a new material or process. Induced means brought on by deferred maintenance, service past life, or a wrong modification. Induced technical failure does not count as an independent technical break on the nine-cell table, because its root sits in the organizational or incentive layer. The evidence is the maintenance record and the usage history; the criterion is Chapter 29's. The distinction sets the level of the fix: for an induced failure you do not change the part, you change the maintenance budget.

    The incentive layer has a break that spans organizations: buyer and supplier incentives pulling apart. The buyer wants cheap and on time, the supplier wants margin and capacity. Both sets lock at contract signature and meet years later as a quality event. The non-OEM bearing in ma-001 is that meeting point, buyer price pressure crossing supplier margin protection. Detection happens at contract review, where you look at the weight ratio between price clauses and quality clauses. A contract with that ratio badly skewed is a break waiting for its year.

    The incentive layer also has a terminal form: an entire industry measured the same way. Pharma runs on sales targets, aviation on on-time rates, construction on schedule. When every firm is scored alike, the break grows in the same place across the industry, and what follows has the character of an industry design rather than a scandal at one firm. Only regulation can break that isomorphism. One firm changing its assessment alone buys a competitive disadvantage; the first to change is the first to die. A regulator forcing the change industry-wide is the executable route, whether that means legislating hours-of-service limits or retiring on-time-rate scoring. The argument for such a move is the break-point register: this industry's break cases in a list, which is page one of any lobbying document.

    There is a fourth currency, less visible: the default option. The incentive layer is strongest when it changes a default without touching a single assessment. The scoring stays, the submission workflow opens on a different screen, and behavior follows the default. Governing defaults is behavioral economics inside an organization, and it is cheaper than rewriting an assessment, so pick a default that moves no currency at all. These moves appear in Chapter 12's register of settings, because a change of default must be logged like any other: which default changed, who benefits, who will not notice.

    Before using the criterion, guard against its commonest misuse. The three layers are not a ranking of blame. A technical break is not the shallow one and an incentive break is not the deep one; the layers classify currencies, they do not rank importance. The misuse comes from reading the model as an onion, the outer layers assumed more fundamental. That reading drags every postmortem toward the incentive layer, because digging always arrives there, and it teaches the organization to despise the technical fix in front of it, which is also part of learning. Read the layers as parallel. All three are candidate roots at once.

    The test is the hypothetical intervention, run layer by layer. "If we fitted a better sensor, would this still have happened?" Yes means the sensor is not the root layer. "If we changed the handover process?" "If we took the downtime loss out of the department's assessment?" Keep asking down the layers. The one that answers "it would not have happened" is the event's main resident layer. Most major events answer "it would not have happened" at more than one layer, which is the next section's subject.

    An event may be tagged to more than one layer, but the tags need rank. The main layer is the one a hypothetical intervention eliminates; the secondary layer is the one that deepened the consequences. Write the ranks in the table header. The standard for ranking is intervention sensitivity: run the intervention on each layer, order by effect size, take the strongest as main. The output is intervention priority, because budget and attention go to the main layer first. Pick it wrong and the secondary repairs, however well executed, only postpone one failure by one season.

    The walkthrough is finished when one judgement sentence exists: which layer is this case's main resident layer, and in which cell is the anchor. Write it on the last row. That sentence feeds two downstream users, the improvement list ordered by resident layer, and the case card, which fills field 11 from the anchor cell. Its presence or absence is the only completion test. A walkthrough without it is a table with boxes ticked.

    Keep a scoring habit alongside. Build the hypothetical-intervention version of the table: in each cell, a line predicting what the intervention would change. Write predictions before the fix and results after it. Prediction against outcome is the raw material of an intervention knowledge base, and it pays off in Chapter 39, where the rehearsal scenario library needs calibration data from historical predictions. No prediction made in advance, nothing to calibrate. Predictions must carry a date stamp: only one written before the fact has standing to be compared against it afterwards. One written in afterwards is narrative, not prediction, and Chapter 6's discipline applies.

    9.2 One Broken Layer Is a Fault

    An event becomes a failure when all three layers break. When one layer breaks and the others catch it, the event is a fault. This is the operational core of the chapter.

    Take the technical layer broken alone, with the organizational layer in place. In place has a standard: the defensive action actually ran. The checklist was signed, the backup actually switched, the training actually happened. Presence on paper does not count, and Chapter 22's real-rehearsal standard governs. The judgement that a technical layer broke alone presupposes a gate verified by rehearsal, because unverified presence is statistically indistinguishable from absence, which is why near misses must separate what the design caught from what luck stopped. If redundancy, checklists, backups and training were genuinely there, the event becomes a near miss: the part failed and the system caught it. Caught events do not enter accident statistics. They enter the maintenance log.

    Near misses deserve rescuing for a second reason: they are the free teaching material of the model. A fault that got caught proves some gate worked. Written up as a case, it gives the model's abstract criteria a concrete face. Production cost is trivial, one maintenance log plus one interview, and the return is high. A newcomer learns inter-layer cooperation more completely from a successful interception than from a failure, since a failure case shows only the layers that were penetrated while a success case shows all of them acting. The production system is Chapter 20's capture ledger, the same mechanism under another name.

    Now break the organizational layer alone: one handover omission, one checklist item skipped. Technical redundancy is still in place and the consequence is usually absorbed as well.

    Absorption has another name, and it is luck. An absorbed organizational break is usually stopped by the incidental conditions of the day: someone happened to look twice, the day happened not to be busy. Luck absorption and design absorption must be counted separately, and Chapter 20's ledger keeps them apart. What the design caught is a credit to the defence; what luck stopped is an IOU written by the defence, and the list of IOUs feeds Chapter 25's margin ledger. The distinction matters for risk measurement, because an event stopped by luck will not keep being stopped under repeated exposure. An organization that counts luck as defence has a margin ledger reading zero.

    Break the incentive layer alone, with nothing yet broken technically or in process, and the consequence arrives as chronic attrition: maintenance deferred, skills draining. Years pass before it meets the technical layer.

    That rendezvous has warning value, because attrition is monitorable. Three quantities will do: the balance of deferred maintenance, training hours as a share of total hours, and the duration of vacancies in key posts. Where those curves cross the technical accident rate, you have the precursor. The two or three years before the crossing is the last window for intervention. Put the monitoring on the governance dashboard beside Chapter 25's defence concentration, and when both indicators appear in the same year, treat it as a yellow alert.

    In aviation's language this structure is defence in depth, and the academic statement is the Swiss cheese model: each layer of defence is a slice with holes, holes are normal, and an accident is the rare penetration where the holes line up. The formulation is James Reason's, in Human Error (Cambridge University Press, 1990; the Crossref record has been checked). The arithmetic deserves a sentence. Any single layer fails with low probability, and several failing at once is a product, which is why failure is rare. But the layers do not fail independently. Organizational and incentive failures move to align with the technical holes: cut maintenance and the technical slice gains holes, raise assessment pressure and the organizational layer stops watching for anomalies. That active alignment is what Part III's twenty-four mechanism chapters describe.

    The holes can be managed instead of awaited. Waiting for alignment is fatalism; active management means a census per layer, a written list of that layer's known blind spots, and cross-comparison of the lists as a forecast of future failures. A frame for the forecast: rows are combinations of three-layer blind spots (technical A × organizational B × incentive C), columns are similarity to real cases (has a close combination happened before), and similar cases link into Appendix B. The frame turns forecasting from intuition into a lookup table. Hit rates will be imperfect, and responsibility still shifts from genius to method; method can be taught, copied and audited. The tools are already distributed: Chapter 22 for real rehearsal of redundancy, Chapter 20 for the capture ledger, Chapter 19 for dissent records; the annual joint inspection of the three is the census. Its output is a hole-alignment risk table, one row per potential penetration with a management action attached. Update it twice a year, because holes are dynamic: new equipment, new processes and new faces all cut new holes.

    The direction of alignment fits one line: holes in the lower layer are made by disease in the upper. Maintenance debt drills holes, training cuts drill holes, configuration decisions drill holes. Holes in the upper layer are mostly absorbed below: the process gate catches technical badness, the redundancy gate catches process leakage. The governing implication is to repair from the top down. When a technical hole census turns up a problem, the repair usually lands in the organizational or incentive layer, and charging the hole itself treats the symptom. The nine-cell time-span column shows the year each hole was drilled, an incentive decision ten years back against a technical hole last month, which makes the drilling relation visible.

    The three-layer model also gives the mechanism chapters their coordinate system. Part II's tools work inside a layer and across a boundary; Part III's mechanisms explain how the layers pull each other off true.

    9.3 Three Kinds of Break at the Boundaries

    Boundaries between layers are where breaks concentrate, because a boundary needs a translation act and translation has no job title.

    The prescription follows from the diagnosis. Create the translator post at the boundary: between the technical layer's anomaly report and the organizational layer's risk register, between the organizational layer's process change and the incentive layer's assessment adjustment. The post need not be full time. An existing quality or safety role can hold it concurrently, provided the job description states the translation duty in writing, including which boundary information must pass through that desk. Once written down, translation stops being something a conscientious individual volunteers and becomes something the organization runs.

    The workload of the post can be estimated. Count the boundaries: after a nine-cell walkthrough, count the cross-cell links, technical to organizational, organizational to incentive. The number of links measures translator workload, and the denser the cross-layer collaboration the more necessary the post. Compare year on year, and you learn whether the density is natural to the organization or manufactured by management. Density is not a disease. Density without translation is.

    The mechanism is one sentence. Every cross-cell link is an information interchange point, and with no translator there information arrives in the sender's language. The receiver, unable to parse it, processes it by his own understanding, and the probability of distortion multiplies with each transfer. After three transfers, fidelity is under fifty percent. That is why dense cross-layer work needs translators more, not less.

    Each break gets a shape.

    Break one: a technical signal the organizational layer never translated. The front line sees an anomaly, but the recording and reporting system holds no category word that can receive it. The information passes through in the form "not an anomaly".

    Category words also fail by being too coarse. The register exists, but the category is a generic "equipment anomaly" and everything goes into it. Coarse categories stop like signals from aggregating, and a signal that never aggregates never becomes a pattern. Design fine categories by failure mechanism, not by equipment, since equipment is replaced and mechanisms persist. The list of mechanism categories is Part III's twenty-four-chapter vocabulary, and aligning the category table with it is the translation bridge between recording and analysis. Chapter 20's capture ledger already files by mechanism.

    The 737-9 door plug (av-030) gives the shape concretely. That door had been opened for work during final assembly, and the form the fact took on the process record was "no reinstallation record". The absence of a record is a technical-layer fact. The release chain's organizational layer never translated "no record on a critical area" into the trigger "stop and verify by hand", so the information passed through the inspection in compliant form.

    That compliant passage is open to any checklist-driven process. A checklist asks about clauses, and an anomaly no clause mentions walks through as compliant. The defence is scenario-based clauses, and Chapter 32's scenario-driven inspection is the specific remedy for break one. The division of labour: this chapter diagnoses that information passed through in compliant form, Chapter 32 prescribes by putting the scenario into the inspection system. Diagnosis in this part, prescription in that one, is the book's arrangement.

    The Atlas Air 747 freighter at Narita (av-007, JTSB report, 2025) supplies a second instance. The abnormal state of the tires during taxi was a technical-layer signal. The crew's felt sense of "running over something" never met an organizational handling procedure, meaning a procedure stating which taxi-phase anomalies require a stop, and the signal went into the air with the aircraft. The JTSB report records it at page 2: the crew perceived a vibration "like running over something" but heard no abnormal noise, and decided to continue the taxi (checked).

    Break two: an organizational requirement in conflict with an incentive assessment. The process says A, the assessment rewards B, and behavior follows B for years. On release and dispatch desks the tension between on-time rate and safety margin is the standard sample: the process says do not release a doubtful aircraft, the assessment says a delayed flight has a cost. You need no site visit to identify this break. Read two documents, the process file and the assessment scheme, and put them side by side. Chapter 17 gives cases from four industries.

    Read specific clauses, not summaries. Summary language is uniform everywhere ("safety first"), and the conflict lives at clause level, in the clause stating release conditions and the line of the assessment that deducts for delay. Only clause-level reading reaches the numbers and the trigger conditions. The output is a conflict register: rows are conflicting clause pairs, process clause against assessment item, columns are the behavioral consequence, meaning what the employee does when condition X arises. The register goes to governance, because only the incentive layer has authority to resolve it. A frontline employee cannot rewrite an assessment.

    Break three: the incentive layer shaping the technical layer's selection. Configuration decisions are made under incentive constraint and repaid years later as technical failure. In the same Atlas 747 event, the poor visibility of the tires from inside the cabin removed the information channel that could have turned a taxi anomaly into a reason to stop. Set that against me-002, where safety needles left the supply catalogue and changed the boundary of clinical practice. Supply and configuration decisions are made under incentive constraint and repaid years later as failure. The technical layer's "invisibility" is not a law of physics. It is the product of one configuration meeting years earlier, and the option list at that meeting had the price column in a prominent place. This break has the longest lookback and is the one postmortems drop most often. The accountability report stops at the organizational layer, but the ledger of the configuration decision sits in the incentive layer.

    Monitoring can be made visual. Draw the three-layer timeline: years across, three swimlanes for the density of technical events, the density of organizational changes, and the incentive budget curve. A year where the three converge is a candidate for break resonance, and the list of such years goes to governance, because each deserves a nine-cell walkthrough in retrospect. All the data comes from ledgers you already keep, maintenance logs, change orders, budget tables. The chart costs half a day and carries more insight than ten special meetings.

    Incentive lookback needs a method of its own. Configuration archaeology runs on year spans, since break three's lookback is often measured in decades: the configuration meeting, the material selection, the supplier contract from fifteen years ago. How deep you dig depends on how much archive survives. Are the minutes and the option lists still there? Where they are not, the substitute is comparison with contemporaneous projects of the same kind: what did others select? The comparison restores the sense of what the decision's boundaries were at the time. The output goes into the incentive row, with the evidence column stating the archive's condition and the method of comparison. Mark the limits of the archaeology honestly.

    9.4 The Walkthrough: Fitting a Case into Three Layers

    Here is the reusable checklist. For any case, answer three questions per layer, nine cells in all:

    Where this layer broke in the event Dated evidence for the break Corresponding intervention
    Technical layer The chain of physical and system behavior Physical evidence, logs, data (the fact sections of the investigation report) Replace parts, add sensors, change the design
    Organizational layer Information flow and the structure of responsibility Meetings, work orders, handover records, training records Change the process, change the checkpoint, change what the handover contains
    Incentive layer Contract and assessment structure Assessment schemes, contract clauses, pricing and configuration decisions Change the assessment, change the contract, change the payment structure

    The easiest column is the first, because everyone can tell a story. The one most often missing is the second, the dated evidence.

    Column two has a width rule. Dated evidence need not be perfect: a frontline original record, an email from the time, a photograph of the duty log will do. Where the date is fuzzy, state the fuzziness, "roughly that week". Honest width beats precise fabrication, because a fabricated date collapses under cross-verification, and one collapse takes the credibility of the whole walkthrough to zero. An empty cell may not be filled with a story. It may hold "no record", and under Chapter 6's discipline "no record" is a diagnostic result.

    Complex cases should use the extended version, with a column for time span: the year and month each layer's break formed. Technical breaks usually form days before the event, organizational breaks months before, incentive breaks years before. The column makes the layers' temporal depth visible, and once visible the timescale of the improvement follows: an incentive repair is measured in years, so do not expect quarterly results. Fill it from the dates of each layer's evidence, the manufacture date of the physical item, the date on the minutes, the signature date of the contract. Spans are inferred, so write the basis.

    A worked example: Alaska Airlines 261 (av-034, NTSB AAR-02/01, executive summary checked). Technical layer: the Acme nut threads in the horizontal stabilizer jackscrew assembly were excessively worn, the assembly failed in flight, the aircraft lost pitch control and went into the sea, and all 88 people on board died, in the report's own wording. Organizational layer: the company extended the lubrication interval and the end-play check interval for that assembly, the FAA approved the extension, and the investigation found that the extension let inadequate lubrication develop into failure-level wear. Incentive layer: the report's conclusions place the maintenance program and regulatory oversight on the list of issues. The specific economic trade-off behind the extension, downtime cost and scheduling pressure, is discussed but not quantified to a citable degree, so this cell is marked "insufficient evidence; reconstruction requires the maintenance engineering record". No story is inserted.

    The discipline of not inserting stories meets a psychological obstacle: the social pressure to fill the blank. In a review meeting an empty cell invites the question of why you did not find out, and the correct answer is to display the search process: which archives you checked, whom you asked, what came back. Displaying the process turns the blank from an embarrassment into proof of work. Auditing calls this the trace, and the trace of the search matters as much as the trace of the conclusion.

    The last intervenable position in this case falls on the organizational layer, at the node where the extension was approved. That is where field 11 of Chapter 5, the minimum counterfactual, takes its value. The two routes meet at that node, which tells you something about the book's architecture: the twelve-field card and the three-layer walkthrough are two tables for one object.

    One detail of the join matters. A counterfactual action must be tagged with its layer, which layer it falls on and which currency it moves. A tagged counterfactual enters the improvement list for that layer. An untagged one is an orphan: after the review meeting it has nowhere to live and the list will not take it in. One line of tagging saves one lesson.

    Where the incentive row is thin, hand it to Chapter 17's principal-agent framework. The row usually gets no further than assessments and contracts, and the framework asks one level down: who is the principal, whose objective is the agent optimizing, where is the monitoring cost spent. The deeper look often rewrites the row, because what read as an assessment problem turns out to be an absent principal. Not required every time: clear contract evidence closes the case at nine cells, and the framework is for rows where evidence is vague.

    Merge the two tables and you have the script for Chapter 39's rehearsal. The nine cells fix the scenario, the twelve-field card lays out the material. The join is a mapping: field 8, the decision point, corresponds to the organizational row; field 9 to the time-span column; field 6 to the evidence column. The mapping table sits in the user notes to Appendix A. Because the mapping exists, a reader who learns either table gets the other for free. The two are one instrument, one cut from the training angle, one from the structural angle.

    9.5 What "All Three Broken" Means for Three Kinds of Reader

    For the person writing the postmortem. A postmortem that stops at the technical layer is a fault report.

    When the walkthrough is done, read the nine-cell table beside the body of the postmortem and work out which cells the narrative paragraphs correspond to. The cells the text never touches are the blind spots, and the list goes back to the author as material for the next version, without reproach. The procedure also runs in reverse: walk first, then write, with the table as the outline. Such an outline covers the three layers automatically and carries the evidence column with it.

    Whether your conclusion crossed the technical layer takes one question: is the subject of the conclusion sentence a part or a structure? "Nut wear caused the crash" has a part as its subject, so it is a fault report. "The approval of the extended inspection interval let wear develop into failure" has a structure as its subject, and only now are you approaching failure. The upgraded form is the subject-chain test. Write the causal chain in full: who approved what in which year, letting whose work under what conditions develop into what result. Written in full, every link is an intervenable position and the list of links is a rich seam of measures. Chain length is postmortem depth: three links or fewer is shallow, five or more begins to touch the incentive layer.

    For the person designing defences. Leave one gate on each layer.

    Acceptance of the three gates should be done together. The technical gate is accepted by real rehearsal, the organizational gate by a drill, the incentive gate by a case replay. The institutional carrier is Chapter 22's annual real rehearsal plus this chapter's walkthrough. Cost is amortized by the combination and completeness rises, because single-gate acceptance cannot see problems of mutual exclusion and three gates in one room expose them.

    The point of three-layer defence is independence. The organizational gate, meaning a mandatory re-check, must not take its signal from the technical gate, meaning a sensor. The incentive gate, meaning no penalty for calling a stop, must guarantee that the organizational gate dares to be used. Chapter 22 carries a case where all three gates were shared.

    One failure mode of the combination deserves flagging: gates that exclude each other. When the incentive gate, no penalty for stopping, fights the organizational assessment of schedule bonuses and penalties, the organizational gate prefers not to be used, because the person who uses it loses the schedule bonus. Detection is the two-document reading of break two in section 9.3. Once detected, the repair is in the incentive layer: put gate use into positive scoring, count uses as a plus and not a minus. That reversal is the decisive switch for whether three gates work together at all.

    For the person deciding on improvement measures. Which layer you can move decides which chapters of Part III you read.

    Lay the measure list out in three columns, incentive, organizational, technical. The columns force every measure to show its currency, and a measure that fits none, "pay more attention" or "strengthen management", is deleted on the spot. The layout costs a table with two extra columns and its filter value shows on first use: in most organizations the first version of the list has an empty incentive column, and the empty column is itself a finding.

    Frontline engineers can move the technical and organizational layers (Chapters 15, 21, 30). Middle managers can move the organizational and incentive layers (Chapters 16, 17, 18). Only those who can change contracts and assessments can move the incentive layer (Chapters 12, 17, 28). An improvement plan cannot exceed your intervention currency. Work out which currency you hold before writing the plan; this is the first parameter of Chapter 35's positioning table.

    The second parameter is layer span, meaning how many layers you can coordinate across. The frontline acts within its own layer, the middle manager crosses two, the executive moves all three. An honest assessment of span sets the size of the plan. A middle manager who cannot cross into the incentive layer should not write incentive actions into the plan, or it will stay on paper. The correct way to cross is to find a bridge, an ally at executive level, and Chapter 35 describes how to choose one.

    Recognizing your currency also applies at organization level: tiered sign-off on the improvement list. The list sits in three columns by currency, and each has a different signer. An engineer signs the technical column, a manager the organizational, and whoever holds assessment authority signs the incentive column. Every measure then carries a signature from someone able to execute it. A measure with no corresponding signer is deleted or transferred to someone with authority. Ownerless measures are the dead stock of an improvement list.

    Self-Check: Five Questions

    1. Run the nine-cell walkthrough of section 9.4 on your organization's most recent significant loss. Which cells cannot be filled with dated evidence?
    2. In your organization's latest improvement list, how many measures fall on the incentive layer? If none do, how many years will the technical repairs hold?
    3. Find one concrete place in your processes where a technical signal has no organizational category word: what anomaly can occur for which the system holds no registration category?
    4. What is your post's intervention currency? Can you change a process, change an assessment, or only change a parameter? The answer decides which chapters of Part III you need.
    5. In your organization's postmortem conclusions, is the subject usually a part or a structure? Who trained that habit?

    Beyond the five questions, here is the end-of-part test for the anatomy section. Take a real investigation report from your own industry at grade A and complete the full cycle from zero: draw the failure chain, mark decision points and points of no return, fill the nine-cell table, judge reversibility, walk the three signal words, write the minimum counterfactual, assign the evidence grade. The first run takes about a day, half a day with practice, and the output is one complete case card, your first grade-A card, at once the graduation certificate of Part II and the entry ticket to Part III.

    One reminder above the model. The three layers are an analytical framework, not an organization chart. Departments such as engineering, operations and HR do not map cleanly onto the layers. HR is not the incentive layer. The incentive currencies, assessments, contracts and pricing, are scattered across several departments. Fill the table by currency ownership, not by department ownership, and ask what change would eliminate this event, never whose department it is.
