    Chapter 36 Thirty Minutes Before the Decision

    What this chapter covers: the full run from the position table to a conclusion page reading "these are the three deaths I most resemble", in a time box of 5 + 15 + 10 minutes, with a walkthrough of a synthetic example end to end. When to turn back: before any decision that touches a point of no return (Chapter 7); and when you want other people to use this procedure and need to hand them a minimum version. Estimated reading time: 30 minutes.

    36.1 The Procedure at a Glance

    The thirty-minute procedure is the executable form of the position table (Chapter 35) and the master gate for all the mechanism material in this book. It does one thing. Before a point of no return, it converts "which kinds of death do I most resemble" from diffuse anxiety into three specific mechanism names and three corresponding checking actions.

    The compressed version for emergencies is worth giving up front as well. Each of the three time boxes shrinks to a third: two minutes filling the table, with only two parameters, incentives and time; five minutes in the library, checking only the first criterion of the first mechanism that hits; three minutes writing the conclusion, with only the likeliest death and one action. The ten-minute version produces one death and one action instead of three, and it is a hundred times better than zero minutes. Its precondition is a real emergency, a decision window measured in hours. If your window is measured in days, run the full thirty. A sense of emergency is not an emergency: often it is the amplifier from Chapter 13, narrative before evidence. The more you feel there is no time, the more you need the procedure.

    One numerical coincidence in the design is worth naming. Five plus fifteen plus ten is thirty, and thirty minutes is roughly the limit at which attention stays at high quality on a single task. Beyond thirty, fatigue degrades the judgment you bring to the library search. Short of thirty, the table and the search lack depth. Thirty minutes is the optimum between cognitive load and judgment quality. Nobody designed the number that way; it was noticed afterwards, when the finished procedure was looked at as a whole and turned out to sit on the sweet spot of cognitive science. That is why it should not be compressed to fifteen, where quality fails, nor stretched to sixty, where fatigue cancels the gain. Thirty minutes is thirty minutes: physics, not a preference.

    The three time boxes:

    Stage Time Action Output
    Fill the table 5 minutes Six-parameter positioning (Chapter 35, table 35.5) 6 grades with evidence
    Search the library 15 minutes Against the hit mechanism chapters: criteria, weak signals, comparison cases Candidates narrowed to 2–3 mechanisms
    Write the conclusion 10 minutes Three-paragraph conclusion page One page: three deaths, three checking actions, check person

    The threshold for using the procedure: the decision touches a point of no return (signature, go-live, announcement, external commitment), or the commitment is large enough that withdrawal would draw on second-tier resources. Ordinary decisions below that threshold do not need the full run. Copy the format of the conclusion page instead, three deaths plus checking actions, and use it in a five-minute spoken version.

    Timing has its own rules. Run the procedure before the decision point, and leave at least one overnight gap (Chapter 7, the cooling-off period). Run it after the meeting and the output is postmortem material. Run it before and the output is armour.

    There is a deeper reason for that difference. The person who runs the procedure before the meeting is still inside the information fog, and the discipline of Chapter 6 comes naturally: you have no outcome to lean on, so judgment is forced to start from what was visible. Run it afterwards and all three disciplines fail. You already know the outcome, and creeping determinism paves every fork in the road into a highway. So the thirty-minute procedure has one legal timing: before the decision. What happens afterwards is a postmortem (Chapter 38), not a thirty-minute procedure. The two have different purposes, and armour and teaching material should not be packed in the same box.

    Ten minutes of preparation come before the clock starts. They do not count toward the thirty, but without them the procedure idles. Four items.

    Materials: the numbers the decision turns on, budget, schedule, dependencies, and the record of comparable past decisions, on one page at most. The thirty minutes will not digest a fifty-page feasibility report. It needs the conclusions and numbers you have already digested. If materials are missing, the gap is handled under the Chapter 35 evidence checklist: whichever class of number is missing, that parameter gets the most dangerous grade.

    Room: a table you can write on and a stretch of time nobody interrupts. The three time boxes do not tolerate handling messages at the same time. Phone silent, and the thirty minutes marked on the calendar as non-displaceable. Displacement is the most common way this procedure dies. It looks postponable at any moment, and after the third postponement the decision point has arrived.

    Role: confirm the candidate check person, against the standard in 36.4. Asking in advance works better than asking afterwards, because advance notice leaves the other person room to decline or to name someone better. Asked afterwards, only obligation remains.

    Entrance: write one sentence answering "which point of no return does this decision touch", using the Chapter 7 test: signature, go-live, announcement, external commitment, or withdrawal drawing on second-tier resources. The sentence goes at the top of the table. A decision whose point of no return you cannot name usually fails the threshold; take the spoken version and leave it there.

    Two patches for the compressed version. The first: compression applies to time boxes only, and the check person is never dropped. The ten-minute output of one death and one action still carries a name and a time; a compressed page without a checker is self-comfort. The second: when a decision run in compressed form came out fine, give it a full thirty minutes later. Walk the judgment again with time to spare, and put the difference into the team's card library, meaning the mechanisms the full run caught that the compressed run missed. Compression is an emergency posture. Routine compression is routine myopia.

    36.2 The First Five Minutes: Filling the Table

    Fill the six grades on the printable version at Chapter 35, 35.5. The discipline has one line: every grade carries evidence, and a grade with no evidence is filled at the most dangerous setting. A parameter you cannot write evidence for inside five minutes is exactly where your information is darkest, and that fact is worth recording. Section 36.6 comes back to it.

    The common error is turning the table into a statement of position. For "incentives: aligned", the evidence cannot be "our team has a good atmosphere". It has to point at a specific appraisal clause, and the test of that clause is whether the role that creates the risk bears the consequence. No clause, and the starting grade is partial misalignment.

    36.3 The Middle Fifteen Minutes: Searching the Library

    Take the 3–6 mechanisms, by chapter number, that the 35.3 table hit. Run three fixed actions on each, at a budget of 2–3 minutes per mechanism. The actions can be that fast because every chapter in Part Three has the same skeleton, in the same positions (writing standard, section 10).

    Action one: check the activation criteria (section 1 of the chapter). Each mechanism chapter gives three criteria of the form "present means already started". Ask of each one: is there identifiable evidence of this in my situation? All three absent, and the mechanism leaves the main list.

    Action two: scan the weak-signal list (section 4). Each chapter carries 6–10 weak signals, and each signal comes with the reason it was pressed down at the time. Any hit gets marked. Two hits or more, and the mechanism moves to the head of the main list.

    Action three: read one comparison case (section 3 plus Appendix B). Read only the case card's decision-point paragraph and its minimum counterfactual paragraph, not the whole account. The purpose is to give the candidate mechanism a concrete face, so that when you discard it you are not discarding something you simply could not picture happening.

    At fifteen minutes you should be holding a main list of 2–3 mechanisms, each with criterion evidence, 1–3 watch items where there is suspicion but no evidence, and the names of the mechanisms you moved off. Removal leaves a trace as well. Three months on, at the postmortem, the judgment "this one did not look like us" is itself up for examination.

    36.4 The Last Ten Minutes: Writing the Conclusion Page

    Three fixed paragraphs, one page.

    Paragraph one: the three deaths I most resemble. One line each: mechanism name with chapter number, one sentence describing the situation, and the criterion evidence that hit. A worked example: "Practical drift (Chapter 15): the spot-check procedure went live three months ago and runs at 2% while the rule says 5%, and nobody has the authority to change the difference (criterion 1 hit)."

    Paragraph two: one checking action per death. The action must be completable before the decision point and it must leave an artifact. Example: "Before go-live, pull the actual execution rate of spot checks against the written value; if the gap exceeds half, the switch does not ship." One action per death, the same discipline as the minimum counterfactual. Three actions equal zero actions.

    Paragraph three: check person and time. State who verifies paragraph two's output, and at what hour.

    For the artifact, use a sticky note or a message rather than a formal document. The ceremony of a formal document is what makes people postpone writing the page. The note has three lines: three deaths, three actions, one check person with a time. It goes on the desk or is pinned in the project tool. A visible conclusion page is ten times more useful than one filed in a folder, and the Chapter 30 register works on the same principle: visibility is a tool's oxygen. A conclusion page without a check person is a diary, not a tool.

    The check person is a key design element of this thirty-minute choreography and deserves its own explanation. The role does three things. It is a time lock: the action you wrote has to appear in the checker's calendar, or it stays words. It supplies an outside view: the checker sits outside your decision chain, so he reads your artifact without your bias. It is an escalation channel: if he finds the action undone or the result abnormal, he has the right to report it upward, a miniature of the stop authority in Chapter 18. The selection standard: no stake in the decision, time to look, and knows what abnormal looks like. People who meet all three are easier to find than you expect. A quality person on another team, a PM on the neighbouring project, or your own manager, as long as he will spend five minutes.

    Fifteen minutes of library search only runs if the stock has been arranged beforehand. Three speed tricks.

    Trick one: criterion cards. Make one card for each mechanism chapter you have hit, three activation criteria on the front and eight weak signals on the back. The source is a transcription of sections 1 and 4, and making the card is itself a close reading. After a dozen of your frequently used mechanisms, the first two actions become flipping cards instead of flipping the book.

    Trick two: pre-marking. In the two or three chapters you use most, highlight the comparison case's decision-point paragraph and its minimum counterfactual paragraph. Action three then changes from finding the paragraph to reading the mark.

    Trick three: a fixed format for the trace. Record every search on the same line: mechanism, criterion entries hit, weak-signal entries hit, reason for removal. A fixed format is what lets a postmortem three months later compare the judgment then against the facts now.

    36.5 Walkthrough of a Synthetic Example

    The following scene is a teaching composite, not a real case, and exists to show how the three time boxes join.

    Scene: a payments team decides to replace manual line-by-line review of refunds with automatic approval plus 5% spot checks. Decision point: the act of flipping the configuration switch. Point of no return: automatic approval takes effect and users begin receiving refunds nobody reviewed.

    Filling the table, 5 minutes. Coupling: medium, since a wrong refund hits a single transaction and the support queue, and a payment can be stopped by hand but volume would drown the staff. Realness of redundancy: low, since the automatic rule's backstop is a manual console on the same code, with no independent check line. Incentives: partly misaligned, since support hours are this group's appraisal item while the loss is carried by risk. Time pressure: short, since it must ship before the promotion. Scale: multi-team, with payments, risk and support involved. Regulatory density: dense, since payments is subject to regulatory examination and rule changes must leave a trace. Every grade has identifiable evidence.

    Searching the library, 15 minutes. The 35.3 table hits four rows: 15, practical drift, time short plus rule against workload; 11, normalization of deviance, a spot-check ratio that is nominal and a consequence that arrives late; 12, goal displacement, the hours metric outweighing the loss; 26, automation and skill decay, manual review skill going unused. Action one, criteria: Chapter 12 criterion 3, "somebody has been penalized for pointing this out", has no evidence yet, so the mechanism drops to a watch item. Chapter 15 criterion 1, "the same deviation twice or more", hits, since last month brought two cases of "spot-check ratio temporarily lowered during peak" with no change process. Chapter 11 criterion 2 hits, since the per-transaction loss is small and has always been explained as contained. Action two, weak signals: Chapter 15 hits two, training teaching something different from the manual, and new hires learning from the old hands. Action three, a case: the practical-drift comparison case confirms the shape, a fork that came from a rule written and not followed.

    Writing the conclusion page, 10 minutes. The three deaths are practical drift (15), normalization of deviance (11), and automation and skill decay (26, promoted from watch item, on the argument that manual review skill decays as it goes unused and six months from now nobody may be able to run the rollback switch by hand). Actions: (1) before go-live, pull the actual spot-check execution rate for the last three months and compare it with the written value; if the gap exceeds half, do not ship; (2) add an independent check line beside the automatic rule, keeping refunds above a threshold on manual review, drawn from a heterogeneous source; (3) keep one manual review drill a month and write proficiency into the shift handover. Check person: the risk interface verifies (1) at the go-live review, and the duty manager verifies (3) monthly.

    The most valuable part of this page is action 2. It comes from none of the three mechanism texts. It surfaced structurally once the three mechanisms had been located, as the obvious repair: put back the independent redundancy that is missing. What the procedure produces is three concrete actions with named checkers, and the vocabulary is a side effect.

    Each of the three paragraphs also has a sample for a different class of decision. Copy the format.

    Infrastructure sample, data-centre migration. Death one, hidden coupling (Chapter 21): the migration list carries no dependency on the old backup system, and the criterion "nobody can state the full blast radius" hits. Action: run a full dependency scan before migration and compare it against the oral interviews. Death two, the illusion of redundancy (Chapter 22): the standby hall and the primary hall sit with the same carrier, and the criterion "same-source backup" hits. Action: run the switchover drill once under conditions where the standby hall is actually carrying production traffic. Death three, handover rupture (Chapter 30): the migration window crosses a quarter boundary and the on-call roster changes over it. Action: the handover page of the migration manual gets a signed cross-check before the changeover. Check person: the platform lead verifies all three at the migration review.

    Hiring sample, a key post. Death one, where intuitive expertise breaks down (Chapter 14): the interviewer's judgment comes from a highly regular interview environment. Action: add a work-sample test built on the same structure as the job's real tasks. Death two, narrative before evidence (Chapter 13): the requisition is already announced externally and the candidate profile was publicly committed at announcement. Action: hand the evidence list behind the hiring standard to a third-party colleague for review before the final round. Check person: the HR interface verifies before the offer goes out.

    Contract sample, a supplier agreement. Death one, horizon mismatch (Chapter 28): the term runs five years while the counterparty's financial health moves on a quarterly cycle. Action: add an annual financial-health review clause and an exit clause. Death two, crossing a scale threshold (Chapter 24): the single supplier's share will pass four in ten. Action: prequalification of a second supplier starts in the same period as signing. Check person: legal verifies before the seal is applied.

    What the three samples share: every death points at a criterion, every action has an artifact and a time of verification, and the whole thing fits on one page.

    With the walkthrough finished, the three boxes deserve a commentary each, because the example hides design intentions that are easy to read past.

    On the filling box. The heaviest grade among the six is realness of redundancy at low. Look at how the evidence is written: the backstop is a manual console on the same code, with no independent check line. That one sentence does two jobs. It evidences the grade, and it already implies the repair, an independent check line from a heterogeneous source. Good evidence carries its own repair clue. Weak evidence, "the backstop feels shaky", is a record of mood.

    On the library box. Chapter 12 was demoted to a watch item because criterion 3 had no evidence. The move is worth enlarging. A mechanism with incomplete criteria can still be a real mechanism, and watch item means "current evidence does not support it", not "it is not happening". Watch items belong in the margin of the conclusion page; this example did not write them there, but it kept the trace. Searching has always included exclusion. Knowing which mechanism lacks evidence is worth, defensively, as much as knowing which one is confirmed.

    On the writing box. Action 3, the monthly manual drill, serves death three, skill decay, and its verification rhythm is monthly, decoupled from the decision itself. That shows the page can hold two classes of action: one-off actions before the decision point, such as pulling the execution data, and periodic actions after it, such as the monthly drill. Both need a checker, and only the check time differs. The example wrote both because an automation decision naturally produces periodic after-effects, which is the theme of Chapter 26.

    The method has a source. Research on prospective hindsight shows that when a task is framed as "imagine the event has already happened, then work back to its causes", people list significantly more causes, and better ones, than when they are asked directly what could go wrong (Mitchell, Russo & Pennington, Journal of Behavioral Decision Making 2(1): 25–38, 1989; Crossref record checked). The writing section of the thirty-minute procedure borrows that effect directly: "which three deaths do I most resemble" is a hindsight question. Gary Klein developed it into the premortem, and Chapter 39 of this book is his ninety-minute version. The thirty-minute version here compresses the effect onto a decision at personal scale, and the price of the compression is a single viewpoint. One person cannot run past his own group's blind spots, which is why 36.4 insists on an external check person.

    36.6bis The Cross-Check List for the Conclusion Page

    Five minutes of cross-check after the page is written, the same instrument as cross-filling: ask a colleague with no connection to the decision to go through it. Five items, one at a time.

    1. Does every death point at a criterion entry, Chapter X, criterion 1, 2 or 3? A death you cannot point at is a story. Go back to the library.
    2. Does every action have an artifact? The artifact can be data, a record, a drill result. It cannot be "awareness", "atmosphere" or "attention".
    3. Can every action be given a date on a calendar? An action you cannot date has no owner.
    4. Does the check person meet all three standards: no stake, has time, recognises abnormal? Miss any one and change the person.
    5. Do the three deaths cover different layers (the three layers of Chapter 9)? Three deaths all on one layer, all technical for instance, mean the search missed organizational and incentive candidates. Go back and search again.

    Passing means all five. Two failures mean the page gets rewritten. Four failures mean the procedure itself has gone out of shape, and you go back to the 36.2 preparation list and start over. The checker signs initials in the box; the cross-check stays under ten minutes, because above that nobody uses it.

    The page also needs lifecycle management. Once the actions have been verified, archive the note in a public place, a fixed column in the project tool, tagged twice: decision object and mechanisms hit. At the end of each quarter, pull the archive statistics, three numbers only: usage (is anyone using it at all), the rate of actions verified on time, and the cross-check item that fails most often. Each number maps to one improvement action. Low usage sends you back to step one of the rollout. A low verification rate sends you to the check person's time commitment. The frequent failure sends you to the cross-check list itself. Statistics stop there, and a dashboard would be over-engineering.

    36.6 Three Ways to Fail

    Failure one: filling the table with the evidence skipped. A grade with no evidence is decoration, and the whole procedure fails with it. The test is easy. Show your table to a colleague with no connection to the decision and ask which grade he does not believe.

    Failure two: the library search turning into browsing cases. Stopping to read the stories blows the fifteen minutes. Read only the decision-point paragraph and the minimum counterfactual. The purpose is screening mechanisms, a case is a calibrator, and one calibrator is enough.

    The calibrator deserves one more step of explanation. Case calibration exists to prevent matching mechanisms on paper. Without a comparison case, people tend to match any situation to the mechanism they know best. The case anchors the match to concrete detail from the scene, and anchored matching is more accurate. That is not theory; it is the common sense of information retrieval, where a query carrying an example beats a query of keywords.

    Failure three: the page reading "heighten attention, keep tracking". An eight-word conclusion equals zero actions. Test: does paragraph two contain a checking action you can date on a calendar? If not, rewrite it.

    A warning that runs the other way. The procedure can also do harm. Exploratory decisions (Part Six, Chapter 40) are not for this procedure, because in a situation that needs trial and error, rehearsing death kills the attempt. Judge whether your decision is exploratory or operational with the three criteria in Chapter 40, and only then decide whether to spend the thirty minutes.

    One successful usage is left to write. The first run of the thirty-minute procedure must have someone alongside. What a first-timer needs most is a person who has been through it, and that person has exactly one job: when you state your evidence, ask how you know. That single question doubles the honesty of the table. The companion needs no formal appointment; any colleague who has read Chapter 35 will do, and a reading group, a training session or a morning stand-up can manufacture companions in batches. Someone alongside the first time, alone the third, teaching others by the fifth. After those three steps the thirty minutes stops being a tool and becomes a habit.

    36.7 Five Common Questions

    Question one: the decision maker has no thirty minutes. Delegate the run. An assistant or the team fills the table and searches the library from the materials, and the decision maker joins for the last ten minutes only. The delegated version loses honesty in the table, since an assistant writing your evidence will soften it a grade. The counter is one extra question from the check person at the page: which of the six grades do you disagree with. Disagreements get refilled.

    Question two: a superior overrules the action. An overrule is one of the procedure's normal outputs: the procedure put the risk on the table, and the table is where the decision chain decides. The page then gains one line: the action overruled, the reason for the overrule, and a signature reading "risk acknowledged". That line turns an overrule from a default into a trace, and a trace is itself a defence. The diffusion of responsibility from Chapter 18 is held off here by one sticky note.

    Question three: several decisions run in parallel. Order them by the timing of their points of no return, and run the nearest one first. Parallel decisions share the same library stock, the criterion cards and the pre-marking, so the second thirty minutes usually runs shorter than the first. Never compress several decisions into one thirty minutes. The time budget of the three boxes is designed for one decision.

    Question four: a remote team. Turn the boxes into a shared-document relay. The table and the search are done asynchronously, and the conclusion page is written face to face in a fifteen-minute video call. The remote version loses on action three, where the screen weakens the case's sense of place. The counter: one person reads the case aloud and everyone else only listens, since reading aloud forces slow reading and slow reading restores the sense of place.

    The common ground under the four: every stage may change shape, and two things may never be dropped, the evidence column and the check person. The first keeps the table true, the second keeps the action happening. Delegation, relay and asynchrony are changes of carrier, not changes of procedure.

    Question five: does this procedure make an organization timid. Teams that have run a large number of conclusion pages report one complaint: having to think of three deaths every time, is this teaching people not to decide. The answer sits in the output. The page produces three actions and a signature, and once the actions are done the decision proceeds as it would have. The procedure blocks two things only. It blocks the case where the actions will not be done and the decision is pushed through anyway, and blocking that is its job. It blocks exploratory decisions that wandered into it, which is the reverse warning in 36.6. Caution and timidity are told apart by output: caution produces actions, timidity produces delay. The procedure recognises actions only. One measurement note while we are here: in teams that run conclusion pages, the number of decisions does not fall. What falls is the number of firefights afterwards and the frequency of "we never thought of that" in postmortems. Those two are what the procedure actually buys.

    36.8 Division of Labour with the Chapter 39 Premortem

    The thirty-minute procedure and the ninety-minute premortem (Chapter 39) are two specifications of one family, split along three axes. Scale: the thirty-minute version serves a single decision, the premortem serves a project or a class of carrier (the extreme quadrant of the parameter map in 35.6). People: the thirty-minute version can be run alone, the premortem needs at least five, since diversity of viewpoint is its fuel and a solo premortem is a longer self-conversation. Timing: the thirty-minute version sits against the decision point, days before it; the premortem sits against project milestones, kickoff, architecture freeze, a month before launch. The outputs differ too: a three-line note against a hazard list with owners attached. The usual order is premortem first, thirty minutes later. Hazards found at the premortem enter the project's checkpoints, and as a checkpoint approaches, each individual decision near it runs its own thirty minutes. With both levels running, an organization's protection at points of no return has scan precision at the single decision and coverage breadth at the project level.

    36.9 Three Steps to Roll It Out

    The thirty-minute procedure lives easily in one person and dies easily in an organization. Rollout takes three steps.

    Step one: a pilot group. Pick a team where decisions are dense: product, operations, post-investment. Two or three people run it ten times. The pilot tracks two numbers only: how many runs, and which of the five cross-check items fails most. After ten runs, hold one postmortem and turn the mechanisms this team hits most into a team criterion card, a team library of its own, with a higher hit rate than the generic one.

    Step two: make the template library and the card library public. The pilot's criterion cards, sample pages and cross-check list go into the organization's shared template library, placed inside tools everyone already passes through: the project kickoff process, a fixed step in the release process. No standalone knowledge base: nobody passes through a knowledge base, and only what you pass through gets used.

    Step three: a quarterly sample. Governance pulls five archived pages each quarter and looks at two things, the rate of actions verified and the completeness of criterion citation. The result does not enter anyone's individual appraisal, because the moment it does, filling the table becomes a statement of position and the error 36.2 warned about returns. It has two uses only: adding to the card library and fixing the procedure. Rollout fails in a highly consistent shape. Step one has nobody alongside, and a lonely tool does not survive its third use. Step two puts the templates in a knowledge base nobody passes. Step three turns the sample into an appraisal, and an appraisal forces acting. Follow the three steps and dodge those three pits, and after a year the conclusion page turns up in the organization's everyday vocabulary. At that point the tool has grown into the organization.

    How the page interfaces with the governance processes already in place decides whether it is one more sheet of paper laid on top or part of the skeleton. Three interface points.

    Interface one: the review meeting. If the organization holds kickoff reviews, architecture reviews or change reviews, the page's three paragraphs become the fixed first page of the review pack, with the rest of the pack unchanged. That first page turns a review from hearing a report into confronting risk: reviewers read the three deaths first and judge whether a worse one was missed, and a missed one goes onto the first page before anyone discusses approval. No charter change is needed, only one more sheet.

    Interface two: the approval chain. The check person's signature box and the approval chain's sign-off box are different instruments and should not be merged. Sign-off is an act of authority, agree or not. Checking is an act of fact: did the action happen, does the artifact exist. Merged, the checker becomes the Nth signer and the factual content thins out, which is the Chapter 18 mechanism replicated at tool level. The correct merge is one required field in the approval chain, named "conclusion page archive number". No number, no passage.

    Interface three: the postmortem. The postmortem of Chapter 38 looks back at the judgment made at the decision point, and the conclusion page is the snapshot of that judgment. The first move of the postmortem is to pull the archived page and check it line by line against what happened: how many of the three deaths occurred, how many of the three actions held, did the checker actually check at the time. The page gives the postmortem a primary record to compare against, and the ceiling on postmortem quality rises with it.

    Common to all three: a new tool does not build its own stove. It makes itself one page and one field inside a process that already runs. Grafting outlives building.

    A sample quarter with both specifications in use. At the start of the month, one project-level premortem, ninety minutes, with the hazard list going into the checkpoints. Mid-month, each decision near a checkpoint runs its thirty minutes: the spot-check switch, the canary percentage, the wording of an announcement, one note each. At month end, ten minutes with the month's notes and the premortem list side by side, marking where the two agree on mechanisms. A mechanism hit by both is the main theme in that project, and its criterion card belongs in the front row of the team library. The calendar demonstrates one thing. The two specifications do not compete for time; they compete for coverage. The premortem sweeps area, the thirty minutes sweep point, and coverage is complete only when the two line up.

    Five Self-Checks

    1. On the irreversible decision nearest to hand, which of the three time boxes can you not actually run? Is the obstacle time or authority?
    2. The last external commitment you signed off without the overnight gap: looking back, was it worth it?
    3. Can the actions in paragraph two of your conclusion page be dated on a calendar? Which one cannot?
    4. Who on your team would make a good check person? Does that person have the authority to stop something?
    5. What does action 2 in the synthetic example, adding an independent check line, correspond to in your own system? Does it exist today?

    The check-person role has an organization-level version worth giving larger readers: a roster. The checker should not always be the same person, because a fixed checker turns the role into a formality. The logic matches the real drill of personnel redundancy in Chapter 22, and each rotation refreshes the checker's understanding as well. The rotation period follows decision frequency, monthly for dense teams and quarterly for sparse ones. The roster hangs in the team's scheduling system, on the same layer as the on-call roster. Treating checking as a kind of duty says by itself how much weight it carries, and duty cannot be skipped.

    An upgrade path follows the five questions. Once the individual version is fluent, build the team version. What changes is that the library stage becomes a collective activity: each member claims one mechanism and checks its criteria, and five minutes later the group reports. Collective search hits more criteria, because different people associate different evidence with the same criterion, and the fifteen minutes usually turn up 1–2 more effective criterion hits than the solo version. The team version is more manual and suits decisions with high irreversibility. Low-risk decisions stay on the individual version.

    A last paragraph for the reader still deciding whether to use any of this. The entire cost is one afternoon-tea slot and one page of sticky note; the entire output is three concrete checking actions. Cost against output, this is the best ratio in the book, cheaper than any ledger, drill or audit, because it needs thirty minutes before a decision and no project, no budget, no cross-department coordination. It is a tool one person can start alone. If you finish the book and want to carry away one thing you can use tomorrow, carry this chapter.
