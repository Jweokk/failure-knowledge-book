    Maritime and Transport | ma

    Five columns: record quality, feedback delay, coupling, the three deadliest mechanisms, and what outsiders most easily learn wrong.

    1 Record quality

    The UK Marine Accident Investigation Branch is the global sample. Reports sit in full text on the gov.uk platform in two tracks, formal reports and general investigations, and all 13 ma cases in this book come from its 2026 output. The investigation runs independent of the maritime judiciary (Chapter 3). The IMO framework sets the principles, but jurisdiction fragments along flag lines: the same accident under a different flag can end with no public text at all (Section 3.4). The notification channel of China's Maritime Safety Administration is of limited availability [: pending verification]. Overall: small and medium vessels under flags of convenience are the dark zone of the record, while the small fishing boats inside MAIB jurisdiction form the densest sample of small-accident records anywhere. Nine of the 13 cases here are fishing vessels and small workboats. That ratio is the MAIB's value in one line. It turned the failures of unimportant people into searchable knowledge.

    2 Feedback delay

    Operational feedback runs in seconds and minutes (a watchkeeper dozing off, a grounding, a collision), and the shape matches aviation. Maintenance and fatigue feedback runs in weeks and months: the sleep debt in ma-013 built up over a single week, and the sea water piping in ma-004 went aboard in 1998. Institutional feedback, meaning flag-state regulation and the survey system, runs in years. Fishing has a structure of its own. Voyage economics make "carry on" the default (Chapter 27), so the delay gets absorbed as a matter of course.

    3 Coupling

    Inside one vessel, coupling is moderate. Propulsion, power, windlass and firefighting form physical chains, and ma-001 is the textbook form: loss of electrical power took the anchor with it. Between vessels, coupling climbs steeply in close-quarters work (the tanker and the fishing vessel in ma-005, the collision inside a traffic separation scheme in ma-008). Coupling to the outside world changes with the water. Coastal and traffic areas wire a single-vessel accident straight into the public order of the waterway through vessel traffic services, and the edge of VTS authority is a regular scene for diffusion of responsibility (ma-008).

    4 The three deadliest mechanisms

    Practical drift (Chapter 15): the watch alarm buried under music (ma-002), a master alone on watch and asleep (ma-003), a new procedure executed off its own script (ma-007). The invisibility of maintenance (Chapter 29): sea water piping installed in 1998 (ma-004), the non-OEM bearing that left the anchor useless after a power failure (ma-001), an inspection gap on a lifting chain (ma-013). Margin erosion (Chapter 25): freeing ports cut smaller year after year (ma-009), working posture hardened into habit (ma-012). Next in frequency come normalization of deviance (the give-way assumption in ma-005) and handover rupture (the VTS authority boundary in ma-008).

    5 What outsiders most easily learn wrong

    Wrong lesson one: build the picture of the industry from large ships and cruise liners. Most hulls in the world merchant and fishing fleets have no modern bridge and no redundancy, and half the lessons in MAIB reports come from vessels under 25 metres. Wrong lesson two: read flag-state registration as a regulatory guarantee (the fragmentation in Chapter 3). Wrong lesson three: discount small accidents as report material. Capsize and man-overboard cases from fishing boats are the best teaching material available on single-person, single-point, low-cost defence. The defences are cheap (a watch alarm, an immersion suit, a second anchor), the failures are cheap and fatal, and the sense of proportion across industries is all inside them.

    6 Three questions from an outsider

    Question one: what do fishing and small-boat cases offer deep-sea shipping? The mechanism does not pick a hull size. Fatigue on watch, custom standing in for assessment, emergency equipment failing at the moment it is needed: the small-boat detail is the most complete, MAIB investigates a small accident with the seriousness it gives a large one, and a small case is a transparent specimen of a large mechanism. Question two: why do maritime reports always mention the flag state? Jurisdiction fragmentation is the institutional feature of this field, and the flag decides who investigates. Chapter 3 describes the fragmentation and carries the retrieval countermeasures. Question three: does any of this carry to road freight? Watchkeeping, handover, deferred maintenance, cascading failure, and the non-OEM bearing chain of ma-001 hold in any mode of transport. Distance and fatigue are the trade's common enemies.

    8 How this page is updated

    A domain page is a live document. Once a year, take the new A and B grade reports in Maritime and Transport and check them against the five columns and the list in section 7. A mechanism that appears for the first time gets a line in section 4. A shift in frequency gets a new count in section 7. A new A-grade case enters the library under the field standards of Chapter 5. Trigger, owner and review date go into the user's calendar (Chapter 39). A domain page, like the ledger, is dead the moment nobody updates it.

    9 The starter kit

    Three items for a reader new to Maritime and Transport. Entry case: ma-001 (a non-OEM bearing wore early and the generator was destroyed by fire; MAIB, 2026; link at Appendix B). Read it first for the house style and the grain size of a report in this field. Entry data source: the primary collection listed under maritime and transport in. Find the entrance, bookmark it, visit once a quarter. Entry mechanism: start with the top row of the section 7 list and read that chapter against a case from here. One mechanism gets two passes, once in the mechanism chapter for the criteria, once in this domain for the local parameters. The three items take about one working day, and at the end of it you own a first coordinate system for the field.

    10 Reservations

    Three limits sit on the judgments above. The case library samples toward events with rich public records, and the skew is described in Chapter 2. The frequency order reflects this book's library, not the true distribution in the field. Institutions and technology move, so this page has a shelf life measured in years, and any citation of it should carry the year. Those three are the book's general disclaimer, and the threshold check of Chapter 1 applies to this book as much as to any other artifact.

    11 The three layers in the maritime accent

    The maritime accent is heaviest at the organizational layer. On fishing boats, inland craft and small vessels, operating procedure exists largely as custom rather than text: outboard work with no assessment in ma-012, a watch with no reserve in ma-003. Custom standing in for text makes practical drift in Chapter 15 and the escalation ladder of Chapter 8 almost impossible to separate here, because the end point of drift is custom, and custom is itself the first step of the ladder. For the reviewer: in the nine-box walkthrough of a maritime case, run the custom archaeology in the organizational box first, asking which generation of vessel the custom started on. The answer points at Chapter 30, handover rupture. Custom cannot be traced back for the plain reason that it was never written down.

    13 Closing note

    A last piece of advice for maritime readers. The density of cases proves a commonplace: a small boat does not mean a small lesson. MAIB investigated each of these sinkings with the same care, Report 8/2026 through Report 17/2026, every one public in full, and that seriousness about small accidents is the field's institutional wealth. Small accidents in other industries still get a line in the news and nothing more. The largest lesson the maritime cases hold for a reader outside the field is that the risk of custom is no fishing peculiarity. Any industry leaning on oral tradition rather than written standards sits somewhere on ma-012's extension line. Does your operation have ma-012-style outboard work, a high-risk act nobody has written down? The answer is in the blank space of your work instruction.

    One more feature of this page is the hull span, from ro-ro ships of tens of thousands of tonnes down to fishing boats of tens of tonnes. A wide hull span carries a wide mechanism span, and the same water produces failures from a ro-ro's radio link dropping to a trawler's freeing ports cut smaller. Reading across the span trains you to strip the equipment parameters and look only at structure. The result of that training: hand any maritime bulletin, whatever the size of the ship, and within a minute you can place it among this book's mechanisms. The method is simple. Cover the vessel name and the tonnage, read only the process description, and repeat ten times. Recognition then runs without deliberate effort.

    14 How the mechanisms vary here

    Normalization of deviance, maritime variation: the fatigue and dozing cases (ma-002, ma-003) compress the scene to its minimum, one person, deep night, no second pair of eyes. Read it this way: the watch alarm is this field's own device, and its effectiveness has to be verified under worst-case audibility. The lesson of ma-002 is that passing a monthly test does not mean audible beneath music. Single-handed watchkeeping passes on flag-state rules, and the gap between compliance and safety is filled with physical exhaustion, which makes maritime the small-organization template of Chapter 32, compliant and unsafe. One further reading rule: if the handling of a dozing event stops at "educate the person", normalization has already started. The person is processed and the structure is left as it was.

    The invisibility of maintenance, maritime variation: ma-001 (a non-OEM bearing wearing early), ma-004 (ageing sea water piping) and ma-006 (an unsupported suction pipe) make a maintenance spectrum of spare-part quality, component life and installation workmanship. The maintenance economics of a small owner is the structural constraint of the field: maintenance spending is far more sensitive to freight rates there than at a large operator. Read it this way: the comparison between what class survey covers and where failures actually land is the right measure of maintenance visibility, and the share of failure points outside survey coverage is the blind-spot rate of the system. ma-013 (a lifting chain parting) pushes the blind spot into lifting gear accessories. Survey covers primary structure, deck rigging depends on crew self-inspection, and the failure landed precisely in the fatigue band of self-inspection.

    The illusion of redundancy, maritime variation: ma-010, three dead in succession in a rescue vessel's oxygen-deficient anchor chain locker, is the book's heaviest case of the rescuer becoming the victim. Confined-space management failed on an emergency response vessel, and the urgency of a rescue exempted the gas testing procedure. Read it this way: the collapse in enforcement of the confined-space entry permit in rescue scenes is the variant of the illusion of redundancy, where the procedural layer of redundancy is absent in the one scene that needs it most.

    15 Reading in pairs

    Read the maritime page against the healthcare page (small teams, high pressure, steep hierarchy; see the paired-reading section there). Against manufacturing: ma-001's non-OEM part reads well beside the supply-chain quality cases in the mn series, where the rule that the source of a part decides the life of the part holds in both. Against construction, see the paired-reading section on that page. Against aviation: av-019 (a hydraulic failure and an out-of-rule ferry flight) reads against the sail-while-sick structure of the ma series, since the ferry segment of aviation and the fishing segment of maritime are both low points in their own regulatory density. Put the low-point cases side by side and the correlation between regulatory density and failure density shows up as one of the most stable empirical regularities in the book.
